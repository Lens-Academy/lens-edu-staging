# Formats brainstorm — speaker notes

**4 slides | Approximately 7–9 minutes at a normal speaking pace**

## Slide 1 — Formats brainstorm

We collected a large pool of ideas on how different elements of a course could be designed and organized. We drew on our own experiences teaching and participating in courses, as well as principles of evidence-based pedagogy.

This is not a list of mandatory requirements or a finished course design. It is a collection of possible practices and formats that can be combined and adapted depending on the target audience, the intended learning outcomes, and the available resources.

We grouped the brainstorm into reading materials, weekly assignments, meetings, tests, final artifacts, and additional activities. One important relationship is that the choice of tests, final artifacts, and additional activities should follow from decisions made about reading materials, weekly assignments, and meetings, rather than being made independently.

I'll briefly go through the ideas for these categories. This is a summary of what came up in the brainstorm, rather than a proposal to use all of them.

## Slide 2 — Reading materials

For reading materials, we discussed a limit of no more than three sources and no more than two hours in total, with realistic time estimates.

Before participants start reading, they should be told why they are reading the material. This could take the form of guiding questions or a brief explanation of its purpose. For materials that are not short, linear texts, it may also be helpful to explain how to engage with them.

We also discussed ways to support engagement during reading: questions embedded throughout the text, accessible hints or footnotes, and expandable explanations for abstract concepts, mathematical notation, or graphs. Another consideration is avoiding the 'Wikipedia effect', where someone follows a chain of links and loses the original thread.

For checking understanding, one idea was self-check questions with immediate feedback. Those answers would not necessarily be graded; participants could see their results. We also discussed the possibility of a score threshold for marking an article as completed, while still allowing articles to be skipped.

There were several optional platform features: highlighting and annotating quotes, saving short notes, exporting them at the end of the course, sharing some comments with a group, and displaying reading progress. 

## Slide 3 — Weekly assignments

For weekly assignments, we collected a variety of possible formats. These included essays, research tasks, short presentations of readings, tests for understanding, games, fill-in-the-blank exercises, and explanations of a concept to a fifth-grader or in a different domain. Participants could also represent material through diagrams, tables, or mind maps, although those tasks need to be designed carefully.

A recurring idea was that assignments should involve applying theory, not only repeating it. For technical material, participants should get hands-on practice whenever that's reasonably possible. Other examples included interacting with the real world: steering an LLM, completing an actual application, or writing a short post. Some tasks could be designed to challenge intuitions about what's realistically achievable, and it's also fine for a task not to have a known solution.

We also talked about feedback. Fast feedback loops are valuable, and assignments that ask participants to develop their own thoughts should be followed by discussion. Essays should allow room for disagreement and criticism.

Across the course, assignments can connect to each other, revisit earlier topics without unnecessary busywork, and gradually lead to a larger project or final artifact — for example, a published post or a replicated study.

Finally, the instructions should be easy to understand. The connection to the week's topic and readings should be clear, as should the assessment criteria, expected time, scope, and what participants can do when they get stuck or the assignment takes too long.

## Slide 4 — Meetings and other stuff

For meetings, the brainstorm began with a basic consideration: before designing a course, decide whether meetings are needed at all, and what specific purpose they serve.

If there are meetings, several ideas concerned discussion and participation. We mentioned some formats that encourage people to make their chains of reasoning explicit, summarizing someone else's point of view, and giving time to participants' ideas and questions. Other possible formats include opinion barometers, discussions of current events, and having participants read different materials and present them to the group.

There were also suggestions about facilitation. Keep to the scheduled start, break, and end times. Share an agenda in advance. Clarify the exact question before beginning a complex discussion. Keep breakout instructions simple. Make off-topic handling clear and support different modes of participation, such as text, voice, or images. Give each participant an opportunity to contribute and receive positive feedback.

The brainstorm did not separately specify formats for the sections on tests or final artifacts. As stated at the beginning, those choices should follow from the decisions about reading, assignments, and meetings.

Lastly, we listed possible additional activities: expert lectures, career consultations, research taste workshops, networking and informal meetups, hackathons, Q&A sessions for homework and readings, research office hours, and training in meta-skills such as writing applications.
