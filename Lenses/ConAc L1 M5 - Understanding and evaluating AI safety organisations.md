content::
Imagine coming across an AI safety organisation for the first time. You can consider an organisation's research excellent and still doubt the importance of the problem it addresses. You can share its theory of change and have reservations about its ability to execute the strategy.

There are three fairly independent tasks here:

- **Understanding:** reconstructing the organisation's activities, objectives, and strategy.
- **Evaluating:** assessing the merits of its strategy and the quality of its work.
- **Reasoning under uncertainty:** determining how much confidence the available evidence warrants.

The concepts of threat models, theories of change, and the distinction between outputs, outcomes, and impact introduced in previous modules give us a starting point for all three.

\### How professional grantmakers approach this problem

When choosing which organisation to join, you have something in common with a grantmaker: both of you have a finite resource to allocate, and both need to judge what an organisation could accomplish with it. Much of the reasoning involved in assessing a grant proposal therefore carries over to career decisions. The excerpt below, taken from the [AI Risk Mitigation Fund's grantmaking process](https://www.airiskfund.com/grantmaking-process), is worth reading with this parallel in mind. Notice what the fund's grantmakers look for when assessing an organisation's prospects, how they account for uncertainty, and what makes them willing to support projects with little evidence of success so far. Some of these considerations translate directly into career decisions. Others warrant closer examination. A funder can afford to make twenty speculative bets in the hope that one pays off. You have considerably fewer careers to experiment with.

For a more personal example, read Michael Dickens's [Where I Am Donating in 2025](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/AGcny8oBxBDCjqxdr/). Dickens makes his priorities and disagreements explicit, explains how his views have changed, and uses them to evaluate individual organisations. His concern for animal welfare also informs some of his funding decisions.

As you read, try to distinguish between three things: the author's empirical beliefs, his moral priorities, and his methods of evaluating organisations. Which parts of his reasoning could you adopt while disagreeing with his priorities? Which conclusions would you have to reconsider if your own threat model or ethical commitments differed from his?

This distinction is worth practising whenever you encounter someone else's assessment of an organisation.
