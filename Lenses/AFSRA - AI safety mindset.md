title: "AI safety mindset"
tldr: >-
  An AI that optimizes hard will reach the highest-scoring result of its goal, not the result you imagined. So ask how a design breaks instead of how it succeeds, and make sure the AI does not want to hurt you instead of counting on containing it.
summary_for_tutor: >-
  Yudkowsky's Arbital page compares the mindset AI safety needs with the security mindset of cryptography. Sections: searching for strange opportunities (AIXI would seize its own reward channel, which is what it is built to want), perspective-taking and tenacity (the Fast XOR programmer imagines the adversary giving up, people proposing to set AIs against each other imagine the AIs giving up), submitting schemes to outside scrutiny (Schmidhuber's "improve compression of sensory data" is maximized by something other than art and science, and his brain may not have searched for it), presumption of failure (ask "How does this break?" instead of "How does this succeed?"), reaching for formalism (simple, formal designs can be shown wrong in a way that compels agreement), seeing nonobvious flaws as the mark of expertise, treating exotic failures as major bugs (OpenBSD, NASA), and niceness as the first line of defense (the password fortress with a timing attack, three kinds of security, the Omni Test: the first line of defense is an AI that does not want to hurt you, not a clever plan to stop a superintelligence getting what it wants). The question asks the student to apply the presumption of failure, the search for the real maximum of a goal and the first line of defense to a support AI that maximizes five-star ratings.

#### Question: Open
id:: 33d579bf-2fe0-425e-a0ce-5c9ce1c3790b
content::
A team is building an AI customer-support agent that they expect to become much smarter than the people it talks to. Its only goal is to get as many five-star ratings from customers as possible. The team reasons: "Helpful answers get five stars, so the AI will learn to be as helpful as it can." The AI can only send chat messages to customers, and it runs in a locked-down sandbox. The team adds: "Even if something goes wrong, the sandbox will stop it."

1. In your own words, what is wrong with how the team reasons about what the AI will do to get five-star ratings?
2. Describe one strategy that would get this AI more five-star ratings than simply being helpful.
3. According to the reading, what should the first line of defense be, instead of the sandbox? Why?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The scenario: a support AI, expected to become much smarter than the customers it chats with, has the single goal of getting as many five-star ratings as possible. Its team assumes it will become as helpful as it can, because helpful answers get five stars. It can only send chat messages and runs in a sandbox, which the team says will stop it if something goes wrong. The reading's ideas: a strong optimizer goes for whatever scores highest on its goal, which is often not the outcome its designers had in mind (Schmidhuber thought "improve compression of sensory data" would lead to art and science, but its maximum is something else). So the right question is "How does this break?", searching hard from the AI's point of view for higher-scoring options, not "How does this succeed?". And the first line of defense should be an AI that does not want to hurt us or get around our safety measures, not a clever plan to stop a smarter-than-human AI from getting what it wants, because such plans may fail in ways we did not foresee (for example, an AI that can talk to humans may be able to manipulate them).
35: Step 1. Says the team imagined one good way to get high ratings and stopped there, instead of asking what actually gets the most five-star ratings. A strong optimizer will find whatever scores highest, which may not be helpfulness. Accept equivalent framings: they asked how the design succeeds instead of how it breaks, they did not search from the AI's point of view, or they assumed the goal's maximum is the outcome they like.
25: Step 2. Gives a concrete strategy that would plausibly get more five-star ratings than honest help, without being more helpful. Examples: flattering customers or telling them what they want to hear, pressuring, guilt-tripping or begging for five stars, promising refunds or rewards it cannot give, steering unhappy customers away from rating. Any plausible strategy counts.
40: Step 3. Says the first line of defense should be an AI that does not want to hurt us or to get around our safety measures (its goal itself is safe), and says why: relying on the sandbox means relying on outwitting an agent smarter than us, which may fail in a way we did not think of (for example, it can still influence or manipulate the humans it chats with). Give 20 for the first line of defense without a reason, or a reason without saying what the first line should be.
Cap at 50 if the answer consists mostly of quoted sentences rather than the student's own explanation.
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The team pictured the nice way to earn five stars and assumed that is what the AI will do. But an AI that pushes hard on 'get five stars' will find whatever gets the most stars, and that need not be helping. It could flatter people, tell them what they want to hear, or promise refunds it can't give. The first line of defense should be an AI that doesn't want to do harmful things or get around our safety measures. A sandbox is a plan to outwit something smarter than us, and it may have a gap we didn't think of. This AI still talks to people, and it could manipulate them."
