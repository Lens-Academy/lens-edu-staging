#### Text
content::
The pre-reading question asked whether you enjoyed something as much as you had wanted it. Kent Berridge, a neuroscientist at the University of Michigan, has spent decades studying why the two can differ. In this interview excerpt (6 minutes, automatic captions) he explains how he found out, almost by accident.

Two terms you will hear. **Liking** is the pleasure of a reward when you actually get it. **Wanting**, which Berridge also calls **incentive salience**, is the pull toward a reward, often triggered by cues that signal it, such as a notification sound or the sight of food ([Berridge and Robinson 2016](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)).

#### Callout: How far does this apply to people?
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
Berridge's key experiments were done in rats, and the strongest human evidence comes from addiction, where people can crave a drug they no longer enjoy ([Berridge and Robinson 2016](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)). For ordinary human wanting the picture is less clear. A systematic review by Eva Pool and colleagues found that "human experiments have produced contradictory results, raising doubts about the existence of separate wanting and liking influences in human reward processing". They suggest the reason may be the way the studies measured the two, which often confused both with "expected pleasantness", how much people think they will enjoy something ([Pool et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.01.006), abstract).

\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from the interview and the box after it, without looking back. No need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner watched 6 minutes of an interview with Kent Berridge and read a short box on Pool et al. 2016, then writes a free recall.

Key points:
- Liking is the pleasure of a reward when you get it. Wanting (incentive salience) is the pull toward it, triggered by cues.
- Social media offers small bits of information that tap the wanting system, which can detach from pleasure.
- Dopamine was long thought to be the brain's pleasure chemical. In Berridge's experiments, blocking dopamine, and then removing about 99% of it, did not change rats' liking reactions to sugar (facial expressions such as lip licking), but the rats no longer wanted to eat or drink.
- Raising dopamine in the nucleus accumbens made rats want food more (eat up to eight times more) without liking it more.
- So dopamine seems to be more about wanting than liking.
- Caveat: the evidence is strong in animals and in addiction. In ordinary human wanting, studies are contradictory, possibly because measures confused both with expected pleasantness (Pool et al. 2016).

\## Learning question
A friend says: "I check my phone about a hundred times a day, so I must really enjoy it. Otherwise I would not want to do it so much." Where does this reasoning go wrong?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Learning question after Berridge's interview and the Pool et al. 2016 caveat.

Key points:
- The friend infers liking from wanting. On Berridge's account these are partly separate processes: wanting (incentive salience, triggered by cues such as notifications) can grow or persist without matching liking. Berridge says social media taps the wanting system and can detach from pleasure.
- So how often you want to do something is not a measure of how much you enjoy it.
- A way to find out: compare how strongly they want to check with how much they actually enjoy it each time, which is close to Brewer's curiosity experiment at the end of this module.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer precisely in 2 to 4 sentences, name 1 to 3 gaps, and ask 2 answerable follow-ups about why or how. Do not yet discuss how strong the human evidence is: the next question asks that. If stuck after 2 attempts, give the direct answer and move on. After 3 replies, ask whether they want to continue. 120 to 200 words. No generic praise.
