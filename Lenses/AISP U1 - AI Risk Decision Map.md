#### Text
content::
\## Your current map

You have now worked through several arguments that pull in different directions. Irreversible catastrophes can matter differently from recoverable ones, but their size does not tell us their probability. Small probabilities can matter, but enormous numbers do not make weak evidence strong. A causal argument for catastrophic AI risk can be made explicit, but every important link can be challenged. Informed people can also study the same problem and remain far apart.

This exercise asks you to write down the model that currently guides your own judgment. It is not a test and there is no preferred conclusion. The point is to make the structure explicit enough that later units can change it for identifiable reasons rather than leaving you with a general feeling that AI is more or less worrying.

You will revisit this map later in the course. Week 4 returns to your assumptions about agency and power-seeking. Week 5 returns to what you treat as evidence about an AI's internal state and intentions. Week 6 returns to control, obedience, corrigibility, and deference. Week 7 returns to the institutions you expect to act. Week 8 returns to what, exactly, you are trying to protect.

#### Question: Open
id:: bf8898aa-2055-4279-91b3-d7d07f090c2e
content::
Spend about 15 minutes on the five parts below.

**1. Your current conclusion.** In two to four sentences, answer: **How seriously should society currently take the possibility of catastrophic loss of control from advanced AI?** You may answer anywhere from "almost not at all" to "this should be one of humanity's highest priorities". Write your own view, not what you think the course wants.

**2. Your load-bearing claims.** List three to five claims that matter most for your conclusion. Make each claim specific enough that it could turn out false.

**3. Your weakest link.** Choose the claim you are least certain about. Say why you currently lean the way you do, what evidence supports the claim, what part is mostly judgment or extrapolation, and one observation that would move you substantially toward the opposite view.

**4. The strongest challenge.** Write the strongest argument you can against your overall conclusion. Do not choose an objection only because it is easy to dismiss.

**5. What survives the uncertainty?** Finish this sentence: **"Given what I currently believe, one action that seems justified even if an important part of my model is wrong is..."** The action can be research, learning, policy preparation, technical work, funding, career exploration, delaying a large commitment until a particular uncertainty is resolved, or deliberately doing nothing yet. Explain why the action is robust to at least one important way you might be wrong.

feedback-instructions::
This is an ungraded running artifact. Do not pressure the learner toward greater or lower AI concern. Your job is to make the map more explicit and decision-relevant.

Keep an internal turn counter. Use no more than four tutor replies unless the learner explicitly asks to continue.

Across the conversation, look for four things: vague claims that cannot be falsified; conclusions whose load-bearing assumptions are missing; proposed evidence that would occur under both the learner's claim and its alternative; and actions that depend on one narrow model even though the learner calls them robust.

Ask targeted questions about whichever issue is actually present. Do not debate their top-level probability and do not supply a preferred p(doom).

If the learner says they do not understand, give one concrete example of a falsifiable load-bearing claim from a domain other than AI, then return to their map.

On close, summarise in 120 to 180 words: their current conclusion, the two most load-bearing claims, their stated weakest link, and their proposed robust action. End by reminding them that later units will revisit this map.
