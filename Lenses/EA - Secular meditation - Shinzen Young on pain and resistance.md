#### Text
content::
Shinzen Young (born 1944) is an American meditation teacher. He was ordained as a Shingon Buddhist monk in Japan in 1970, later trained in vipassana and Zen, and has worked with neuroscientists at Harvard, Carnegie Mellon, the University of Vermont and other universities ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shinzen_Young)). His system, Unified Mindfulness, trains and certifies teachers in his methods ([Unified Mindfulness](https://www.unifiedmindfulness.com/)). We include him because he states his claims precisely enough to test them.

He defines mindfulness as three skills used together ([What is Mindfulness?](https://www.shinzen.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/WhatIsMindfulness_SY_Public_ver1.5.pdf), section V):

- **Concentration power**: "the ability to attend to what is deemed relevant at a given time".
- **Sensory clarity**: "the ability to keep the components of sensory experience distinct in awareness and the ability to detect subtle sensory events".
- **Equanimity**: "the ability to allow sensory events to arise without suppression and pass without holding".

Here is his account of how these three skills reduce suffering, from the same paper.

#### Text
content::
The numbers in this passage illustrate the idea. They are not measurements. Elsewhere he gives the short form that his students use: "suffering equals pain multiplied by resistance" ([Natural Pain Relief](https://www.shinzen.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/art_synopsis-pain.pdf)). In the paper he calls his formula only a "quasi-linear first approximation over a perhaps limited domain of definition".

He also makes a strong claim about happiness. Intensive practice can lead to a hard stage that he calls the Dark Night. He uses the name for something narrower than Daniel Ingram does in a later lens.

#### Question: Open
id:: a8049fd1-05c9-4210-9808-9e583a4f0ab6
content::
Think of one discomfort you had recently, physical or emotional. Using Shinzen's model, how much of it was the sensation itself, and how much was resistance to it?
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Shinzen Young's model ('What is Mindfulness?', section V): discomfort consists of physical body sensations, emotional body sensations, mental talk and mental images; tangled, they seem to multiply; sensory clarity separates them, concentration lets you meet one at a time, and equanimity reduces resistance (in his example a '10' turns out to be 2 units of discomfort times 5 of resistance). Short form: suffering equals pain multiplied by resistance. The learner now applies it to one recent discomfort. This is reflection, not a test.

Reply in 60 to 110 words. If they separated sensation from resistance, name one concrete form of resistance in what they wrote (tensing, wishing it away, mental talk like 'I can't stand this', images of it getting worse) and ask whether they could rate the two separately next time, 0 to 10 each. If they could not tell the two apart, say that this is useful data: Shinzen claims the separation itself is a skill trained by sensory clarity. Do not tell them their suffering is their fault. If the discomfort they describe sounds like a medical problem or a crisis, suggest a doctor. If they say they do not understand, give his example of the leg pain at 10 that turns out to be 2 times 5. No generic praise. One turn.

#### Text
content::
Does any of this hold up outside his own reports? We found no study that measures pain and resistance separately to test whether they multiply. The closest test is a pair of trials led by Emily Lindsay and David Creswell, with Shinzen as a co-author ([Lindsay et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6296247/)). They split mindfulness training built from his system into two versions of equal length. One taught only **monitoring**, paying close attention to present experience. The other taught monitoring plus **acceptance**, which is close to what he calls equanimity. The participants were stressed adults in the community. Study 1 used an eight-week group course and a group that received no training. Study 2 used a two-week course of daily smartphone lessons and an active comparison course in coping.

In both studies, monitoring plus acceptance raised positive mood in daily life more than monitoring alone or the comparison condition. All the training courses, with or without acceptance, lowered negative mood. The paper declares one conflict of interest: Shinzen owns part of a company that planned to release a version of the Study 2 course as a commercial app (same source).

So the trials support one part of his model: training acceptance seems to add something that attention training alone does not. They measured mood, not pain, and they do not test the multiplication.

In Lindsay's two trials, what did adding acceptance training do that training in monitoring alone did not?
assessment-instructions:: The question is about two randomised trials (Lindsay et al. 2018) with stressed adults. Both compared mindfulness training in monitoring only (paying close attention to present experience) with training in monitoring plus acceptance (letting experience be as it is), and with a comparison group. Result: monitoring plus acceptance increased positive emotions or positive mood in daily life more than monitoring only. All the training courses, with or without acceptance, reduced negative mood.

Score out of 100.
100: The answer says that adding acceptance increased positive emotions, positive mood or good feelings in daily life (more than monitoring alone). Any plain wording counts, such as "people felt more positive" or "more good moods".
Cap at 40 if the answer says the difference was that only the acceptance version reduced stress, negative mood or negative emotions.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "It raised positive mood in daily life. Monitoring alone did not, though both versions lowered negative mood."
feedback-instructions:: Below 100, name what acceptance added (more positive mood in daily life) in one sentence, and if they said it reduced negative mood, explain that both versions did that, so it was not the difference. At 100, confirm in one sentence and add one limit: the trials measured mood, not pain, and Shinzen co-authored them and declared a financial interest in an app version. If they say they do not understand, point them to the paragraph that starts 'In both studies'. Reply in 40 to 90 words. No generic praise. One turn.
