title: "Diseased thinking: dissolving questions about disease"
tldr: Arguing over whether obesity or alcoholism is a "real disease" hides the questions that matter. Should the person get blame or sympathy, and may they use a treatment that works? Ask those directly.
summary_for_tutor: Scott Alexander takes the debate over whether marginal conditions such as obesity, alcoholism or social anxiety are "real diseases". "Disease" is a cluster of features (biological cause, involuntary, rare, unpleasant, discrete, treated with science-y interventions). Cancer has all of them, obesity only some, so asking whether obesity is "really" a disease is like asking whether Pluto is really a planet. The word still matters because of hidden inferences, whether people get sympathy or condemnation, and whether medical treatment is acceptable. He answers those directly from a determinist consequentialist view. Condemn only where condemnation reduces the condition enough to be worth the hurt feelings, otherwise sympathize. Allow any treatment that is available and effective, judging side effects, cost and dependence as for any illness. He contrasts this with a model of blame based on free will, where everything hangs on whether a condition is biological or chosen. The question gives two parents arguing over whether a pupil's "gaming addiction" is a real disease and asks the learner to name the two questions the word stands in for and what each answer should depend on.

#### Question: Open
id:: f74e7537-88c4-4dde-b349-7a47e4d1bc43
content::
Two parents at a school meeting argue about a pupil who plays video games until 3 a.m. every night. One says "gaming addiction is a real disease", so the boy deserves sympathy and should be allowed to try the medication his doctor suggested. The other says "it's not a disease, it's a lack of willpower", so he needs a firm telling-off and no pills.

Use the essay's approach on this argument.

1. Name the questions the parents actually care about, which the word "disease" stands in for.

2. For each one, say what the answer should depend on, according to the essay.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay argues that whether a marginal condition is a "real disease" has no further meaning once its features are known. The label matters only because people use it to decide two other things: whether the person gets sympathy or blame, and whether medical treatment is acceptable. The essay says to answer those directly. Blame or condemn only where condemnation actually reduces the condition enough to be worth the hurt it causes, otherwise give sympathy. Allow a treatment whenever it is available and effective, weighing the usual concerns (side effects, cost, dependence) the same way as for any illness. The essay rejects deciding these by whether the condition is "biological" or freely chosen.

50: Blame or sympathy. Names it as one of the questions (whether the boy should be blamed or told off, or treated with sympathy) and says it should depend on whether blaming or telling him off would actually reduce the late-night gaming enough to be worth the hurt. 20 for naming the question without this criterion. An answer whose criterion is whether the gaming is his fault, freely chosen or biological gets 20 at most for this element.

50: Treatment. Names it as one of the questions (whether he should be allowed to use the medication) and says it should depend on whether the medication is available and works, with its side effects, cost or risk of dependence weighed as for any other condition, not on whether gaming addiction counts as a disease. 20 for naming the question without this criterion.

Cap at 30 if the answer says the argument is settled by finding out whether gaming addiction really is a disease.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The parents really want to know two things. First, should the boy get sympathy or a telling-off? That should depend on whether telling him off would actually make him game less, enough to be worth making him feel bad. Second, should he try the medication? That depends on whether it works and whether its side effects and costs are acceptable, the same as for any treatment. Whether we call it a disease adds nothing once those are answered."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Scott Alexander's "Diseased thinking" and applied it to two parents arguing over whether a pupil's gaming addiction is a real disease. The essay's key ideas: "disease" is a cluster of features, and once you know which a condition has, "is it really a disease?" means nothing more. The label is used to decide two other things. Blame or sympathy should depend on whether condemnation reduces the condition enough to be worth the hurt. Treatment should be allowed when it is available and effective, judged by side effects, cost and dependence as for any illness. Deciding by whether the condition is "biological" or chosen is the free-will model the essay argues against.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap, often a blame criterion based on whether it is "his fault" or a missing criterion for the medication. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example what would have to be true for a telling-off to be the right response. At 100, confirm briefly.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as its contrast between yelling at a cancer patient and telling a lazy person to get up and work. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
