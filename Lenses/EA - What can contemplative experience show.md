Many rows of the map, above all the contemplative ones, rest on what practitioners report from the inside: no self found, a peace that does not depend on circumstances, oneness. How much weight such reports deserve depends on two questions. Do people in different traditions have the same experience and only describe it differently? And is an experience, however convincing, evidence for what it seems to show?

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on mysticism sets out the positions. Read the opening, then four short parts. The entry uses "mystical experience" broadly, for experiences said to give insight that ordinary perception does not, including Buddhist insight with no God in it.

\## A critique from inside the science and Buddhism dialogue

Evan Thompson is a philosopher of mind. In 1991 he co-wrote *The Embodied Mind* with Francisco Varela and Eleanor Rosch, which he calls "the first book that related Buddhist philosophy to cognitive science", and he took part in the Mind and Life dialogues with the Dalai Lama (both from the interview below). In *Why I Am Not a Buddhist* (2020) he criticises how Buddhism is presented in the West. Read two parts of an interview with him.

A friend says: "Meditators in Buddhism, Advaita and Christian mysticism all report the same oneness and the same absence of a separate self. So these experiences show us what reality is really like, and science has confirmed there is no self."

Using what you just read, where could this go wrong? Name at least two separate problems.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read excerpts from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Mysticism" (Jones and Gellman) and an interview with Evan Thompson, then was given a plausible but flawed claim: "Meditators in Buddhism, Advaita and Christian mysticism all report the same oneness and the same absence of a separate self. So these experiences show us what reality is really like, and science has confirmed there is no self." This is practice, not a test.

Problems a strong answer can find (any two are good):
- "All report the same" is the disputed essentialist thesis. Constructivists hold that the mystic's concepts and culture shape the experience itself, so similar words need not mean the same experience. The entry also notes that brain studies find different patterns for different kinds of mystical experience and practice. And we only ever have post-experiential reports, so the dispute is hard to settle.
- Even if the experiences were the same, that would not show they are accurate. A common experience could come from a common brain (the entry notes secularists explain the commonality by shared neurology). The step from "it seems so" to "it is so" is the epistemology question (Q1 and Q2).
- Lack of checkability: unlike sense perception, mystical reports cannot be cross-checked, triangulated or tested for whether the perceiver was in the right state.
- Thompson: science does not say there is no self. It says the self is a construction, and a useful one. Evidence on meditation and the brain is "very tentative" and hyped. His retreat experience suggests practice can sculpt experience with the concepts it is given, so a report of no-self may partly reflect the instructions.
