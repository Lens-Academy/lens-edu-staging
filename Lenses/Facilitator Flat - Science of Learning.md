5. **Dual coding.** {++{"author":"Anshuman","timestamp":1783351738154}@@When you encounter the same information in two different formats, you retain it better than when you encounter it in one format alone. ++}Information {--{"author":"Anshuman","timestamp":1783351769378}@@processed across more than one modality (e.g. verbal + visual) is retained better than information processed through one alone.--} In a lecture-with-slides format this is a major lever — you keep the key claim visible while you talk about it, and the two encodings reinforce each other. In a pure-discussion format like Lens cohort meetings there's less surface for it to do work during the meeting itself. The strongest dual-coding effect in Lens's format comes from the structure as a whole: participants read (textual encoding) and then discuss (verbal encoding). That's one reason discussion-after-reading outperforms re-reading.

6. **Worked examples → faded scaffolding.** Beginners need explicit examples. As they progress, the support fades and they generate more themselves. Your sessions across a course should follow this arc: more facilitator structure in Week 1, less by Week 6.

#### Chat
instructions::
This is a short **reflection** (≈3 min), not a quiz or an assessment. The trainee has just read Kosslyn's "Science of Learning" and the six mechanisms above (desirable difficulty, generation effect, spacing & interleaving, elaboration & connection, dual coding, worked examples → faded scaffolding).

Open with this, in your own warm phrasing:
"Recall a time you yourself learned something really well. Looking at the six mechanisms — was any of them at play? Now do the same for a time you learned something badly: was a mechanism missing?"

Then:
- Draw it out of them. If they can name the mechanism that was (or wasn't) at work, good — that's the recall-then-connect doing its job.
- They may find none of the six match cleanly. That's fine — say so. Kosslyn covers sixteen mechanisms and we only pulled six. The point is the recall-then-connect, not a tidy match. If nothing matches, the experience may have been driven by something other than these mechanics — motivation, identity, who they were with — and noticing *that* is itself useful.
- If they treat the mechanisms as abstract, nudge toward a concrete facilitator move — "generation effect" maps directly to "ask before tell."
- Keep it short: 2 to 4 exchanges. Don't lecture or grade.

If there's room, one good extension: "If a participant told you the session feels harder than just reading the chapter would be, what would you say back in two sentences?" (This is desirable difficulty — the discomfort is the mechanism, not a bug.)

Close by connecting it forward: the test of whether they've internalised the science is whether they can use it to explain real learning experiences from their own life — and, soon, their participants'.
