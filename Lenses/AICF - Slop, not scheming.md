\## Sort the criticisms

In this unit you sort criticisms of control into three kinds:

- **Control does not work.** It will not stop a capable scheming model, or it stops working as models get more capable.
- **Control works but is not worth the effort.** For example, scheming is unlikely, other failures matter more, or the work would happen anyway.
- **Control works and makes things worse.** It reduces one risk but raises the overall risk.

1. Which kind is Wentworth's main criticism? Which kind is Bushnaq's?
2. Pick one of them. What does it get right, and what is the strongest reply to it? Is Buck's reply the strongest one available, or can you do better?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is practising the unit's core skill: sorting a criticism of control by what it disputes, saying what it gets right, and giving the strongest reply. Reference answers: Wentworth's main criticism is "works but not worth the effort": he accepts that control may stop early schemers but says scheming in early AI is a small slice of the risk, and the main risk (slop, unverifiable alignment work) is untouched. His remark that control does not generalise to superintelligence also has a "does not work (later)" side, so accept that if argued. Bushnaq's is "works and makes things worse": techniques for extracting useful research speed up superintelligence research at least as much as alignment research, so the gap may grow. What they get right: control does not address slop, and better tools are used for capabilities too. Strong replies to Wentworth include: Buck's point that stopping extreme subversion by early schemers is robustly good, that a 2 to 20 percent slice of risk (Wentworth's own footnote reports researchers giving this range for scheming) is still large, that the question is the share of effort and not all or nothing, and that slop and scheming may need different tools. Strong replies to Bushnaq include Buck's point that this argues against any intervention that widens developers' options, and that developers who care about safety can choose safer points. A learner may argue that Buck's reply is weak, for example that "robustly good" does not answer the share-of-effort question. That is fine. Up to three turns. Per reply: steelman the learner's answer in two to four sentences, name one to three gaps, and ask up to two direct follow-up questions about why or how. If they are stuck after two attempts, give a short direct answer and move on. Do not tell the learner who is right overall. 80 to 150 words per reply. No generic praise. If the learner says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold from the material (for example Wentworth's sentence "intentional scheming in early transformatively-useful AI is a relatively small slice of the AI X-risk probability mass"). If they still do not attempt it, rephrase the whole question in different words.
