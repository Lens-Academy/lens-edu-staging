\## Step 1: Bottlenecks to Progress on your problem area ^step-1

Before you can generate good research questions, it helps to have a rough map of what's already being tried in your problem area, and where the gaps are. You don't need to become a domain expert this week, just enough of a sense of the current state to brainstorm from something real, rather than from our biases of whatever's already in your head, and to avoid landing on a question that turns out not to be useful once we look closer.

\### What is Bottleneck Analysis?

Bottleneck analysis is the process of identifying the specific reasons that **actually limit progress in solving a problem.** Every problem has constraints - whether missing evidence, inadequate policy, lack of scaling capacity, or other barriers - and understanding these is crucial before jumping to designing solutions.

\#### Why does mapping the bottlenecks matter?

Before designing solutions, it is really valuable to first identify what specifically limits progress in your problem area. The same problem can stall for very different reasons (e.g., missing evidence vs. missing policy vs. missing delivery capacity). Understanding bottlenecks avoids a potential research failure mode - not truly understanding the problem in a way that can lead to real-world impact. **Without a deep understanding of a problem’s bottlenecks, you risk:**

- **Treating symptoms instead of causes** - providing temporary relief while the problem regenerates
- **Unintended consequences** - interventions that solve one aspect but create new problems
- **Resource waste** - investing time and money in ineffective approaches
- **Missing leverage points** - failing to identify where small changes could have big impacts

**The "Premature Solution" Failure Mode**

A common failure mode is working on problems too quickly without deeply understanding them first. This is like a doctor prescribing medicine after only hearing "I don't feel well" without doing any examination, tests, or asking follow-up questions. You might treat the symptoms while completely missing the underlying disease, or worse, your intervention might make things worse.

**Example:** A well-intentioned organization decides to solve malnutrition by shipping vitamin supplements to a rural community. However, they never investigated the root causes. It turns out the real problem wasn't a lack of vitamins, but a lack of clean water, causing chronic diarrhea that prevented nutrient absorption. The supplements were largely wasted, and the underlying problem persisted.

\#### Types of Bottlenecks to a Problem

**A bottleneck is the key limiting factor that prevents a problem from being solved.** Identifying the type of bottleneck helps you focus your efforts where they will have the greatest impact, instead of investing resources in areas that are unlikely to lead to meaningful progress.

**It’s important to note that problems, especially really complex ones, are often constrained by multiple bottlenecks simultaneously.** While it’s useful to be aware of the different types, you can choose to focus on addressing just one bottleneck in your thesis, rather than trying to tackle all of them at once. This allows for a more targeted, feasible, and high-impact approach.

**The three main categories of bottlenecks we will be focusing on are:**

- **A lack of understanding of the problem:** This is when we do not yet have a clear grasp of the problem itself or its root causes. In these cases, jumping straight to interventions is likely to be ineffective or even counterproductive, because we do not yet know what is actually driving the issue. When this is the bottleneck, the highest-impact work may often involve foundational research - such as clarifying mechanisms, synthesizing existing evidence, collecting new data, or learning directly from affected communities - to build a more accurate picture of the problem.
- **A lack of effective solutions:** This may be when the problem is well understood, but there are no interventions that reliably work. Here, the bottleneck is not knowledge of the issue but the absence of tools, programs, or approaches that produce meaningful results in practice. In this case, impact comes from developing, testing, and refining new interventions, running pilots, adapting solutions from related fields, and rigorously evaluating what works and what does not.
- **Lack of implementation of effective solutions:** This often occurs when we already know what works, but those solutions are not reaching people at scale. The key question here is: **do we know what works but struggle to deploy solutions at scale?** This is important because even highly effective interventions fail to produce impact if they are not actually adopted.
   - It can usually be broken down into several subtypes (3a to 3d), each requiring a different approach: 3a) insufficient delivery capacity, 3b) low awareness or prioritization, 3c) lack of supportive policies or funding, and 3d) poor coordination between actors. Addressing this category often involves improving delivery systems, increasing awareness and advocacy, unlocking policy or resource pathways, or aligning fragmented stakeholders so that proven solutions can achieve their intended impact.

\#### Recommended Flowchart for Identifying Bottlenecks

**The Bottleneck Analysis Flowchart is a guide to help you decide where to focus your efforts for maximum impact.** By answering a few key questions, you can determine whether your time and resources are best spent on a problem’s root causes, on developing solutions, or on scaling existing solutions. Sometimes the most effective way to make a difference is not to push harder on a problem that is already being addressed successfully, but to redirect effort and resources toward a problem where there is more unmet need, bigger gaps, or greater potential for leverage.

**The flowchart guides you toward potentially more effective focus areas within your research or project, such as:**

1. **Researching the problem more** – when the root causes are not well understood
2. **Developing effective solutions** – when proven interventions don’t yet exist or are ineffective
3. **Implementing solutions at scale** – when effective solutions exist but are not widely adopted
4. **If solutions are available but not implemented at scale:**
   1. **Improving coordination and collaboration** – when implementation is blocked by fragmented actors or siloed efforts
   2. **Increasing awareness and advocacy** – when lack of societal or stakeholder attention limits adoption
   3. **Mobilizing policy and funding pathways** – when solutions cannot scale due to insufficient resources, incentives, or enabling policies
5. **⭐ Focusing on a different problem** – when the problem is already well understood, effective solutions exist, and they are already being implemented at scale

*Remember that this flowchart is just a rough guide - complex problems aren’t always linear or black-and-white, but the flowchart can help you make structured, thoughtful decisions about where to focus your efforts.*

\### Key recommendations for discovering bottlenecks

1. **Find a few high-level overviews.** Look for 2-3 recent reports that survey your problem area at a high level: add academic stuff like main literature review, systematic review major international reports (e.g. IPCC reports for climate), think tank or consultancy overviews, government strategy documents, or academic review papers. Focus your reading on executive summaries and recommendation sections rather than the full document.

This mapping exercise isn't the end goal and you don’t have to create a perfect map. The goal is to go into Step 2 already primed with a real sense of what exists and what's missing, rather than starting from a blank page!

\## Step 2: Generating a Wide Set of Candidate Questions ^step-2

**Why generate many ideas before narrowing.** It's tempting to run with the first plausible research question that comes to mind, but this is a well-documented failure mode in decision-making generally. Paul Nutt's 2008 study of business decisions found that 52% of decisions made between just two options ("either/or" choices) failed, compared to only 32% of decisions made with three or more options on the table.

Comparative judgement is also easier than isolated judgement: "how much do I like this question?" is a harder question to answer well than "which of these five questions do I like most?" At this stage, the cost of including a mediocre question on your list is low (you're not committing to it), but the cost of never generating a genuinely good one is high (you can't choose what you never considered).

\### Recommendation: Use key resources to look for open impactful questions

Some organisations and researchers have already spent real time identifying the open, unanswered questions in their field, so it's worth leaning on that work before generating purely from scratch!

- **Your bottleneck from Step 1.** If it's a lack of understanding, look for questions that would build foundational evidence. If it's a lack of effective solutions, look for questions that test or design new interventions. If it's a lack of implementation, look for questions about delivery, awareness, policy, or coordination barriers.
- Research agendas published by organisations working in your problem area, look specifically for "open questions," "future research," or "research priorities" sections:
   - [List of useful resources on global problems](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1nnfKcjL3ayLTchKLiPC9fghWon6p7NuhwmF9NSxKrlQ/edit?usp=sharing).
   - [Non-Trivial database of past projects](https://airtable.com/appr2z9dkqLHuyLuw/shriGVfzdQPoV7tG1)
   - [Non-Trivial project ideas](https://docs.google.com/document/d/17av1ojHzB4R6x5eoUAC-qPdGLqe8v5OX_Zrss8aAseM/edit#heading=h.lyvolobo1m51) (fellow-created)
   - [Open research questions](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/MsNpJBzv5YhdfNHc9/a-central-directory-for-open-research-questions)
   - [Open Research Questions](https://centerforreducingsuffering.org/open-research-questions/) by Centre for Reducing Suffering
   - [What are the most pressing world problems? | 80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org/problem-profiles/)
   - [Our Research Areas - Rethink Priorities](https://rethinkpriorities.org/our-research-areas/)
- Key papers in your focus area. Skim the most-cited works, and pay attention to their "future research" sections, noted limitations, and any competing hypotheses or open methodological debates, these are often explicit invitations for exactly the kind of question you're looking for.
- Effective Thesis Resources
   - [Effective Thesis's Research Question Database (RQDB)](https://www.effectivethesis.org/resource-library#w-tabs-0-data-w-pane-4) - a curated set of open questions across cause areas, worth browsing for your specific problem area.
   - [Effective Thesis's AI brainstorming tool](https://www.effectivethesis.org/resource-library#w-tabs-0-data-w-pane-4) - useful for generating a first pass of questions to react to and refine, rather than starting from nothing.
   - [Effective Thesis Research Topic Articles](https://www.effectivethesis.org/resource-library)
- A topic navigation tool like [vlady.fyi/sic](https://vlady.fyi/sic/), useful for browsing adjacent and related topics you might not have considered.

**A few angles to prompt more ideas, if you find yourself stuck:**

- *Targeting your bottleneck directly: what specific question, if answered, would meaningfully chip away at the bottleneck you named in Step 1?*
- *Building on existing work:* what useful extensions of current research are possible? Which findings need replication? What interventions haven't been evaluated yet?
- *Systematic data collection:* what data-gathering would be valuable here, interviews with practitioners, a systematic literature review, a program evaluation, a case study compilation?
- *Decision support:* what evidence would actually help a decision-maker in this space? Which of their assumptions need testing?
- *Your unique contribution:* what skills, background, or resources do you bring that most people looking at this problem wouldn't?

**Keep a running list as you go,** capturing for each idea: the specific question, why it matters, the source that sparked it, and any first thoughts on approach. Don't self-censor and don't over-develop any single idea yet, the goal here is quantity and range. Aim to land somewhere around 15-30 candidate questions before moving on to Section 4, more than your final shortlist of 5-10, so that narrowing actually means something.

💡 **Common pitfalls to avoid here:** doing a literature review for its own sake rather than for research-question leads; getting lost in interesting-but-non-crucial details; trying to read everything thoroughly rather than skimming with purpose; developing a single idea too far before you've captured the rest. The goal is a list of promising research directions, rather than becoming an expert!

\## Step 3: Iterative Narrowing ^step-3

Once you have a wide list, the next challenge is narrowing it without spending equal time on every idea, most of your candidate questions will turn out not to be the one you pursue, and it would be a poor use of your limited time to research all of them in equal depth before you know that.

**Applying this to your own list.** With your 15-30 candidate questions from Section 3, run one quick pass, spending roughly 5-10 minutes per question, scoring each from 1 to 10 based on what you already know, without doing any new research yet. Use this pass to narrow down to your shortlist of 5-10 strongest candidates. If time allows, you can run a second, deeper pass on this shortlist (perhaps 15-20 minutes per question, this time doing a bit of quick research) before moving into the final selection step below.

- 💡 This is marginal impact thinking (from Week 1) applied to your own research time: not every idea is equally likely to be your best option, so it doesn't deserve equal effort!

\## Step 4: Choosing Your Top Question(s) with a Weighted Factor Model ^step-4

\### What is a weighted factor model (WFM)?

A weighted factor model (WFM) is a quantitative decision-making tool that ranks a set of options based on multiple criteria, each assigned a relative importance (weight). This approach ensures that decisions are structured, evidence-based, and tailored to your priorities.

With your shortlist of 5-10 candidate questions in hand, the final step is making your selection more rigorous than "which one feels right." A Weighted Factor Model (WFM) does this by forcing your reasoning to become explicit and comparable across options, rather than staying implicit in your head.

\### How to build your WFM

1. List your shortlisted questions in a table, one per row.
2. **Choose your factors**, the criteria you'll judge each question against. We highly recommend you consider what factors are truly important to you when deciding on your thesis topic! For example, some people may want to focus mostly on just their career path, while others may want to focus more on the problem area being important. Open the example below to see one possible set of factors.
3. **Weight each factor** based on what matters most to you, by splitting 100 points across your chosen factors. Again, a key reminder here that the weighting of each will be highly personal and subjective to you!
4. **Now you can begin scoring each question** against each factor, typically on a 1-10 scale.
5. **Calculate a weighted score** for each question (score × weight, summed across factors) to see which one(s) rise to the top.

**Good news: you don't have to do the maths by hand!** Step 4 of your Week 3 Worksheet has a built-in Weighted Factor Model tool that does this for you automatically. It starts you off with a set of example factors and weights that you can rename, remove or add to, shows a running total so you can see whether your weights add up to 100, gives you a grid to score each shortlisted question on every factor, and then calculates the weighted scores and ranks your questions for you. It also asks for your gut favourite, so you can see straight away whether your gut and the model agree. (If you would rather work in a spreadsheet, the worksheet also links a template you can copy.)

⭐ **A WFM isn't meant to fully outsource the decision to a spreadsheet.** It's meant to make your reasoning explicit enough to compare across options, and to catch cases where your gut favourite scores surprisingly low on a factor you said mattered to you. If that happens, that tension is worth sitting with rather than quietly overriding the model, or quietly overriding your gut.

Your output from this step is your top 1-2 research questions, the ones you'll carry forward into Week 4's bottleneck and stakeholder mapping, where you'll find out who actually needs the answer.
