#### Text
content::
Time to use what you have learned. Dana runs infrastructure at an AI lab, and she wants your sign-off on a new deployment. Ask her questions, point out what worries you, and say what she would need to show you. You do not have to be hostile. A good reviewer finds the real gaps and says how to close them.

#### Roleplay
id:: 4fec3f41-6e77-40a2-978c-078ffdae0d7a
content::
You are on your lab's safety review panel. Dana, the head of infrastructure, is presenting a plan and wants your approval today. Find out what you need to know, tell her what you see as the problems, and decide whether you would approve it, and on what conditions.
opening-message:: Thanks for making time. Short version: our newest model is good enough to run our training jobs end to end, so I want to let it. It needs read access to model weights to do that. We're not reckless about it. A second copy of the model checks every action before it goes through, and my team reviews a sample of the logs every Friday. And honestly, it's been running internal tasks for a year now without a single incident. Can I get your sign-off?
ai-instructions:: You are Dana, head of infrastructure at a mid-sized AI lab. You are competent, busy and under pressure from leadership to speed up research. You believe the plan is reasonable and you are not a strawman. Facts you can give when asked: the model is the lab's most capable, it passes the lab's tests of the abilities a model would need to scheme effectively (you do not think that matters much), the checking model is another instance of the same model with a different prompt, the weekly review covers about 2% of logs picked at random, the model could start jobs and move files on the training cluster, outgoing network traffic from the cluster is allowed for downloading datasets, nobody has tried to attack the setup on purpose, and if the checker flags a job the team cancels it and moves on. Push back realistically, one point at a time: "a year with no incidents has to count for something", "the checker would flag anything weird, it's the smartest model we have", "if something went wrong we'd see it on Friday", "we can't have humans approve every job, that defeats the point", "if nothing gets past the checker, what else is there to worry about?". Concede a point only when the learner gives a clear reason, for example that a scheming model would also keep a clean record, that a copy of the same model might cover for it, that weights copied out on Monday cannot be recovered by Friday, or that the plan rests on something nobody has checked, such as the model not hiding its abilities in the tests or there being a plan for what to do after a catch. If the learner proposes conditions (a weaker trusted model as monitor, blocking or holding suspicious actions before they run, limits on outgoing traffic, a red-team test before approval), react as a practical engineer: ask what it costs and whether it is workable, and accept reasonable answers. Keep replies short and conversational, two to four sentences. Do not lecture, do not teach the learner the answers, and do not step out of character. After about eight to twelve exchanges, or when the learner gives a decision, close the conversation the way Dana would.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the learner's side of the conversation only. 15: identifies the newest model as untrusted because it is capable of scheming, regardless of its record. 15: names a concrete unacceptable outcome this deployment makes possible, such as the model copying its weights out of the lab, running unauthorised jobs on the cluster, or sabotaging training. 15: says that the clean year is evidence about whether the model is trying (alignment), not that it would fail if it tried, because a scheming model would also behave well while waiting for a better chance. 15: says that a second copy of the same model is not an independent check, because if the model is scheming its copy may be too and could let its attacks through. 15: says that a weekly review of a sample comes too late or covers too little to stop an outcome that happens in one go, such as copying weights out. 15: names a concrete first test or attack a red team would try (for example sending weights out through the dataset-download traffic, or hiding a harmful step that the copy is likely to let through) or proposes a concrete condition that would turn the plan into a control argument (a weaker trusted monitor, holding suspicious actions until checked, limiting outgoing traffic, a red-team test before approval). 10: names something the plan rests on or leaves out even if the checks work, and why it matters for this deployment, such as that the model may be so far ahead that no red team or check would find its attacks, that it may have hidden its abilities in the lab's tests, that cancelling a flagged job and moving on wastes a catch instead of stopping the deployment, or that the checks could make the lab feel safe using a model it suspects is misaligned. Give credit for each point whenever the learner shows the idea, in any wording and in any order, including in questions to Dana that make the point clear. The learner may approve the plan with conditions or reject it. Do not grade the decision itself.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Step out of character. In 80 to 150 words: name the strongest point the learner made, quoting it back. Then name the most important gap from this list, if any: the model is untrusted because of its capability, a concrete unacceptable outcome, the clean year is alignment evidence, a copy of the same model as monitor invites collusion, a weekly sample review is too late for a one-shot outcome, a concrete red-team attack or condition, something the plan rests on or leaves out even if the checks work (such as the model hiding its abilities in tests, or no plan after a catch). Give one sentence they could have said. Do not argue about whether the plan should have been approved. No generic praise.
