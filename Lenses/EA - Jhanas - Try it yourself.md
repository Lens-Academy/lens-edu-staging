Method: reach access concentration (fully with the meditation object, only wispy background thoughts, often with very subtle breath), sustain it 'long enough', then shift attention to a pleasant sensation, preferably physical (hands, heart centre, third eye, top of head, shoulders), and do nothing else. The sensation grows non-linearly into piti (rapture, mostly physical) with sukha (joy, happiness): that is the first jhana. Stay half a minute if piti is very strong, up to 5 to 10 minutes if weaker. Do not expect it soon. Strong concentration practice is not risk-free. Brasington warns above that the first piti can be very strong and can keep you awake. Jhourney, a company that runs jhana retreats, estimates that about 1% of its participants have had difficult emotions come up during a retreat, and TIME reported on a participant with a history of depression who lived "in a state of very intense alarm" for about a month afterwards. Jhourney screens participants with exclusion criteria from psychedelic research ([TIME, 2024](https://time.com/7007856/jhourney-meditation-jhanas-retreat-bliss/)). In our view, if you have a history of depression, trauma or psychosis, talk to an experienced teacher before trying an intensive. Cheetah House, a non-profit founded by meditation researcher Willoughby Britton, lists the kinds of difficulties meditators report and offers support ([Cheetah House](https://www.cheetahhouse.org/symptoms)).

\## Experiment 1: two weeks of daily practice

**Claim tested:** that a lay person practising at home can reach access concentration and the first jhana with Brasington's method.

**Dose:** one sit a day of at least 45 minutes. Brasington writes that for learning jhanas you need "a quality daily on-the-cushion meditation practice worthy of the word daily, hopefully of at least forty-five minutes and preferably an hour or more" (*Right Concentration*, [quoted on Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/44961316-right-concentration-a-practical-guide-to-the-jhanas)). Jhourney's CEO suggests more for people learning without a retreat: "probably 1–2 hours daily to see real movement" ([Jhourney, 2026](https://jhourney.io/blog/what-are-the-jhanas)).

**Duration:** 14 days. This is our choice, not Brasington's. For scale: Jhourney claims most of its retreat participants reach jhana in under 40 hours of meditation, on a retreat with daily guidance ([TIME](https://time.com/7007856/jhourney-meditation-jhanas-retreat-bliss/)). Fourteen sits of 45 minutes are about 10 hours, so if nothing happens, that is weak evidence against the method. Brasington himself says not to expect access concentration "anytime soon".

**Method:** follow the instructions above. Use the breath to reach access concentration, then move attention to a pleasant sensation and do nothing else.

**Log after each sit** (our design): minutes sat. Did you reach what Brasington calls access concentration (fully with the breath, only wispy background thoughts)? If yes, roughly after how many minutes, and did you find a pleasant sensation? Strongest piti (0 to 10) and strongest joy or happiness (0 to 10). One line on what got in the way.

\## Experiment 2: does inner pleasure reduce the pull of outer pleasure?

**Claim tested:** MN 14 says someone who has both seen the drawbacks of sensual pleasure and attained a pleasure apart from it "cannot be tempted by sensuality". Ayya Khema says that after the second jhana we "don't go looking" for sense pleasures, though we still enjoy them. Jhourney's CEO writes: "When contentment is internally accessible, you stop needing as much from the world" ([Jhourney, 2026](https://jhourney.io/blog/what-are-the-jhanas)).

**Design** (ours, a simple baseline-then-practice comparison on yourself of the kind module 3 describes): pick one sense pleasure you reach for most days without really deciding to, such as checking your phone, snacking or sweets. For 7 days before Experiment 1, keep a tally of each time you reach for it, and each evening rate how strong the pull was that day (0 to 10). Keep the same tally and rating through Experiment 1 and for two weeks after the first time you have strong piti or joy in a sit.

**Before you start,** write down what you predict: how much will the tally and the rating change, and when? Also write down whether you think you meet the other condition in MN 14, having clearly seen the drawbacks of the pleasure you picked.

**What would count against the claim:** no change in the tally or rating after you can produce piti and joy reliably. **Confounders to note:** the sits themselves take time from the habit, expecting a change can cause one, and a stressful week can raise the pull. This test only means something if Experiment 1 worked.

\## Experiment 3: an intensive

Most teachers and Jhourney teach the jhanas on retreats (see the readings). Options, from free to expensive:
- Rob Burbea's full 2019 to 2020 jhana retreat is free to listen to on [Dharma Seed](https://dharmaseed.org/retreats/4496/), so you could follow it as a self-guided retreat at home. Without a teacher you have no one to check in with, so the safety notes above matter more.
- Brasington and other teachers in his list ([Interpretations of the Jhanas](https://www.leighb.com/jhanantp.htm)) teach residential retreats.
- Jhourney's online retreats cost $1,100 and in-person ones started at $1,800 in 2024 ([TIME](https://time.com/7007856/jhourney-meditation-jhanas-retreat-bliss/)). The company sells this service, so read its claims with that in mind.

**Measure** the Experiment 2 tally and rating for a week before, then again two weeks after and two months after. Jhourney's own survey of its retreat alumni found the "afterglow" faded within days to weeks ([Jhourney, 2024](https://www.jhourney.io/blog/life-in-hd-an-investigation-of-the-jhanas-impact-on-jhourney-retreat-attendees)), so a measurement in the first days afterwards says little about lasting change.

Respond in a practical way. If they picked Experiment 2 alone, point out it only means something once they can produce piti or joy (Experiment 1). If they picked an intensive, ask whether they have someone to check in with, and mention the safety notes if they have a history of depression, trauma or psychosis. If the timing is vague, suggest naming a start date. If they chose none, accept that, and ask in one sentence what would make it worth trying, without pressing. Do not promise results or say the jhanas will make them happier. 60 to 110 words. At most 2 replies. No generic praise.
