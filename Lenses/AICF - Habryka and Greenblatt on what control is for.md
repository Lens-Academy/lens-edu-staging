content::
In March 2024 Oliver Habryka and Ryan Greenblatt, who works at Redwood Research, wrote a dialogue on LessWrong about how useful control is. Habryka is the sceptic. He starts by restating the case for control in his own words, and Greenblatt agrees with the restatement. Read it as a list of routes, then notice which one Habryka thinks could backfire. Unit 4 covers the rest of his criticisms.

content::
Habryka's summary, which Greenblatt accepts, contains several routes from control to lower risk. List them in your own words. For each, write one sentence on what has to go right for it to work.
placeholder:: A short list is fine.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read the opening of the Habryka and Greenblatt dialogue. Routes in Habryka's summary: (1) get useful work out of AIs that may be scheming, (2) learn from failures by catching treacherous turns and using each case for a better technical and societal response, and then three ways to turn that into an existential success: (3) use the controlled AIs to make progress on alignment and come up with a plan, (4) use them for a task that lets us control much smarter systems, (5) use them to make coordination on caution easier, for example scary demos or better ways to learn what the world's population wants. Greenblatt adds mundane uses: much better security, using up "free energy" a misaligned AI could exploit, and building cheap control infrastructure for other actors. What has to go right, for example: the AIs stay controllable long enough, their work can be checked, catches lead to a response, the new capabilities are not used mainly to race. In at most six sentences, name which routes the learner found, which they missed, and whether each "has to go right" condition is something the route really needs. No generic praise. At most two turns.

Habryka worries that the useful work will mostly be capabilities work that shortens timelines. Which premise of the useful-work route does this attack? What does Greenblatt's reply concede, and what does it keep?

feedback-instructions:: The learner just read the dialogue. Habryka's worry attacks the premise that the useful work extracted from controlled AIs will go to safety, or at least that it buys more safety progress than it costs in faster AI progress and weaker coordination (he also asks why the new capabilities would not be used to thwart a coordinated slowdown). Greenblatt's reply separates what safety-concerned people with limited influence should do from what will happen in practice. He keeps the plan for the limited-influence case: apply control, delay as long as affordable with transformatively useful AIs, and use them to move beyond this regime. He concedes that there should be a concrete plan for the powerful AIs now, and says it would be better to seriously restrict scaling immediately if that were viable. In at most five sentences: say whether the learner identified the premise, correct any misreading of Greenblatt in one sentence, and add the piece they missed. If the learner takes a side, accept it, and ask what observation would tell them whether control work is mostly speeding up AI. Do not say who is right. No generic praise. At most two turns.
