Should you spend the next four years doing a PhD if you think transformative AI might arrive within three?

In [More people getting into AI safety should do a PhD](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/yi7shfo6YfhDEYizA/more-people-getting-into-ai-safety-should-do-a-phd), Adam Gleave argues that aspiring researchers often underestimate the value of doctoral training. The ability to develop an original research agenda and supervise other researchers takes considerable time to cultivate. He considers research leadership an important talent bottleneck in AI safety.

In November 2026, a LessWrong user published [I'm leaving my PhD to pursue AI Safety work](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/rjzqbfsW552PyMBNv/i-m-leaving-my-phd-to-pursue-ai-safety-work), explaining how successive updates to their AI timelines had led them to abandon their doctoral programme and pursue interpretability research full-time.

Both decisions can be reasonable under different assumptions about AI progress, personal circumstances and available alternatives.

A peculiarity of planning a career in AI safety is that the problem you are preparing to solve may change substantially while you are preparing to solve it. Some research questions may become irrelevant. Entirely new professional roles may emerge. Skills that are difficult to acquire today may become less valuable as AI capabilities improve.

There are several ways to account for this uncertainty.

**Consider a range of futures.** Suppose you expect transformative AI around 2030. How would your career plan fare if it arrived in 2028? What if it arrived in 2045? A plan that makes sense across several plausible scenarios may be preferable to one that looks exceptionally good under a single forecast.

**Distinguish reversible and irreversible decisions.** You can spend a weekend exploring a research agenda, undertake a part-time project or apply for a fellowship without committing to a new career. Leaving a permanent position, exhausting your savings or relocating to another country can be considerably harder to reverse. The amount of evidence you require before making a decision should reflect the cost of getting it wrong.

**Pay attention to what you would learn.** A three-month research project might have modest direct impact but help you discover whether you could become an excellent researcher. A conversation with an experienced grantmaker might reveal an entire category of work you had never considered. The information gained from an activity can substantially improve your subsequent career decisions.

**Set conditions for changing your mind.** If you decide to spend six months preparing for research positions, what would convince you to continue for another six months? What would convince you to explore research engineering instead? Thinking about this in advance can help you distinguish persistence from an unwillingness to abandon an unsuccessful strategy.

For a more systematic approach to these decisions, see [How to investigate your career uncertainties and make a judgement call](https://80000hours.org/career-planning/process/feedback-and-investigation/) and [Planning to adapt](https://80000hours.org/career-planning/process/plan-to-adapt/) by 80,000 Hours.
