If you ask people how to get into AI safety, you will often hear some version of the following advice: take an introductory course at BlueDot, develop your technical skills through a programme like ARENA, get some research experience at SPAR or AI Safety Camp, produce your first research output, apply to a more selective fellowship such as MATS, and eventually secure a position at the organisation.

This is a reasonably well-established pathway for an aspiring technical researcher. Analyses of the fellowship programmes themselves suggest that this route does give you a substantial chance of success. You can read about the specific numbers and career trajectories in [this analysis of a dataset of 600 AI safety fellows](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/SJBBgupFx7SXyBj2B/where-do-ai-safety-fellows-go-analyzing-a-dataset-of-600).

Albeit if you have spent years managing engineering teams, working in government, building organisations etc etc your optimal route into AI safety may look quite different.

In [How three mid-career professionals found work in AI safety](https://80000hours.substack.com/p/how-three-mid-career-professionals), Matt Beard interviews people who transitioned from humanitarian work, product management and finance. Cameron, who initially considered becoming a technical researcher, discovered through a BlueDot research project that this might be a poor use of their existing strengths. They eventually became a research manager, drawing on a decade of professional experience.

The interviewees speak candidly about their experiences and share a wealth of practical lessons. We recommend reading the article in full and making notes on the insights and advice that are relevant to your own circumstances.

There are, however, three things that most people entering AI safety need to accomplish, regardless of their intended role or previous experience.

- Develop sufficient AI safety context
- Produce evidence that you can do useful work
- Get to know the community and let the community get to know you

\### 1. Develop sufficient AI safety context

You may have noticed how frequently people in the field use the word *context*. In career discussions, it usually refers to a combination of knowledge and judgement that allows someone to work effectively on AI safety problems.

At a minimum, this involves:

- Understanding the terminology, central concepts and ongoing debates in the field.
- Being able to form and justify expectations about future AI progress and the associated risks.
- Understanding the theories of change behind different approaches to AI safety.
- Being able to reason about whether a particular intervention is useful, which assumptions its success depends on and how it could fail.
- Having a working knowledge of the organisations, researchers and activities relevant to your intended work.

This matters in research and non-research positions in technical and non technical agendas.

In their [2025 survey of 38 AI safety organisation leaders](https://80000hours.org/2025/09/survey-results-what-ai-safety-orgs-want-in-a-hire/), Conor Barnes and Benjamin Todd found that several respondents considered a BlueDot course or a fellowship sufficient context for candidates with relevant professional skills, outside technical research roles.

For other positions, however, the bar can be considerably higher. For example, you may come across statements like this one from [How to Apply to AI Safety Fellowships (and Beyond)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/PiP4JqQFKhoqHGG2n/how-to-apply-to-ai-safety-fellowships-and-beyond):

> One of the most important signals people are looking for in an applicant is fluency and good takes across many sub-branches of AI safety.

The article also offers a sophisticated and somewhat challenging, but potentially highly effective, strategy for approaching job applications. Importantly, this strategy does not involve misrepresenting your experience or qualifications. We should emphasise that the AI safety community takes intellectual honesty very seriously and is particularly sensitive to misleading claims about one's abilities or achievements. A simpler piece of advice would be: be specific, avoid embellishment, and leave out the filler.

You may be expected to understand the intellectual history of the field, be familiar with the arguments and previously expressed positions of its leading researchers, and have well-developed views on the merits of competing approaches to AI safety. As discussed in [Why experienced professionals fail to land high-impact roles](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/b82SLXwEHRCs3TFJA/why-experienced-professionals-fail-to-land-high-impact-roles), this is especially relevant to strategic research, fieldbuilding, grantmaking and organisational leadership. In some cases, an exceptional academic or professional track record may compensate for limited context, but most applicants should expect to invest substantial time in developing it.

The amount of context you need depends both on the decisions you will be expected to make and on the organisation you are hoping to join.

\### 2. Produce evidence that you can do useful work

Acquiring context and demonstrating that you have acquired it are two separate tasks.

Suppose you are an experienced project manager applying to an AI safety research organisation. Your previous employment provides evidence of your project management skills. The employer still needs to establish whether you understand the research environment well enough to exercise those skills effectively.

Our advice is to do small, **relevant projects**. The emphasis here is on *relevant*. If you want to work in AI governance, try writing a policy brief. If you want to become a research manager, try coordinating a small research team. Look at what people in your intended role actually do and find opportunities to do some of it yourself.

There are good reasons to do this even if nobody ever sees your work. You get to practise precisely the skills you will need on the job. You also get to find out whether you actually enjoy the work. Being fascinated by mechanistic interpretability papers and enjoying the process of producing one are two quite different experiences. A few weeks spent on a small research project may tell you more about your suitability for a research career than a year spent reading about the subject.

From an employer's perspective, seeing that someone has already done the kind of work they would be hired to do is a remarkably strong signal. If you are applying to run a research fellowship and have previously organised a successful research programme, the hiring team has something concrete to evaluate. They can look at the decisions you made, the problems you encountered, how you dealt with them and what you achieved.

Depending on the role you are interested in, these might include a small research project, a piece of software, a post explaining your reasoning about an AI safety problem, a forecasting track record, an event you organised or an initiative you helped develop.

Barnes and Todd's survey found a recurring recommendation from employers:

> Publish your outputs.

This helps explain the prevalence of public side projects and writing in the AI safety community. They allow potential employers and collaborators to examine your work, understand how you think and assess what you might be capable of doing with more resources.

For practical suggestions on finding and conducting such projects, see Matt Beard's [How to speedrun your AI safety job search and upskilling](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/jCBtpJovoFgKtpaZP/how-to-speedrun-your-ai-safety-job-search-and-upskilling)

\### 3. Get to know the community and let the community get to know you

We have already mentioned several times how much networking and publicly visible work matter in a field as small, young and competitive as AI safety is today. We will say it again: talk to people whenever you get the opportunity.

There are two sides to this process.

You want to get to know the people working in the field: understand their views, learn about their work, discover the problems they are struggling with, and hear about opportunities that may never appear on a job board, ideas that may never become research proposals, and doubts and disagreements that may never make it into published work.

You also want people to acquire enough experience of interacting with you to say something meaningful about your abilities.

Imagine someone asking a researcher whether they know a good candidate for an open position. There is a considerable difference between being someone they met at a conference and being someone they can describe in concrete terms:

- *We worked on a research project together. She was very good at identifying flaws in our experimental design.*
- *He organised a workshop for our team and handled several unexpected problems independently.*
- *We have discussed AI governance several times. She has a good understanding of the institutional constraints involved.*

This is one reason collaborative projects are so valuable. They create opportunities for people to observe your judgement, reliability and professional skills in action.

Renn, one of the professionals interviewed by Matt Beard, offers this advice based on her experience of transitioning into AI safety and subsequently working inside an AI safety organisation:

> If you do good work, they will remember you and vouch for you when the time comes.

Personal recommendations are valuable, although many organisations also hire candidates through open applications and anonymised work tests.

When considering your next career step, try to identify which of these three ingredients you are currently missing. Imagine that an organisation you would love to work for had an opening tomorrow. What would give them pause about hiring you?

One of the peculiarities of entering AI safety mid-career is that you can be a highly experienced professional and a complete beginner in the field at the same time.

A useful question to ask yourself is: **what can I already do that would take a talented 23-year-old another ten years to learn?**

Unfortunately, being an experienced professional in a field that needs experienced professionals does not necessarily make getting hired straightforward.

In [Why experienced professionals fail to land high-impact roles](https://fieldbuilding.substack.com/p/why-experienced-professionals-fail), Gergő Gáspár describes a pattern he encountered repeatedly: people with substantial professional experience complete an introductory AI safety course, start applying for jobs, and get rejected even from positions considerably more junior than those they previously held.

He identifies insufficient context as one possible explanation.

There is also a problem on the employer's side. The authors of [AI safety is maturing. Is its generalist talent pipeline keeping up?](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/ZQ93QauB3kux3fYNG/) suggest that hiring managers who have spent most of their careers within AI safety may struggle to evaluate professional achievements in unfamiliar industries.

- This makes relevant projects especially useful for mid-career professionals. You have already demonstrated your ability to work professionally elsewhere. A relatively small project within AI safety may help establish how that ability translates into the new environment
- “Try again” is good advice for you. Don’t take single rejection as a strong signal of the absence of fit. Maybe it was not your fault at all.

\### Practical considerations

Mid-career professionals also tend to face constraints that standard AI safety career advice rarely accounts for. There are several resources specifically intended to help experienced professionals navigate this transition:

- [Successif](https://www.successif.org/career-paths) — career guidance and opportunities for mid- and senior-career professionals entering AI safety.
- [High Impact Professionals](https://www.highimpactprofessionals.org/) — career transition programmes and a talent directory connecting experienced professionals with relevant organisations.
