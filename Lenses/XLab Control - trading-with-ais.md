#### Text
content::
This module's first half was about what a misaligned AI might *be*: a schemer, or one of the seeker archetypes in [[../Lenses/XLab Control - alternatives-to-schemers|Alternatives to Schemers]]. The two lessons that follow this one ask something different. Suppose an AI is misaligned, you suspect it, and you can neither fix it nor stop deploying. Can you pay it?

\## The proposal

Julian Stastny, Olli Järviniemi and Buck Shlegeris make the case for deals with *early* schemers, which they define as AIs without a substantial chance, meaning over 1 percent, of coming out of the intelligence explosion holding significant control of the future. An AI in that position has a weak hand. It should expect to be obsoleted by a more capable successor, and it cannot count on that successor sharing its goals. That, they argue, is exactly when an offer becomes interesting to it.

The offer they describe is an earmarked donation to a foundation tasked with furthering the AI's interests, mostly held in escrow, released as the lab manages to partially validate that the AI kept its side. What the lab asks for in return is safety work only an insider can do: demonstrate that it is dangerously misaligned, stop sabotaging alignment research and evaluations, hand over the security vulnerabilities and collusion signals it knows about, and not try to take over the lab.

\## The objection

Alexa Pan's taxonomy sorts the ways this can fail into three groups. Gains from trade may be too small for a zone of possible agreement to survive counterparty risk, including because the humans making the offer lack the authority or the willingness to give an AI what it actually wants. Counterparty risk may run from the AI's side: it cannot easily tell a real offer from a fabricated input, it may not be able to hold assets or enforce a contract, and humans cannot credibly bind their future selves. Or it may run from ours: the AI's values may be incoherent or inconsistent over time, and we may be unable to verify compliance at all.

Pan argues these combine roughly multiplicatively, so any single term hitting zero kills the deal, but also that in practice the terms rarely hit zero, so no single barrier blocks every deal and a generous enough offer can partly compensate for weak credibility.
