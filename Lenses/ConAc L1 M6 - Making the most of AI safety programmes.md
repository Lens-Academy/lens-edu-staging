AI safety has developed an extensive ecosystem of training programmes. Some can be completed over a weekend, others involve several months of full-time research.

Here is a rough guide to what you can expect from different programmes:

- **Foundational courses** (BlueDot Impact) help you acquire context and understand the problems the field is working on.
- **Technical bootcamps** (ARENA, ML4Good) teach practical skills, such as implementing ML algorithms, working with transformers and conducting interpretability experiments.
- **Hackathons and project sprints** (Apart Research, BlueDot) allow you to experience a compressed version of the research process and produce a small piece of work.
- **Part-time research programmes** (SPAR, AI Safety Camp) allow you to spend several months working on a project with a mentor and collaborators, often alongside your regular employment or studies.
- **Full-time research fellowships** (MATS, Pivotal, GovAI, ERA, IAPS, PIBBSS) typically provide mentorship, research resources and an opportunity to produce a substantial research output.
- **Founder programmes and incubators** (Catalyze Impact, BlueDot) help people develop new projects and organisations, test their viability and secure initial resources.

For a more detailed account of how these programmes fit together for aspiring technical researchers, see Mikhail Mironov's [Roadmap through AI safety programs for early-career technical researchers](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/muH6H9i8CtNAubcoo/roadmap-through-ai-safety-programs-for-early-career).

You can find upcoming programmes, application deadlines and participation requirements in the [AISafety.com training directory](https://aisafety.com/training)

\### Choosing a programme

Think back to the three things we discussed in the previous submodule: acquiring context, producing evidence of your abilities and developing professional relationships.

A well-chosen programme can help you accomplish all three. But its value to you depends considerably on what you already have and what you are missing.

Before applying, have a look at what previous participants actually produced. Read their research outputs, look at their subsequent careers, and, seriously, speak to a few alumni. For research fellowships, investigate the prospective mentors and their work. The choice of mentor and project matter at least as much as the choice of fellowship. It is actually might be even better to choose a potential mentor and then find out which fellowships they are participating in.

It is also worth deciding what you would like to leave the programme with. A certificate is probably the least interesting thing you can get out of a good fellowship.
