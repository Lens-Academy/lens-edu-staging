\## Two studies, one kind of tool
Generative AI can improve work, and it can make it worse. The same tool produced opposite outcomes in two studies. The difference is the condition in which the person uses the tool.

\## Bednar: help when the person stays in the loop
Bednar and colleagues (2025) ran a randomised controlled trial with about 100 upper-level law students at Minnesota. The students completed a sequence of common legal tasks: synthesise legal source material, apply the material to a client's problem, and write a memo. The authors divided the students into two groups. One group used the AI during the synthesis and revision stages. The other group could not use the AI until the revision stage. Neither group used the AI during the application task. This design let the study measure whether earlier AI use changed later reasoning when the tool was not available.

Students who used the AI during synthesis produced much better analyses. The gain was about 50 to 70 percent. It was larger than earlier studies found. They also finished faster. The benefit carried into the application task, where no student had the AI: students who used the AI earlier performed better than students who never used it. The authors read the result as indirect: better initial work gave later work a stronger base.

The revision stage told a different story. Both groups used the AI to improve the memo each student had written alone. The AI helped the students with the weakest memos. Students who had written strong memos often produced worse revisions. The AI displaced good reasoning, even among capable students.

The study has limits. It used law students, not practising lawyers. The tasks were more structured than most real assignments. It does not measure the long-term effects of sustained AI use.

\## Budzyń: harm when the practice disappears
Budzyń and colleagues (2025) ran a retrospective, observational study at four endoscopy centres in Poland. The centres took part in a larger trial called ACCEPT and introduced AI for polyp detection at the end of 2021. The authors compared colonoscopies done in the three months before the AI arrived with those done in the three months after.

A colonoscopy is a procedure in which a doctor examines the large bowel with a camera. The adenoma detection rate is the share of procedures in which the doctor finds at least one polyp. It is a clinical measure: it records what the doctor actually found, not what the doctor believed about their own work.

1,443 patients underwent a colonoscopy without the AI in the study period: 795 before the AI arrived and 648 after. The adenoma detection rate fell from 28.4 percent to 22.4 percent, an absolute drop of 6.0 percentage points (95 percent confidence interval -10.5 to -1.6; p=0.0089). In the multivariable analysis, exposure to the AI lowered the odds of finding a polyp by a factor of 0.69.

The drop appeared on procedures done without the AI, where the tool was not present. That is the important detail: the loss sits in the doctor, not in the tool. The design is observational, so it cannot prove that the AI caused the decline. Patient mix or other factors may contribute. The pattern still matches the concern this unit raises.

\## What separates them
The two studies used the same kind of tool. The outcomes differed because the conditions differed. Five features separate a case where AI helps from a case where it harms:
- the expertise of the person using the tool;
- whether the AI supports the thinking the person directs, or replaces the judgement;
- whether the task gives feedback the person can learn from;
- whether the output is easy to verify;
- the stakes and the time pressure.

In the legal study the person stayed in control of the task, and the task gave feedback. In the endoscopy study the AI performed the whole detection, and the repetitions that keep a skill alive disappeared. Where the person stays in control, AI is more likely to help. Where the tool takes over the job, AI is more likely to harm.

\## Your turn
Which features above, expertise, feedback quality, task verifiability, stakes, or time pressure, do you think explain why the two studies diverged? Name one domain of your own work where you would expect AI to help, and one where you would expect it to harm.

assessment-instructions:: The student has just compared Bednar and Budzyń in Unit 3 of "Thinking about Thinking". They are explaining the divergence through conditions, not labelling AI good or bad, and applying it to their own work. This is one reflective question, not a multi-turn sequence.

Key points to check they grasped:
- Bednar: randomised controlled trial, about 100 law students, AI in early synthesis improved analysis by roughly 50 to 70 percent and later no-AI performance; AI in revision regressed strong work
- Budzyń: retrospective observational study, four centres, adenoma detection fell 6.0 percentage points (28.4% to 22.4%) on non-AI procedures after AI exposure; the outcome is a clinical record of polyps found, not a self-report
- The contrast is about conditions (who stays in the loop, feedback, verifiability, stakes, time pressure), not the tool itself
- Neither study generalises to all lawyers, all doctors, or all knowledge workers

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style: warm but rigorous. Do not over-validate; avoid generic praise. Correct errors in one sentence.

What to do: acknowledge the conditions they named; if they declared AI simply good or bad, return them to the conditional framing; if they picked domains, check the reasoning links to the features; close by pointing to the optional full-study cards and the next lens.

What not to do: run a multi-turn sequence; introduce the other studies; over-lecture. This is one turn, then send them on.
