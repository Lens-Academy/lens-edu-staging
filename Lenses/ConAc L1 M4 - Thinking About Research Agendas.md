\### How do research agendas relate to each other?

Some agendas make conflicting assumptions about how AI systems will develop or what makes them dangerous. Others are complementary. The success of one research programme may provide the knowledge or technical infrastructure needed for another to succeed.

For example, AI control research depends on our ability to evaluate the effectiveness of monitoring protocols. Advances in evaluations can therefore make previously impractical control strategies viable.

In other cases, even establishing the relationship between two agendas requires substantial intellectual work. Consider research on the objectives of individual AI agents and research on the gradual disempowerment of humanity. The former tends to explain risks in terms of the properties of individual intelligent systems. The latter examines how interactions between technological, economic and social systems could produce undesirable outcomes. These approaches describe the world using different conceptual frameworks. Establishing how their claims relate to each other may require developing a conceptual vocabulary that accommodates both.

A further complication is that the assumptions behind an agenda are seldom documented in one convenient place. Some may be articulated in a research proposal, others in an old LessWrong post or an interview with one of its authors. Some may never have been made explicit.

Much of what experienced researchers recognise as a deep understanding of the AI safety landscape consists in being able to reason about these relationships. It involves understanding where different research programmes reinforce each other, where their assumptions conflict, and where an apparent disagreement arises from differences in how the problem itself is conceptualised.

How can you develop this ability?

\### Reconstructing a Theory of Change

Start with a research agenda you would like to understand. Try to reconstruct the reasoning that would make pursuing it a sensible decision.

- **What is the problem?** Which undesirable outcome motivates this research? How is that outcome expected to arise?
- **What is the proposed intervention?** Which part of the causal process leading to that outcome is the research intended to affect?
- **What would constitute progress?** What intermediate results are researchers trying to obtain?
- **How would these results be used?** Who would use them, under what circumstances, and what would they be able to do as a consequence?
- **What assumptions does this reasoning depend on?** Consider technological expectations, institutional conditions, the availability of complementary research results and the behaviour of relevant actors.
- **What evidence supports these assumptions?** What observations would give you reason to reconsider them?

You will rarely find answers to all these questions in a single publication. A technical paper may explain a proposed method in considerable detail while saying very little about the circumstances under which its application would reduce catastrophic risk.

It can be useful to reconstruct the strongest plausible Theory of Change yourself. Keep track of which claims the authors explicitly make and which assumptions you have introduced in your reconstruction. Where several interpretations are possible, preserve that uncertainty.

Once you have reconstructed the Theories of Change of several agendas, you can examine their relationships. Do they depend on the same unresolved scientific problem? Would progress in one make another more viable? Does the case for pursuing one become weaker if the assumptions behind another turn out to be correct?

\### From understanding to prioritisation

At this point, you might be wondering how you are supposed to keep track of hundreds of research agendas, their assumptions and the relationships between them.

Our advice is simple: you don't have to.

As you work in the field, you will naturally acquire familiarity with the research most relevant to your work. You will also accumulate knowledge about adjacent areas through collaborations, conversations and reading. There is no need to maintain an exhaustive mental map of AI safety.

A more manageable starting point is the risk you want to reduce.

At a high level, there are two ways of improving the prospects of a desirable AI future: making beneficial developments more likely or bringing them forward, and preventing or delaying developments that could lead to harmful outcomes. The practical value of either approach depends on how the relevant risk arises.

Try to understand the causal structure of the problem. What needs to happen for the undesirable outcome to occur? Which conditions are necessary for it? Which of them could conceivably be changed? What knowledge or technical capabilities would we need to change them?

Some of these questions will lead you towards existing research agendas. Others may expose gaps between them or reveal promising interventions that few researchers are currently pursuing.

Starting from the problem also gives you a basis for deciding how much attention a particular agenda deserves. Its prominence within the AI safety community is a poor substitute for understanding how its success would affect the outcome you care about.

The skill we want you to develop is the ability to reason from a problem to a research direction, to understand the case for pursuing that direction, and to assess how much of that case survives scrutiny. This will remain useful long after today's maps of the research landscape have become outdated.

\### Conclusion

The AI safety research landscape is not a fixed collection of problems with a corresponding collection of proposed solutions.

It is an evolving collection of attempts to understand problems, formulate them in ways that make scientific progress possible, develop solutions, test those solutions, and work out how the resulting knowledge and technology might contribute to a desirable future.

Some of these attempts are organised into clearly articulated research programmes. Others are less explicit. Some are supported by substantial bodies of empirical evidence. Others are motivated primarily by theoretical arguments or conjectures about future systems.

We can learn about existing agendas by reading reviews, exploring maps and examining the work researchers have produced. We can also reason about agendas that do not yet exist by considering different problems, intervention points and forms of scientific progress.

And we can use Theory of Change to investigate how these different efforts might contribute to what we ultimately want to achieve.
