#### Text
content::
Jeffery Martin is a social scientist who has spent more than 15 years interviewing people who say they live in a lasting state of the kind contemplative traditions call awakening or enlightenment ([his site](https://drjefferymartin.com/)). His academic name for it is "persistent non-symbolic experience" (PNSE). In his popular work he calls it "fundamental wellbeing" ([Life Itself, summarising Martin](https://lifeitself.org/learn/fundamental-wellbeing)). He also designed a paid online course that aims to bring people into this state, and this module looks closely at its numbers.

This module covers what Martin reports, what stays the same in the people he studied, what his course claims, what critics say, and a few things you can test on yourself. First, Martin describes the state in a short interview on the TV series *Closer To Truth*.

#### Question: Open
id:: 3809c054-3207-4e43-a338-c61ff472ef09
content::
Martin says the far end of the continuum has "no emotion" and "no sense of agency whatsoever", but "tremendous well-being", and that people there would never want to go back. The interviewer says "count me out". Where do you stand right now, and what would you want to know before you would trust either reaction?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just watched a short Closer To Truth interview in which Jeffery Martin describes persistent non-symbolic consciousness: the individualized sense of self dissipates, agency declines until it disappears, it is a continuum, and the far end has no emotion and no agency but, he says, "tremendous well-being". Outsiders say "count me out", while people in the state say they would never go back. The learner says where they stand and what they would want to know before trusting either reaction.

This is a reflection, not a test. There is no right position. In 60 to 110 words, short paragraphs, no lists: restate their position in one sentence, then pick the most useful thing they said they would want to know and say briefly which later part of this module bears on it. The module next reads Martin's paper on the locations and what did not change in his participants (partner reports of stress, implicit bias tests, people who lost or rejected the state), then the evidence for his course, then experiments. If they did not name anything they would want to know, ask one question: whose report about someone's wellbeing would they trust more, the person's own or someone who lives with them, and why? Do not argue for or against the far end. No generic praise. One turn, then send them on.
