title: "Hug the Query"
tldr: The closer your evidence is to the question itself, the more it settles. Watching the plane fly beats checking the calculations, and checking the calculations beats comparing who has more authority.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky argues for looking at evidence as close as possible to the question, because closer evidence screens off more distant arguments. In his example, Lord Kelvin has more authority than the Wright Brothers, but authority matters less once you check both sides' calculations, and the calculations matter less once you watch the plane fly. Arguing about who is more rational, or which bias someone shows, is distant evidence and cannot settle a factual question ("If there are biased reasons to say the Sun is shining, that doesn't make it dark out"). Judging a speaker is sometimes necessary when you cannot check the closer evidence, but it should feel like something is missing. The question gives a council argument over whether a traffic light reduced accidents, with accident records available, and asks which evidence should settle it and why it makes the other two arguments matter much less.

#### Question: Open
id:: 7d8457d3-2765-4e08-aa5b-b9146c61bd45
content::
A town put a traffic light at a busy crossing a year ago. At a council meeting, members argue over whether it reduced accidents.

- Ana says the engineer who proposed the light has been wrong before, so it probably did not help.
- Ben says Ana opposed the light from the start, so she is biased against it.
- The town's police have a record of every accident at the crossing for the year before and the year after the light went in.

Which of these should settle the question, and why does it make the other two matter much less?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay argues that evidence closer to the question carries more weight and "screens off" more distant arguments. Once you can check the direct evidence, the authority or rationality of the people making claims adds little. Pointing out that someone is biased does not settle a factual question, because biased reasons for a claim do not make it false. In this scenario, the accident records are the evidence closest to the question. The engineer's track record and Ana's possible bias are about the people, not about the accidents.

40: Picks the accident records as what should settle the question.

60: Explains why they make the other two matter much less: the records bear directly on whether accidents went down, while the engineer's past mistakes and Ana's bias are only indirect clues about who is likely to be right. Once you have the direct evidence, those clues add little or nothing, and a person's bias or poor record does not make the light work or fail. An answer may also note limits of the records (other changes in traffic, too few accidents to tell) without losing points.

Cap at 40 if the answer treats Ben's point about Ana's bias, or the engineer's record, as settling the question.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The accident records. They are about the actual question, whether accidents went down. The engineer's record and Ana's bias are only hints about who is likely to be right, and once you can look at the accidents themselves you don't need the hints. Ana being biased wouldn't make the light work, and the engineer being wrong before wouldn't make it fail."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Hug the Query" and judged a council argument over a traffic light. The essay's key ideas: evidence closer to the question is stronger and screens off more distant arguments (authority, then calculations, then watching the plane fly). Accusing someone of bias cannot settle a factual question. Judging the speaker is a fallback for when you cannot check, and should feel like something is missing. Here the accident records are the closest evidence, and the engineer's record and Ana's bias are about people, not accidents.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap, usually saying the records are better without saying why they make the arguments about people unnecessary. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example whether Ana being biased would change how many accidents happened. At 100, confirm briefly. If they raise limits of the records, treat that as good thinking, not as a gap.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as how watching the plane fly made Lord Kelvin's authority irrelevant. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
