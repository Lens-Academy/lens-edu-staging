#### Text
content::
The Buddhist teaching on the self is called **anatta**, which the Access to Insight glossary renders as "not-self" and "ownerless" ([glossary](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/glossary.html)). Its best-known statement is a short discourse which, as the translator's note says, was "the first of the Buddha's discourses during which his listeners became arahants", that is, fully awakened. It goes through five **aggregates**, the "physical and mental components of the personality and of sensory experience in general" ([same glossary](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/glossary.html)): form, feeling, perception, fabrications and consciousness. **Fabrications** are, in the same glossary, "the forces and factors that fashion things (physical or mental), the process of fashioning, and the fashioned things that result".

Read it whole. The argument is the same for each aggregate, so the text abbreviates the middle ones.

\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from the three readings, without looking back. No need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read SN 22.59 (tr. Thanissaro), Thanissaro's essay "No-self or Not-self?" and excerpts from Sujato's reply, then wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- SN 22.59: each of the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, fabrications, consciousness) is not self. Two reasons: if it were self, it would not lend itself to dis-ease and you could say "let it be thus, let it not be thus", and it is inconstant, so stressful, so not fit to be regarded as "mine, my self, what I am". This applies to every instance, past, present or future. Seeing this leads to disenchantment, dispassion and release. The discourse never says "there is no self".
- Thanissaro: asked directly whether there is a self, the Buddha stayed silent. Both "there is a self" and "there is no self" are views that lead to stress. The useful question is whether holding onto something causes stress and whether it is really me or mine. Anatta is a not-self strategy, not a doctrine of no-self.
- Sujato: the not-self versus no-self distinction does not exist in Pali. Bodhi: the Buddha avoided saying "there is no self" to avoid siding with the annihilationists and to avoid confusing Vacchagotta, not because there is a hidden true self or because it was only a strategy. "All phenomena are nonself" means no self will be found anywhere. Sujato: strategy and ontology cannot be separated, a strategy works only if it fits what is real. The Buddha saw clinging to a self "that is just not there" as the trap.

Be a brief, honest mirror: say what they got right without inflation, name what is missing, correct any error in one sentence, and treat gaps as normal. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. No follow-up questions and no generic praise. One reply, then tell them to move on.

\## Learning question
A friend who has just started meditating says: "The Buddha taught that there is no self. So my job in meditation is to look closely until I confirm that I don't exist."

Using the three readings, what is wrong or too quick in this?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read SN 22.59, Thanissaro's "No-self or Not-self?" and excerpts from Sujato's reply, and got a plausible but flawed claim: "The Buddha taught that there is no self. So my job in meditation is to look closely until I confirm that I don't exist." This is practice, not a test.

Points a strong answer finds (any two are good):
- The discourse itself does not say "there is no self". It examines each aggregate and says this is not self, because it changes, is stressful and cannot be controlled. Whether that adds up to "no self" is exactly what Thanissaro and Sujato dispute.
- On Thanissaro's reading the Buddha set the question "is there a self?" aside, and holding "there is no self" is itself a view that causes stress. The practice is to ask of each thing one holds onto whether it is stressful and really mine, and to let go. Its test is whether suffering lessens, not whether one "confirms" non-existence.
- On Sujato's and Bodhi's reading the teaching does say no self will be found anywhere, but the Buddha avoided the phrase "the self does not exist" because it sounded like annihilationism (a self that exists and is destroyed). So "I don't exist" misstates even the no-self reading: what is denied is a lasting, essential self, not the person who meditates.
- What the friend would "look" for matters: the sutta's tests are change, stress and lack of control, which can be checked in experience, while non-existence cannot be directly observed.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in 2 to 3 sentences, name one gap, and ask one or two direct follow-ups (for example: "What exactly does the sutta give as the reason form is not self?" or "On Thanissaro's reading, how would you tell whether the practice is working?"). If they are stuck after two attempts, give two of the points briefly. Do not say which reading is right. 100 to 180 words per reply. After 3 replies ask whether they want to continue. No generic praise.

\## Further reading
The [Wikipedia article on Anatta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatta) surveys how different Buddhist schools read the teaching. A classic later image of the conventional self is the [chariot simile](https://encyclopediaofbuddhism.org/wiki/Simile_of_the_chariot) from the *Questions of King Milinda*: "chariot" is "the designation in common use" for pole, axle, wheels, framework and the other parts together, and the simile applies the same to the self and the aggregates.
