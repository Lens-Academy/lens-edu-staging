\## Before you read

The last lens ended on a handoff: every serious plan finishes with AIs doing most of the remaining safety work. This one asks what all that work is buying. Every reading in this course so far has pointed at prevention: stop the takeover, catch the deception, keep the humans in the loop. The course title also promised shaping, and shaping needs a target.

William MacAskill opens Forethought's Better Futures series with the cleanest version of an uncomfortable claim: preventing catastrophe and achieving a good future are different jobs, and almost everyone works on the first. Whether you buy his numbers or not, the split itself is the tool to take away.

One thing to hold while you read. Frontier-lab leadership has published positive visions of its own: Dario Amodei's Machines of Loving Grace ([[../articles/Amodei-machines-of-loving-grace]]) and The Adolescence of Technology ([[../articles/darioamodei-dario-amodei-the-adolescence-of-technology]]) describe futures worth wanting, written from inside a company building the systems. MacAskill writes from a research nonprofit about what anyone, anywhere, should aim at. Who ends up holding the pen on the good future is itself an open question, and the question below asks where you think it currently sits.

A practical note: this page carries our short summary of the essay's argument in our words, checked against the original, with a link out; you will find it after the question. The full essay is a fifteen-minute read and worth it if this lens catches you.

*The framing, questions, and summary on this page were written by Claude, an AI, and reviewed by a human. The essay they describe is MacAskill's own work; follow the link to read it as he wrote it.*

\## Your turn first

Commit before you read, one word first. Suppose the prevention work succeeds: no takeover, no extinction, humanity still here and still in charge. Is the future that follows good by default? Yes or no. Defend one of them; "it depends" teaches you nothing here.

Then write one sentence naming what is doing the work in your answer. Not the conclusion again: the belief underneath it. If you said yes, name the force that pulls things toward good once the disaster is dodged. If you said no, name what goes missing even though everyone survived.

Then one more line, and it is the harder one. In the good outcome you just pictured, who set the priorities? Name the actor you actually expect to hold that pen: labs, governments, voters, markets, the AIs, nobody in particular.

\## The argument, in brief

What follows is our summary of William MacAskill, "Introducing Better Futures", the opening essay of Forethought's Better Futures series (August 2025). The full essay: https://www.forethought.org/research/introducing-better-futures. Where words are quoted, they are his.

MacAskill splits "make the future go better" into two factors. Surviving: avoiding near-term catastrophe, extinction or permanent disempowerment. Flourishing: how good the future is, given that we survive. Most safety work, and every reading in this course so far, aims at the first factor. The series argues that work on the second is "in the same ballpark of priority".

The scale argument runs on headroom: "As long as we’re closer to the ceiling on *Survival* than we are on *Flourishing*", the second factor has more room to improve. His illustrative numbers: suppose our chance of surviving this century is above 80 percent, a view he calls widely held, while the future we get if we survive is under 10 percent as good as the best feasible future. Then fully solving survival buys 20 more percentage points of chance at a 10-percent-good future, about 2 percent of the best future's value. Fully solving flourishing buys an 80 percent chance at a fully good one, about 72 percent of that value: 36 times more. Push the estimates to 99 percent survival and 1 percent flourishing and the ratio approaches ten thousand. On his central numbers, merely nudging the value of the surviving future from 10 percent to 12.5 percent would be worth as much as eliminating extinction risk entirely. The numbers are illustrative; the structure is the point. He adds a subtler one: worlds that needed saving are, once saved, likelier to be badly coordinated ones, which quietly discounts survival work and not flourishing work.

On neglectedness: most people, on self-interest and on their moral views alike, care far more about near-term catastrophe than long-term flourishing, so flourishing failures attract less work. They are also harder to see. Ask someone for utopia and you usually get today's world minus its worst flaws; MacAskill argues the gap between that common-sense utopia and the best feasible future may dwarf the gap between today and the utopia.

On tractability he is candid: he calls it the strongest argument against his own view, and it is why he claims same ballpark rather than higher priority. The work he thinks might be tractable includes reducing the risk of a human concentration of power, giving advanced AI good reflective values rather than mere obedience, and improving how post-AGI decisions get made, from space governance to the rights of digital beings.

The rest of the series argues three claims: a flourishing future is not the default even if we avoid catastrophe, because it is a narrow target that future people are unlikely to aim at; persistent positive impact on the long-run future is possible by routes other than preventing catastrophe; and there are concrete things to do about it today.

One refusal matters as much as the claims. MacAskill names no destination: "we should act on the assumption that we have almost no idea what the best feasible futures would look like", and committing now to one vision would repeat the mistake of past utopian projects. What he does endorse is a near-term target he calls viatopia, "a state of the world where society can guide itself towards near-best outcomes, whatever they may be": risk low, many moral views alive, many futures still open, big decisions made carefully.

Now hold your yes or no against this. The argument does not say prevention was the wrong bet. It says prevention alone leaves most of the value on the table, and that getting the rest is a job someone has to actually be doing.
