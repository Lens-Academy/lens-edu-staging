\## Before you read

The last lens ended with a handoff: every serious plan finishes with AIs doing most of the remaining safety work. This lens asks what all that work is for. Every reading in this course so far has been about prevention: stop the takeover, catch the deception, keep the humans in the loop. The course title also promised shaping. Shaping needs a target.

William MacAskill opens Forethought's Better Futures series with the clearest version of the claim. Preventing catastrophe and achieving a good future are different jobs. Almost everyone works on the first. You do not have to accept his numbers. The split itself is the tool to take away.

One thing to keep in mind while you read. Frontier-lab leadership has published positive visions of its own. MacAskill writes from a research nonprofit about what anyone, anywhere, should aim at. Who ends up deciding what the good future contains is itself an open question. \## The argument, in brief

What follows is our summary of William MacAskill, "Introducing Better Futures", the opening essay of Forethought's Better Futures series (August 2025). The full essay: https://www.forethought.org/research/introducing-better-futures. Where words are quoted, they are his.

MacAskill splits "make the future go better" into two factors. Surviving: avoiding near-term catastrophe, extinction or permanent disempowerment. Flourishing: how good the future is, given that we survive. Most safety work, and every reading in this course so far, aims at the first factor. The series argues that work on the second is "in the same ballpark of priority".

The scale argument depends on how much room is left: "As long as we’re closer to the ceiling on *Survival* than we are on *Flourishing*", the second factor has more room to improve. His illustrative numbers: suppose our chance of surviving this century is above 80 percent, a view he calls widely held, while the future we get if we survive is under 10 percent as good as the best feasible future. Then fully solving survival buys 20 more percentage points of chance at a 10-percent-good future, about 2 percent of the best future's value. Fully solving flourishing buys an 80 percent chance at a fully good one, about 72 percent of that value: 36 times more. Push the estimates to 99 percent survival and 1 percent flourishing and the ratio approaches ten thousand. On his central numbers, merely nudging the value of the surviving future from 10 percent to 12.5 percent would be worth as much as eliminating extinction risk entirely. The numbers are illustrative; the structure is the point. He adds a subtler point. Worlds that needed saving are, once saved, more likely to be badly coordinated worlds. That quietly reduces the expected value of survival work, and not of flourishing work.

On neglectedness. Most people care far more about near-term catastrophe than about long-term flourishing, both from self-interest and from their moral views. So flourishing failures attract less work. They are also harder to see. Ask someone to describe utopia and you usually get today's world without its worst flaws. MacAskill argues that the gap between that common-sense utopia and the best feasible future may be far larger than the gap between today and that utopia.

On tractability he is candid. He calls it the strongest argument against his own view, and it is why he claims same ballpark rather than higher priority. The work he thinks might be tractable includes reducing the risk of a human concentration of power, giving advanced AI good reflective values rather than mere obedience, and improving how post-AGI decisions get made, from space governance to the rights of digital beings.

The rest of the series argues three claims. First, a flourishing future is not the default even if we avoid catastrophe, because it is a narrow target that future people are unlikely to aim at. Second, persistent positive impact on the long-run future is possible by routes other than preventing catastrophe. Third, there are concrete things to do about it today.

One refusal matters as much as the claims. MacAskill names no destination: "we should act on the assumption that we have almost no idea what the best feasible futures would look like". Committing now to one vision would repeat the mistake of past utopian projects. What he does endorse is a near-term target he calls viatopia, "a state of the world where society can guide itself towards near-best outcomes, whatever they may be": risk low, many moral views still present, many futures still open, big decisions made carefully.

Now compare your yes or no with this. The argument does not say prevention was the wrong bet. It says prevention alone leaves most of the value unclaimed, and that getting the rest is a job someone has to actually be doing.
