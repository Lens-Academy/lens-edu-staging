This lens is about how much brief positive psychology exercises (writing about good things, gratitude letters, acts of kindness and the like) change wellbeing. A few terms first.

- A **meta-analysis** pools the results of many studies of the same question into one estimate.
- An **effect size** says how large a difference is, independent of sample size. Two kinds appear below. A **correlation r** runs from 0 (no relation between doing the exercise and the outcome) to 1. A **standardized mean difference** (SMD, also written d or g) is the difference between the group averages divided by how much people vary. By our own calculation, r = .10 corresponds to an SMD of about 0.2, and an SMD of 0.45 means the average person who did the exercise ends up better off than about two thirds of the people who did not.
- **Small-sample bias.** Small studies with large effects get published more often than small studies with no effect, so averages over many small studies come out too high.
- An **inactive control** group does nothing or waits. An **active control** group does something comparable but supposedly inert, such as writing about early memories. Beating an inactive control is easier, because expectation and attention also count.
- A study is **preregistered** when the researchers publish their plan and analysis before collecting data, so they cannot pick the analysis that gives the best result afterwards.

\## Who runs the studies, and who takes part

- In Seligman's 2005 trial of five exercises, the 577 participants were visitors to the website of his book *Authentic Happiness* who clicked a link called "Happiness Exercises" ([Seligman, Steen, Park and Peterson 2005](https://wholebeinginstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/Positive-Psychology-Progress-Seligman-Steen-Park-Peterson-2005.pdf)). People who seek out a happiness book and its exercises may not respond like other people.
- Sheldon and Lyubomirsky report that in their eight-month study, only people who signed up for a study advertised as a "happiness intervention" became happier from the exercises, and those who put in more effort gained more ([Sheldon and Lyubomirsky 2021](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Sheldon-Lyubomirsky-2021.pdf)). They read this as showing that the exercises need buy-in. It also makes the effects hard to separate from expectation.
- In 2010 the US Army gave a 31 million dollar contract, without competitive bidding, to Seligman's center at the University of Pennsylvania to develop resilience training for soldiers ([Salon 2010](https://www.salon.com/2010/10/14/army_contract_seligman/)). The psychologists Roy Eidelson and Stephen Soldz argued that the Army's own evaluation of the program, which reported that it worked, was too flawed to show that ([Eidelson and Soldz 2012](https://www.ethicalpsychology.org/Eidelson-&-Soldz-CSF_Research_Fails_the_Test.pdf)).
- The field's leading figures also write popular books, such as *Authentic Happiness* above, and their centers offer trainings, such as the PERMA workshops you saw with Seligman's talk. In our view this does not make their findings wrong, but it is a reason to look at independent reanalyses like the ones above.

#### Question: Open
id:: 4028876f-75b6-477e-b33b-b7cf1c3199e8
content::
Without looking back, write down what you remember about the size of the effects in the three reviews.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read three pieces on the effects of positive psychology interventions and recalls them. You are a brief, honest mirror, diagnostic not instructional. Key points: (1) White, Uttl and Holder 2019 reanalysed Sin and Lyubomirsky 2009 (reported r = .29 for well-being, .31 for depression) and Bolier et al. 2013 (r = .17 subjective well-being, .10 psychological well-being, .11 depression); many studies were small, small-sample bias was pronounced, and after accounting for it the effect on well-being was small but significant, about r = .10, while effects on depression were variable, depended on outliers and were generally not significant. (2) Wilkie et al. 2026: a preregistered network meta-analysis of 183 trials (22,811 adults without diagnosed conditions); most interventions beat inactive controls; single positive psychology interventions, mindfulness, compassion, yoga and exercise had moderate effects (SMD 0.41 to 0.49); combined exercise and psychological programmes had the largest (0.73); nature-based were not significantly better; risk of bias was often moderate to high and publication bias was possible, but sensitivity analyses supported the results. (3) Folk and Dunn 2023: few studies met the bar of large and preregistered; gratitude and social connection had the best support; exercise, nature and meditation lacked rigorous studies of this kind. Acknowledge what is right, name the most important missing point in one sentence, correct errors in one sentence, normalise gaps. 60 to 110 words. No generic praise. One turn, then tell them to move on.

Given this evidence, how large a change would you expect from doing one of these exercises for a week?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner states what change they would expect from a week of one positive psychology exercise, given the evidence in this lens: about r = .10 (roughly SMD 0.2) on well-being after correcting for small-sample bias (White et al. 2019), SMD around 0.4 to 0.5 against inactive controls (Wilkie et al. 2026), and only a few large preregistered studies, which favour gratitude and social connection (Folk and Dunn 2023). Note also that averages hide variation between people, and that a self-test cannot rule out expectation unless it uses a comparison period. Accept any expectation the learner can justify from the evidence. If they expect a large or lasting change, ask what in the evidence supports that. If they expect nothing, ask what the corrected small effect would mean for them. Do not tell them whether to try an exercise. 60 to 100 words. At most two replies. No generic praise.
