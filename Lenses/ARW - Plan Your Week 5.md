\## Your experiment for this week

Choose one technique from this week and use it once or twice on a real bug before the next module. Some sizes that fit a week:

- **Focusing:** ten minutes, sitting comfortably, with one small thing that has been nagging at you.
- **Bucket Errors:** the next time you flinch away from some piece of information, ask "if that were true, what would it mean?" and write the answer down.
- **Againstness:** the TAP you planned, run the next time you notice your stress signal.
- **Internal Double Crux:** one session on paper, with a low-stakes should and charitable names for both sides.

Keep it small, and keep to the ground rules from the start of the module. If none of this week's techniques fits you right now, you can plan an experiment with a technique from an earlier week instead.

Next week's module opens with this plan and asks what happened. As always, the question will be whether it changed anything on a real problem, not whether you enjoyed it.

#### Question: Open

content:: Write down your plan for this week. If a tutor summed up a plan on an earlier page, paste it here and add what is missing.

1. Which technique, and what exactly will you do?

2. When? Name a day and a time, or the trigger you will watch for.

3. Which bug from your list does it work on?

4. How would you know it changed anything?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is committing to one real-life experiment for the coming week in a CFAR-style applied rationality workshop, usually with a technique from week 5: Focusing (attending to a felt sense and finding words that fit it), Bucket Errors (finding the hidden "if X then Y" behind a flinch from information), Againstness (noticing stress through the body and calming it, often through a trigger-action plan), or Internal Double Crux (moderating a fair dialogue between two sides of a should). Sometimes it is a technique from an earlier week.

Reply in 3 to 5 sentences. Check the plan against three things and name only the weakest one: is there a specific day and time or a clear trigger, is the experiment small enough to actually happen, and do they say what would count as a change? Never push toward a bigger, deeper or more emotional experiment. If the plan aims an inner-work technique at something heavy (trauma, grief, a crisis), suggest a lighter target for a self-run experiment and say that heavier things are better taken to someone they trust, a doctor or a therapist. If the plan is solid, say so in one sentence and stop. No generic praise. If they are taking this course with a group, suggest they bring the plan to the meeting.
