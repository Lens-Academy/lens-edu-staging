tldr: Following the rules of Science, or any set of rules, does not stop you from being a fool. No known procedure makes your reasoning safe, so you have to take responsibility for it yourself.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky says that people he has seen grow as rationalists first lost their emotional trust in the sanity of the people around them, which lets them judge strange ideas on their merits. He then describes his own misplaced trust. At 18 he trusted Science and thought that making a Novel Prediction and offering to test his idea meant he had done everything required and was safe. Science would have been fine with him wasting ten years on a stupid theory, so long as he admitted the error afterwards. Science demands too little, blesses good intentions too easily and accepts slowness. There is no known procedure, set of rules or morality of reasoning that guarantees you will not have been a fool, not even Bayescraft, which is harder to use and has no textbooks. Science is too vague to scare you, while probability theory you cannot fully apply makes clear the task is hard. The postscript leaves a known flaw in the essay on purpose. The question gives a student who thinks a novel, testable prediction makes his plan to spend ten years on a theory blameless, and asks what is wrong with that and whether a stricter set of rules would fix it.

#### Question: Open
id:: 38b6ff3c-423e-4c1b-a4e1-e809995865ab
content::
A PhD student says: "My theory makes a novel prediction that an experiment could prove wrong, and I will test it. So I have done everything good reasoning requires. If it takes me ten years and the theory fails, I still reasoned responsibly."

Answer in your own words.

1. According to the essay, what is wrong with the student's view?

2. Would following a stricter or better set of rules fix the problem? Explain using the essay.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The essay's idea: following the rules of Science, such as making a novel prediction and offering to test it, does not make your reasoning safe. Science would accept you wasting ten years on a bad theory as long as you admit the error at the end. It demands too little and blesses good intentions too easily. Beyond that, there is no known procedure or set of rules, including Bayesian reasoning, that you can follow and know you have not been a fool. Bayesian reasoning is harder to apply and you can never be sure you are doing it right. You stay responsible for whether your ideas are actually right.

Score out of 100.

50: Part 1. Meeting Science's rules (a testable prediction, willingness to test) does not mean the student reasoned well or is safe. Following the rules can still leave you a fool. A good reason, for example: Science is fine with ten wasted years as long as he admits the error, so it does not protect him from picking a bad theory in the first place. Any reason of this kind earns full points. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer says only that the theory might be wrong, without saying that obeying the rules does not make the reasoning good or protect him.

50: Part 2. No. According to the essay there is no known procedure or set of rules you can follow and know you have reasoned well. A good reason, for example: even a better method such as Bayesian reasoning is hard to apply and gives no certainty you are doing it right, so the student must take responsibility for judging whether the idea is good. Any reason of this kind earns full points. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer says "no" but gives no reason, or says a better method would help but not that no method makes you safe.

Cap at 40 if the answer says that a better set of rules (for example Bayesian reasoning) would make the student's reasoning safe or beyond criticism.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. He thinks that because he followed Science's rules, he can't be faulted. But Science would happily let him spend ten years on a stupid theory, as long as he admits it was wrong at the end. Following the rules doesn't make the theory any good or protect him from wasting his life on it. 2. No. The essay says there is no known set of rules that guarantees you haven't been a fool. Even Bayesian reasoning is harder to use and you never know if you're doing it right. He has to judge for himself whether the idea is likely to be right."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Yudkowsky's "No Safe Defense, Not Even Science" and judged a PhD student who thinks that making a testable novel prediction makes ten years on his theory blameless. Key ideas from the essay: young Eliezer thought a Novel Prediction and a promise to test made him safe, and Science would have been fine with him wasting ten years. Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent recall, well done). Below a full score, name the single most important gap, usually missing that no set of rules at all, not even a better one, makes reasoning safe. At full score, confirm briefly and mention that the PhD student is in the same position as Yudkowsky at 18. Grade only against what the essay supplied.

If the student says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold: point to Yudkowsky at 18, who made a novel prediction and still went badly wrong, and ask what that shows. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on where their answer stands.
