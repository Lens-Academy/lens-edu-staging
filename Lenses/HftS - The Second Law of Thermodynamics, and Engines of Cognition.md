title: "The Second Law of Thermodynamics, and Engines of Cognition"
tldr: "Knowing something means your brain is in a state that matches the world, and you only get there by interacting with the world. No clever reasoning in a sealed room can give you accurate beliefs about what is outside it."
summary_for_tutor: "Yudkowsky explains that the Second Law of Thermodynamics follows from Liouville's Theorem: phase space volume is conserved, so no machine can squeeze one subsystem into fewer states without another spreading into more. He shows this with small state tables (a refrigerator as X and Y subsystems), then argues the Second Law is Bayesian: entropy measures our uncertainty, and if you knew the exact state of every molecule in hot water you could use Maxwell's Demon to turn it into electricity and ice. Knowledge is physical: it is mutual information between a mind and its target. One subsystem cannot gain mutual information with another without interacting with it and doing thermodynamic work, so to form accurate beliefs about something you really do have to observe it. If blind faith worked, you could build a demon with blind faith in molecule velocities. He ends: unless you can say which specific step of an argument gives true knowledge of the unseen, an elaborate argument cannot do it either, just as an assembly of wheels cannot make energy unless one wheel does. The question gives a man who claims to learn, in a sealed room, which card a friend in another city drew."

#### Question: Open
id:: 5eb5796c-73f0-4146-b9f5-4283c6d93b75
content::
A man says he can sit in a sealed room, with no contact of any kind with the outside, and come to know which card a friend in another city has just drawn. His method has many careful mental steps, and he says that together they get him there.

1. Using the essay's argument, why should you doubt that he can know the card?

2. He points out how many careful steps his method has. In your own words, why does the essay say that doesn't help, and what would you ask him to point to?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Background for the grader: the essay argues that knowledge is physical. An accurate belief about something means the mind's state is correlated with that thing (mutual information), and one system cannot gain such a correlation with another without interacting with it, through some chain of cause and effect, and doing physical work. If minds could gain accurate beliefs without that, you could build a Maxwell's Demon and break the Second Law of Thermodynamics. It ends: just as a big assembly of wheels cannot create energy unless one wheel breaks the laws of physics, an elaborate argument cannot give true knowledge of the unseen unless one specific step does. Score out of 100. 60: says that to form an accurate belief about the card, his mind must come to match or track the card, which requires interacting with it through some chain of cause and effect (observing it, directly or through others), and the sealed room cuts every such link, so his beliefs about the card cannot become accurate. 25: says adding steps cannot help, because if no single step gets information about the card without contact, the whole chain cannot either (like a machine of wheels that cannot make energy unless one wheel does). 15: says you would ask him to name the specific step that gains knowledge of the card without any contact. Cap at 30 if the answer accepts that enough careful reasoning could reveal the card. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Knowing the card means his brain has to end up in a state that matches the card, and the only way to get that is through some chain of cause and effect from the card to him. A sealed room cuts every link. More steps don't help: if no single step brings in information about the card, a long chain of them can't either, just as a pile of gears can't make energy unless one gear does. I'd ask him which step gets information about the card."
