#### Text
content::
Psychologists use **emotion regulation** for the ways "individuals influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience and express these emotions" ([Gross 1998](https://emotion.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/1353/2021/11/Gross-1998-The-Emerging-Field-of-Emotion-Regulation-An-Integrative-Review.pdf)). Much of their research compares three things people do once a feeling is there:

- **Reappraisal**: reframing the situation to change its emotional impact ([Troy et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6188704/)). It is "a primary process of traditional cognitive-behavior therapy" ([Kashdan et al. 2006](https://mason.gmu.edu/~tkashdan/publications/exp_avoid_vulnerablity_BRAT.pdf)).
- **Acceptance**: engaging with your feelings and accepting them without judging them or trying to change them ([Troy et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6188704/)). Researchers treat it as one of the two skills that mindfulness training teaches, next to watching your experience ([Lindsay et al. 2019](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5b352f70b105987a3c71d3d1/t/5c6b402ee4966b1492b279ac/1550532654929/Lindsay+et+al+acceptance+loneliness+PNAS+2019.pdf)).
- **Suppression**: hiding the feeling from others, or trying not to feel it or think about its cause. Experiments test all three kinds separately ([Webb, Miles and Sheeran 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22582737/)).

Keep the moment you described on the last page in mind while you read. Which of the three, if any, did you do?

The first reading is the only study we found that compares reappraisal and acceptance head to head in the same people. Read the abstract. Its first sentence, not shown, says both strategies are associated with better psychological health over time.

#### Text
content::
The third option, suppression, has the clearest result. In Gross and John's studies of how people habitually handle feelings, people who suppress "experience and express lesser positive emotion, yet experience greater negative emotion", and suppression went with worse relationships and lower wellbeing ([Gross and John 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.2.348)). These were questionnaire studies, so they show what goes together, not what causes what.

Experiments point the same way. A meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons found that hiding the expression of a feeling did change outcomes (an effect size of d = 0.32), but trying to suppress the feeling itself did not (d = -0.04), nor did suppressing thoughts about what caused it (d = -0.12). Reappraising the situation had d = 0.36 and taking another person's perspective d = 0.45 ([Webb, Miles and Sheeran 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22582737/)). An effect size **d** says how far apart two groups are in standard deviations: 0.2 is usually called small, 0.5 medium.

A randomised trial took a mindfulness app apart. Of 153 adults, one group learned to watch their experience and accept it, one learned only to watch, and one got a control training. Only watching plus accepting reduced daily loneliness, by 22%, and added about two social interactions a day ([Lindsay et al. 2019](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5b352f70b105987a3c71d3d1/t/5c6b402ee4966b1492b279ac/1550532654929/Lindsay+et+al+acceptance+loneliness+PNAS+2019.pdf), free PDF, the abstract takes 3 minutes). feedback-instructions:: The learner just read Troy et al. 2018 (abstract, two instructions, conclusion), the abstract of Ford et al. 2018, and our summary of Gross and John 2003 and Webb, Miles and Sheeran 2012, and wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- Emotion regulation: changing which feelings you have, when, and how you experience or show them. Reappraisal changes what the situation means. Acceptance lets the feeling be without judging or changing it. Suppression hides or pushes it down.
- Troy et al. 2018 (two samples, N = 142, sad film clips, each person tried both strategies): reappraisal lowered negative and raised positive emotion more. Acceptance felt less difficult and in one sample dampened skin conductance (a body stress signal) more. Both "effective" for different reasons, which may depend on the person and the situation.
- Ford et al. 2018: people who habitually accept their feelings and thoughts had better psychological health. Acceptance predicted less negative (not more positive) emotion during stressors, and that explained better health 6 months later. Accepting mental experiences mattered, accepting situations did not.
- Suppression: suppressors feel less positive and more negative emotion and have worse relationships (Gross and John, correlational). Hiding the expression changes outcomes in experiments, suppressing the feeling itself does not (Webb et al.).
