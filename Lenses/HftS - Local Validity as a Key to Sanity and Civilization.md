title: "Local Validity as a Key to Sanity and Civilization"
tldr: "A step of reasoning is valid or not regardless of whether you like where it leads. Judging your own side's arguments by the same rules as the other side's keeps your thinking sound and keeps shared rules trusted."
summary_for_tutor: "Yudkowsky links three ideas. In mathematics a proof step is locally valid if it only turns true statements into true statements, whatever the final conclusion (x = y gives 2x = 2y, but xy = xz does not give y = z). Outside mathematics there are bad arguments for good conclusions, and a strong mind judges an argument separately from whether it agrees with the conclusion: someone who believes in global warming should still wince at a single scorching day offered as evidence, because they would roll their eyes at a single cold day offered against it. In law and society, rules let people move from bad equilibria to better ones only if they are applied evenhandedly, even against allies, as with the juror who said 'I can be fair to one side but not the other', the debate about Al Franken, the Chinese general who sentenced his own son, and the judge Pendarvis in Little Fuzzy. When people stop believing rules are applied fairly, cooperation falls apart. The question gives a friend who supports a bike lane and cites a single crash, next to an opponent who cited a single quiet month, and asks how the kind of thinker the essay admires would judge the friend's argument and why the essay thinks this matters."

#### Question: Open
id:: 1f88ed2c-af88-459c-a245-561f5edd70ca
content::
You support a new bike lane on Main Street. A friend who also supports it says: "The bike lane will make the street safer. Just look at the terrible crash there last week." A month ago an opponent argued: "Main Street is safe enough. Nobody was hurt there all month."

1. How would the kind of thinker the essay admires judge your friend's argument, even though they agree with its conclusion?

2. In your own words, why does the essay think it matters to judge arguments this way?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Background for the grader: the essay says a step of reasoning is valid or invalid regardless of whether its conclusion is true, and there are bad arguments for good conclusions. The thinker it admires judges an argument separately from whether they agree with the conclusion: someone who would roll their eyes at a single cold day offered against global warming should reject a single hot day offered for it. It says this matters because sane answers require judging arguments independently of conclusions, and because shared rules (in law and in argument) only hold people together when they are applied evenhandedly, even against allies. When people believe rules are enforced only against one side, they stop cooperating. Score out of 100. 50: says they would call it a bad argument despite agreeing with the conclusion, because a single crash says little about whether the street is dangerous or whether a lane would help, and they would reject the same kind of argument from the other side (the single quiet month). Either reason earns the full 50 if the answer is clear that the argument is judged on its own merits, not by its conclusion. 50: gives either reason the essay offers: (a) you can only reach correct answers if you judge arguments independently of their conclusions, since bad arguments are as easy to find for true conclusions as for false ones, or (b) rules applied evenhandedly, including against your own side, are what let people who disagree trust the rules and keep cooperating, and if rules are seen as applied to one side only, that trust and cooperation break down. Cap at 30 if the answer says the friend's argument is fine because the conclusion is right. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "They'd say it's a bad argument even though they want the bike lane. One crash says as little about safety as one quiet month, and they'd never accept the opponent's version. It matters because you only get to true answers if you judge each argument on its own merits, and because shared rules only work when people trust that they are applied to both sides."
