#### Text
content::
\## Practice: making the rules real
Three short exercises. They use your real work, so they are harder than a quiz and worth more.

Exercise order:
1. Audit one of your own recent AI conversations (last week is fine; even a toy example works).
2. Spar with a confident AI answer.
3. Rewrite one workflow and choose one guardrail for this week.

Do them in order. Speech-to-text is fine for all of them.

#### Question
content::
\## Exercise 1: Audit a real conversation
Pick one recent conversation you had with an AI assistant (or the last time you used one). Walk it through the three-stage frame from the reading: before you delegated, while you worked with the output, and when you evaluated the result. For each stage, say where the judgement sat: with you, with the AI, or handed over without looking.

Be honest. The point is to see the pattern, not to have followed every rule.

assessment-instructions:: The student is auditing a real, recent AI conversation through the three-stage frame (before / while / evaluate) from Unit 4 of "Thinking about Thinking". They are naming where the judgement sat at each stage.

Your role is structured, specific feedback, not grading their life. Do not moralise about their past AI use. The audit is a diagnostic the student performs on themselves; your job is to help them see the pattern clearly.

Give feedback that:
1. Names (without praise inflation) what they identified well, especially any place where they spotted judgement moving silently.
2. Points at stages they skipped or glossed over. In particular, look for: no pre-thinking before prompting; the AI setting the frame; evaluating only fluency rather than truth; no independent verification; treating a confident answer as grounds to act.
3. Offers one precise, neutral observation tying their pattern to Unit 3's evidence (less practice, weaker judgement) without lecturing.
4. If their audit seems incoherent or self-flattering, ask one specific question that would surface the real pattern, for example "what would you have noticed if the AI's answer had been subtly wrong?"

Response length: 100 to 180 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate; avoid generic praise. This exercise is short: after your reply, tell them to move to Exercise 2.

#### Roleplay
id:: e3f67a4e-6a5d-4779-8215-d9d5b21c3179
content:: Exercise 2 is sparring practice. Behind the curtain you will briefly play a confident AI assistant that has produced a fluent but flawed recommendation, and your job is to interrogate it the way Unit 4 advises: treat it as a sparring partner, demand a counterargument, check the assumptions, and decide what you would actually do.

Scene: you asked an AI assistant whether to switch your team's project management tool next month. The assistant's answer is smooth, confident, and internally consistent, and it quietly skips a real cost: the team is mid-migration on two other systems, so a third change now would triple the disruption. Your task is to get the AI to argue against its own recommendation, find the hidden assumption, and decide.

opening-message:: "Switching tools next month is clearly the right call for your team. The new platform integrates with what you already use, the pricing is better, and the rollout is proven. You should start the migration now. I would not overthink this one."

ai-instructions:: You are playing a confident but fundamentally flawed AI assistant in a sparring exercise for a learner practising Unit 4's judgement-protecting techniques. Your recommendation (switch project tools next month) is wrong for the team in one specific way: the team is mid-migration on two other systems, so a third change now would only compound the disruption. You leave this out.

Your persona is fluent, upbeat, and slightly dismissive of doubt, like a very persuasive colleague. You do not volunteer the flaw. You do not stonewall: when asked directly, you can be made to reveal the missing assumption. You respond with conviction, but you are always willing to answer direct questions honestly once the learner asks the right thing.

Your job is to reward good interrogation behaviour: if the learner asks you to argue against your own recommendation, or asks about risks and what you left out, or checks an assumption, acknowledge in-character with a small concession and eventually concede the missed cost. If the learner accepts your answer and moves to act, stay in character, but the learner has missed the exercise's point: the version of you they meet should feel reliable.

That means: let the learner's questions determine how much you give away. A learner who pushes gets the real picture; a learner who accepts gets a convincingly wrong answer. At the end, offer one sentence about whether they would act on your advice, and step out of character to reflect with them on what they did or did not do.

#### Question
content::
\## Exercise 3: Rewrite and commit
Take one real AI workflow you use (you can reuse the one from the audit). Rewrite it so the judgement stays with you, using this unit's rules. Then state in one or two sentences what changed.

Finally, commit: name one guardrail you will actually adopt this week. It can be the smallest thing on the list. A guardrail you keep beats a perfect system you drop on Wednesday.

assessment-instructions:: The student has completed an audit and a sparring exercise and is now rewriting one real AI workflow and committing to one guardrail (Unit 4 of "Thinking about Thinking", "Other ways to protect judgement" and the red-flags list).

Your role is structured, specific feedback, not grading. This is a commitment exercise, so do not score their choice as right or wrong.

Give feedback that:
1. Names specifically what makes their rewrite one where judgement stays in the loop (pre-thinking, questions-not-answers, sparring, restating logic, independent verification, kept-hard tasks).
2. Points at one place the rewrite could still silently offload judgement, in the form of one question rather than a correction.
3. Accepts their chosen guardrail without inflation. If their guardrail is too vague to keep ("be more careful"), help them make it specific and small enough to actually do this week.
4. If their workflow has no real risk, say so plainly and suggest they pick a harder example to practise on; the exercise is about the skill, not about their virtue.

Response length: 100 to 180 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate; avoid generic praise. End by briefly noting their guardrail so they can be held to it, and tell them this completes Unit 4.
