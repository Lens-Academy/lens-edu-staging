Why does the good feeling of getting something fade? Randolph Nesse, a psychiatrist known as one of the founders of evolutionary medicine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randolph_M._Nesse)), gives an answer from evolution. Read three short parts of his 2004 paper. Open it in a new tab: [Nesse 2004, "Natural selection and the elusiveness of happiness"](http://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Phil.%20Trans.%20R.%20Soc.%20Lond.%20B-2004--1333-47.pdf) (Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, free copy). About 10 minutes in all.

**Part 1**, the main idea. In section 2(b), "Six reasons why the body is not better designed", read the first paragraph, from
> Most people like to imagine that normal life is happy

to
> They are aspects of mechanisms that influence us to act in the interests of our genes.

**Part 2**, feelings and goals. Earlier in the paper Nesse writes that positive emotions help "when progress towards a goal is faster than expected". In section 4(a), "Affect and goal pursuit", read from
> Our brains could have been wired so that good food

to the end of the section:
> are the seeds of intense dissatisfaction that often precede serious depression.

**Part 3**, the kind of goals modern life rewards. Read the first paragraph of section 7, "Modern goals", from
> The mechanisms that regulate behaviour were shaped for

to
> creating the exact situations that disengage motivation and cause depression.

#### Text
content::
Psychologists who study **hedonic adaptation**, the fading of the good or bad feeling that follows a change in life, describe a second route by which the good feeling of a gain fades. Kennon Sheldon and Sonja Lyubomirsky write that the gains from a positive change are eroded by "declining positive emotions" and by "increased aspirations for even more positivity" ([Sheldon and Lyubomirsky 2012](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/The_Challenge_of_Staying_Happier.pdf)). In their three-month study of 481 students, continued appreciation of the change and variety in it slowed the fading. So getting what you want can raise what you want next.

Not everyone sees this as a problem. The philosopher Thomas Hobbes, writing in 1651, made endless wanting the definition of happiness, which he calls "felicity". His spelling is that of 1651:

to:: the attaining of the former, being still but the way to the later.

#### Question: Open

content::
\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from this page, without looking back. No need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read three passages from Nesse 2004, a paragraph on Sheldon and Lyubomirsky 2012 and a short passage from Hobbes.

Key points:
- Nesse: we were not designed for happiness or for unhappiness. Happiness and unhappiness are not ends but means, aspects of mechanisms that regulate behaviour in ways that tended to increase reproductive success.
- Bad feelings are reliably aroused by losses, threats and failing to reach important goals. The old advice to adjust goals to what is possible is, in Nesse's words, wise advice with a growing scientific foundation, but most people keep pursuing goals, some feasible, some grand. Many goals are zero-sum competitions or depend on other people.
- Modern goals: big rewards go to people who put most of their effort into one domain or goal. Such goals are long, large and all-or-none with few alternatives, which the regulation systems were not designed for, so people persist in hopeless goals, which can lead to depression.
- Sheldon and Lyubomirsky: gains fade through declining positive emotions and rising aspirations. Appreciation and variety slow this.
- Hobbes: felicity is a continual progress of desire from one object to the next, there is no final rest.

Your role is diagnostic, not instructional: a brief, honest mirror. 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs, no lists. Acknowledge what is correct without inflation, name what is missing without lecturing, correct errors in one sentence, normalise gaps. Close with one calibrating sentence. Do not ask a question or invite a reply. One turn. Tell them to move on. No generic praise.

\## Learning question
An ambitious friend says: "Once my company is a success, I will finally be content for good." What would Nesse's account predict about how they will feel once they reach the goal, and why?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Learning question after Nesse 2004, Sheldon and Lyubomirsky 2012 and Hobbes.

What a good answer contains:
- On Nesse's account, feelings are means that regulate goal pursuit, not a lasting reward for reaching a goal. Good feelings track progress that is faster than expected, so once the goal is reached and progress stops, the boost is not designed to last.
- Rising aspirations (Sheldon and Lyubomirsky) add a second route: success raises what the friend will want next.
- Section 7: a company is the kind of long, large, all-or-none goal that strains the regulation system on the way, and if it fails there may be few satisfying alternatives.
- What the account does not predict: that the friend will be miserable or that success brings nothing. It predicts the contentment will not be permanent.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer precisely in 2 to 4 sentences, name 1 to 3 gaps, and ask 2 answerable follow-ups about why or how. If they reach the answer early, ask how a Hobbesian would describe the same friend, or what evidence could test Nesse's prediction. If stuck after 2 attempts, give the direct answer and move on. After 3 replies, ask whether they want to continue. On close, say what they showed clearly and what is still vague. 120 to 200 words. No generic praise. Do not tell the learner whether to pursue big goals.
