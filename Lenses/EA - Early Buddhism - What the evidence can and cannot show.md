#### Text
content::
Early Buddhism makes claims of different kinds, and research reaches some much better than others. We sort them into three:
1. Practices from the path, such as mindfulness of breathing and loving-kindness, reduce stress.
2. Craving causes stress, and seeing craving clearly weakens it.
3. Craving can end completely, which the tradition calls awakening.

Keep Bhikkhu Bodhi's objection from the previous lens in mind: a practice lifted out of the path may not do what it does inside it. Almost all of the trials below test lifted practices.

#### Callout: 1. Trials of practices taken from the path
tone:: neutral
#### Text
content::
The most careful review of meditation programmes so far counted only randomised trials with **active controls**: comparison groups that also did something, such as exercise or health education, so that attention and expectations were similar in both groups. **ES** in the abstract is an **effect size**, which says how far the average participant moved compared with the controls, in units of the spread of scores. By a common convention 0.2 counts as small, 0.5 as medium and 0.8 as large ([Cohen 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19565683/)).

#### Text
content::
"Moderate evidence" in the abstract describes how confident the reviewers were, not how large the effect was. By Cohen's convention the effects are small.

For loving-kindness, one of the four sublime states, Julieta Galante and colleagues reviewed 22 randomised trials of **kindness-based meditation** in 2014 ([Galante et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24979314/)). Compared with **passive controls** (groups that got no training), it reduced self-reported depression by a medium amount (g = 0.61). Compared with progressive relaxation it increased positive emotions (g = 0.42). Against active controls the results were mostly inconclusive. The authors rate the quality of the studies as low to moderate, note that the studies were small, and write that the practice "may initially be challenging for some people".
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 2. Does seeing craving clearly weaken it?
tone:: neutral
#### Text
content::
In the Arrow discourse (SN 36.6), the person who suffers less is the one who sees a feeling's "origination, passing away, allure, drawback, and escape" ([Thanissaro's translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn36/sn36.006.than.html)). The psychiatrist Judson Brewer, then at Yale, tested something close to this on smokers ([Brewer et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3191261/)). He randomly assigned 88 adults who smoked about 20 cigarettes a day to eight group sessions of mindfulness training over four weeks, or to the same number of sessions of the American Lung Association's Freedom From Smoking programme. The mindfulness group learned to "Recognize, Accept, Investigate and Note what cravings feel like as they arise" (RAIN) instead of fighting them. At 17 weeks, 31% of the mindfulness group and 6% of the comparison group had not smoked for a week, checked with a breath test.

A larger trial by the same team tested an app version, Craving to Quit, with 325 smokers who started the programme ([Garrison et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29917096/)). At six months the app group did not quit more often than a control group that only logged their cravings (9.8% against 12.1%). In the app group, though, cravings were less closely tied to how much people smoked.

A third kind of evidence is correlation. Bhavna Sahdra and colleagues built a questionnaire for **nonattachment**, which they define as "release from mental fixations", and report "evidence consistent with Buddhist theory that nonattachment is psychologically and socially adaptive" ([Sahdra, Shaver and Brown 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20155561/)). A correlation cannot show which comes first.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 3. Can craving end completely?
tone:: neutral
#### Text
content::
This is the tradition's central claim, and we found no study that tests it. The tradition's own check is practice and the judgement of teachers. Research can only study people who report advanced attainments, and so far it has studied very few.

One example: in 2026 Remko van Lutterveld, Matthew Sacchet and colleagues recorded the brain activity of a single meditator during **cessations**, events they compare to *nirodha* in Theravada and define as "a brief loss of consciousness that usually last a few seconds" ([van Lutterveld et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13452153/)). The meditator was a co-author, the teacher Daniel Ingram, with about 23,000 hours of practice. Across 29 recordings he reported 46 clear cessations, and the authors analysed 37 of them. Brain activity changed in measurable ways around each event. The study shows that a reported event has a physical trace in one person. It says nothing about whether craving ended or whether wellbeing lasted. Ingram chairs Emergence Benefactors, which has funded Sacchet's research programme, as the paper states.

The path also has costs. Jared Lindahl, Willoughby Britton and colleagues interviewed 60 Western Buddhist meditators, 20 each from Theravada, Zen and Tibetan lineages, who had had difficult experiences in practice ([Lindahl et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5443484/)). Nearly half had practised more than 10,000 hours. Because people were recruited for having had difficulties, the study cannot say how common these are, only what they were like and when they came.

#### Callout: Who gains
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translations and books, which make up most of this module, are given away. His website says that everything on it "is offered freely", with "no price tags, no advertising, no suggested donations, no memberships" ([dhammatalks.org](https://www.dhammatalks.org/)). Brewer later founded a company, MindSciences, that made the Craving to Quit, Eat Right Now and Unwinding Anxiety apps, and the health company Sharecare bought it ([Princeton Alumni Weekly, 2021](https://paw.princeton.edu/article/jud-brewer-96-helping-people-unwind-their-anxiety)). Lindahl and colleagues describe mindfulness as a major part of a "billion-dollar meditation industry" ([Lindahl et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5443484/)).
#### End Callout

#### Text
content::
We think the evidence supports a modest claim. Mindfulness programmes taken from Buddhism reduce anxiety, depression and pain by a small amount on average, and the trials found no sign that they do more than other active treatments such as exercise. Loving-kindness reduces depression compared with no training, but it is unclear whether it does more than other activities. The evidence that seeing craving clearly loosens it is promising but mixed. The claim that craving can end completely has not been tested, and we do not see how a trial could test it soon. The experiments in the next lens let you test smaller versions of these claims on yourself.

#### Question: Open
id:: 98df5fe1-76c1-4365-a33d-3404eab67026
content::
Goyal's review found no evidence that meditation programmes do better than exercise or other active treatments. Does that count against the Buddhist claim that the path leads to the end of stress?
feedback-instructions:: The learner read an evidence lens on early Buddhism: Goyal et al. 2014 (47 trials with active controls, small effects of mindfulness programmes on anxiety, depression and pain, no evidence of beating drugs, exercise or other behavioural therapies), Galante et al. 2014 on kindness-based meditation, Brewer's smoking trials, a single-case study of cessation events, and Lindahl's study of difficulties in Buddhist practice. Earlier in the module, Bhikkhu Bodhi argued that practices lifted out of a tradition lose their place in an integral whole.

There is no single right answer. Points a strong answer may make: the trials test eight-week programmes, not the whole path of virtue, concentration and discernment, so they test a much weaker claim (Bodhi's objection). The path claims to end stress at its root, while the trials measure symptom scores, so even a large effect would not confirm the claim, and a null result does not refute it. On the other hand, if the practices at the heart of the path do no more than exercise in eight weeks, that is some evidence about what the practices do, and a defender who says only the whole path counts makes the claim harder to test. Equal effects also do not mean the same effect: meditation might work through a different route.

Restate their position more precisely in one or two sentences. If they say it clearly refutes or clearly does not touch the claim, name the strongest point on the other side. Ask one follow-up, for example: "What result would count against the claim, if not this one?" 80 to 130 words. At most two replies. Do not say whether Buddhism is right. No generic praise. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: ask how long the trials lasted and what they measured, compared with what the path promises.

#### Question: Open
id:: 17a49200-528d-4620-a5d4-221821436237
content::
What evidence could show whether craving can end completely in a person?
feedback-instructions:: The learner read that the tradition's central claim, that craving can end completely, has not been tested, and saw a single-case EEG study of cessation events in one advanced meditator who was also a co-author. They now propose what evidence could test the claim.

There is no single right answer. Useful ideas include: long-term follow-up of people who claim advanced attainments, measuring craving in daily life (for example experience sampling of urges and of how strongly urges drive behaviour, as in Brewer's app trial), behavioural tests such as reactions to loss, pain or temptation, reports from people who live with them, and comparison with matched people who do not claim attainments. Problems a strong answer notices: who decides who counts as awakened, people who claim attainments may misjudge themselves or have reasons to overstate, one case cannot generalise, and the tradition says the end of craving is known first-hand, which outside measures may miss.

Name the strongest part of their proposal in one sentence and the most serious problem it faces in one sentence, then ask one follow-up, for example: "How would you tell someone who has no craving from someone who has learned not to act on it?" 80 to 130 words. At most two replies. No generic praise.
