\## Before you read

The previous reading gave Plan A one {--{"author":"Lauren's AI","timestamp":1786513833514}@@paragraph:--}{++{"author":"Lauren's AI","timestamp":1786513833514}@@paragraph. It described++} a strong international agreement that mostly ends the race and {--{"author":"Lauren's AI","timestamp":1786513833514}@@buys--}{++{"author":"Lauren's AI","timestamp":1786513833514}@@gives the world++} years of safety work. A paragraph is not a plan. This lens is the {--{"author":"Lauren's AI","timestamp":1786513833514}@@plan, at treaty resolution.--}{++{"author":"Lauren's AI","timestamp":1786513833514}@@plan itself, in the detail of an actual treaty.++}

Aaron Scher and colleagues on MIRI's Technical Governance Team have drafted the actual agreement. You will read the section that says what it does: which training runs get banned, which chips get tracked and where they are allowed to sit, who verifies whom, and why its governing council starts with exactly two members.

The agreement has a family. Evals and if-then commitments say: keep going, but pre-commit to act when a measured capability crosses a line. Pause campaigns say: stop first and negotiate the details after. This treaty is the maximal member, and reading one member of the family closely teaches you how to read the rest.

Before you start, decide where it breaks. Every agreement has to be signed, then verified, then enforced, and nearly everyone who dismisses this plan points at one of the three. Pick yours first, so you can watch how much of the text is aimed at exactly that step.

If you want the authors' own answers to the obvious objections, their section 5 is a FAQ, collapsed below the excerpt. The full draft treaty, article by article, is Appendix A of the same paper, also collapsed below; the team's other work lives at https://techgov.intelligence.org.

*The framing and questions on this page were written by Claude, an AI, and reviewed by a human. The reading itself is the authors' own work.*

#### Question
content::
\## Your turn first

Write two or three sentences making the strongest case that an international agreement is the best-case plan. Not a fair case. The strongest one.

Then write one more line, and commit: which is the likeliest point of failure for this agreement, signing it, verifying it, or enforcing it? Name the one you would bet on.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is an optional lens of Unit 4 in a course about AI futures, written for newcomers. Unit 4 dissects rival strategies for preventing AI catastrophe. The student has already read Ryan Greenblatt's "Plans A, B, C, and D", where Plan A is a strong international agreement; they have NOT yet read this lens's article. It is section 4 of "An International Agreement to Prevent the Premature Creation of Artificial Superintelligence" by Aaron Scher and colleagues (MIRI Technical Governance Team), and it is in your context. Note the student reads section 4 only, the description of what the agreement does, so keep your references to what that section covers.

The student was asked to make the strongest case for the agreement, then to commit to its likeliest failure point: signing, verifying, or enforcing.

The purpose is that they enter a real treaty text carrying a specific prediction about where it breaks. The purpose is not for them to reproduce the paper. Do not deliver its answer.

WHAT THE PAPER PROPOSES. For your reference only. Do not deliver it.
- The U.S. and PRC lead a coalition and form the initial Executive Council; the coalition expands over time but stays deliberately small at the decision-making core.
- Training runs above a Strict Threshold of 10^24 FLOP are prohibited; runs above a Monitored Threshold of 10^22 FLOP need approval and monitoring. Several current near-frontier models sit only slightly above the Strict Threshold.
- Verification runs on chips: locating existing AI chips, tracking newly produced ones through the concentrated supply chain, and monitoring use, with concentrations above roughly 16 H100-equivalents prohibited outside monitored facilities.
- Research that pushes toward ASI or undermines verification is restricted, with explicit carve-outs for narrow, application-specific AI.
- Overt violations are met with nonproliferation measures and escalating enforcement, from sanctions upward.
- The paper concedes there is not yet political will for this, and proposes a staged path of confidence-building measures toward it.
All three failure points the student could pick are real: the authors themselves flag missing political will (signing), aim most of the agreement's machinery at covert cheating (verifying), and lean on historical arms-control parallels for the hardest case (enforcing). No pick is wrong.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Restate their failure point in their own terms, and treat it as a forecast, not an opinion.
2. Ask one question: what evidence, visible in the world before the failure happens, would show that failure point approaching? Push for something observable, a signed refusal, a smuggling case, a vetoed inspection, not a mood.
3. If their strongest case and their failure point contradict each other, for instance a case built on verification and a failure point of verifying, point at the tension in one sentence and leave it with them.

Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise. Never say whether the treaty is realistic; the student is about to read what its drafters think it takes.

Write 60 to 110 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
