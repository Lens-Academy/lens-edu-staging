tldr: "How much evidence you need depends on how many possibilities there are and how sure you want to be. A test that is rarely fooled can still pick out many wrong answers when there are millions of alternatives."
summary_for_tutor: "Yudkowsky works through a lottery with 131,115,985 possible combinations and black boxes that always beep for the winner and beep for a losing combination 1 time in 4 (a likelihood ratio of 4 to 1). Each independent box multiplies the odds. Evidence can be measured in bits. Evidence 131 million times likelier for the winner only gives even odds, because about one losing ticket also passes. Ten boxes (about a million to one) still let through about 131 losers per winner. The general rule: the larger the space of possibilities, the less likely the hypothesis beforehand, or the more confident you want to be, the more evidence you need. The question gives a stadium face scanner checking 1,000,000 fans for one wanted person, with a false alarm rate of 1 in 10,000, and an operator who concludes a flagged person is 99.99% likely to be the suspect. The student estimates the number flagged, the real chance, and names what the operator left out."

#### Question: Open
id:: a9867ec2-69e3-474c-a217-12d4569f1ff8
content::
A camera system at a stadium checks the faces of 1,000,000 fans for one wanted person, who is in the crowd. It always flags the wanted person. It also wrongly flags 1 in every 10,000 other people. The operator sees an alert and says: "The system is wrong only once in 10,000 times, so this person is 99.99% likely to be our suspect."

1. Roughly how many people will the system flag in total?

2. Roughly how likely is it that a flagged person is the suspect?

3. What did the operator leave out of his reasoning?

assessment-instructions:: Background for the grader: about 999,999 innocent fans times 1 in 10,000 gives about 100 false alarms, plus the one real suspect, so about 101 people are flagged and a flagged person has about a 1 in 101 (about 1%) chance of being the suspect. The essay's rule: the larger the space of possibilities, or the less likely a hypothesis is beforehand, the more evidence you need. A filter that lets through 1 in 10,000 wrong cases is not strong enough when there are a million candidates. Score out of 100. 25: about 100 or 101 people flagged (accept anything from 100 to 101, or "about a hundred"). 25: about 1% or about 1 in 100 (accept 1 in 100 or 1 in 101, or 0.99% to 1%). If an answer gets one of these two numbers wrong only because the other was wrong (for example a consistent but miscounted total), give 15 of 25 for the dependent one. 50: says the operator ignored how many people were scanned (or, equivalently, how unlikely any one fan is to be the suspect beforehand, or that a million candidates produce far more false alarms than the one true match). Cap at 20 if the answer agrees that a flagged person is about 99.99% likely to be the suspect.
