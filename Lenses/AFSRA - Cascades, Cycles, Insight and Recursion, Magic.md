title: "Cascades, Cycles, Insight… and ...Recursion, Magic"
tldr: A pile of uranium with k = 0.9994 fizzles, and one with k = 1.0006 runs away. A self-improving AI can cross a similar threshold, so a small change in how much each improvement leads to can mean the difference between levelling off and an explosion.
summary_for_tutor: >-
  Two linked 2008 posts by Eliezer Yudkowsky from his debate with Robin Hanson, listing five sources of discontinuity in the growth of minds. Cascades: one improvement leads to another (in hominids, farming led to writing and specialization), so steady optimization pressure can still produce what looks like a jump. Cycles: output fed back into input, as in Fermi's first nuclear pile, where the neutron multiplication factor k decides everything (k below 1 levels off, above 1 runs away, and the first pile had k = 1.0006). Insight: a piece of abstract knowledge, like calculus, that lowers the cost of a whole range of problems. Recursion: an optimizing compiler run on itself only gets faster at doing the same thing, so its gains top out (k < 1). EURISKO could modify its own heuristics but ran out of steam because it lacked insight. Engelbart's tools did not take over the world because the mouse did not improve itself (k < 1). Changes closer to the root of the causal tree (the cognitive and metacognitive levels) have bigger effects. Magic: a smarter mind may find jumps you cannot imagine, as nanotech would have surprised the Victorians. The question asks the student to apply the k threshold and the role of insight to a lab's self-improving system.

#### Question: Open

content::
An AI lab has a system that proposes improvements to its own design. The engineers measure that, on average, each improvement the system makes to itself leads it to find 0.8 further improvements of the same size. A new version raises this number from 0.8 to 1.1. A manager says: "1.1 is about 40% more than 0.8, so in the long run we should expect about 40% more total progress from the new version."

1. The number the engineers measure works like the k of Fermi's pile, the average number of further fissions that each fission causes. Using this idea from the reading, what is wrong with the manager's prediction?
2. The lab can make one of two upgrades to the old version. Upgrade A runs it on hardware twice as fast, using the same methods to look for improvements. Upgrade B gives it a new general method that makes a whole class of design problems easy for it. Which upgrade is more likely to raise the number of further improvements each improvement leads to, and why?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The reading's ideas used here: in a cycle, the multiplication factor k decides the behaviour. Below 1 the process levels off at a limited total (the self-applied optimizing compiler tops out at a 25% gain), and above 1 it keeps growing and runs away, like Fermi's pile, where k = 0.9994 versus k = 1.0006 made all the difference. So a small change in k across 1 changes the outcome in kind, not in proportion. Speed-ups that make a system do the same thing faster, like an optimizing compiler run on itself, do not improve its ability to improve itself and only add a limited gain. An insight is knowledge that lowers the cost of solving a whole range of problems, and Yudkowsky says EURISKO ran out of steam because it lacked insight.
50: Step 1. Says that 0.8 is below 1, so the old version's improvements die out and its total progress levels off at a limited amount, while 1.1 is above 1, so each round leads to more improvements and progress keeps growing (runs away). The difference is a change in kind across a threshold, not a 40% difference in total progress. Give 25 if the answer only says that crossing 1 matters, without saying what happens on each side.
50: Step 2. Chooses Upgrade B and explains why. 25: says that the general method makes many further design problems cheap or easy, so each improvement brings more further improvements within reach. 25: says that faster hardware only runs the same search faster, without making the system better at finding improvements, so the number of further improvements per improvement stays about the same. An answer that chooses A gets 0 for this step.
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The manager treats it as a smooth scale, but 1 is a threshold. With 0.8, each improvement leads to fewer new ones, so the chain dies out and total progress stops at a limited amount, like a subcritical pile. With 1.1, each improvement leads to more than one new one, so progress keeps growing without levelling off. That's not 40% more, it's a different kind of outcome. Upgrade B is the one to worry about. A general method is like an insight: it makes many future improvements cheap, so each improvement can lead to more. Faster hardware is like the optimizing compiler, which does the same thing faster but doesn't get better at improving itself, so it adds a fixed boost and tops out."
