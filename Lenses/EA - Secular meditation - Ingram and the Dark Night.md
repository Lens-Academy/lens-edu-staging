#### Text
content::
Daniel Ingram is a physician who worked in emergency medicine. His book *Mastering the Core Teachings of the Buddha* (MCTB) came out in 2008, and the second edition (2018) is free online. With Kenneth Folk he is associated with the "pragmatic dharma" movement. He says he is an **arahat**, the last of the four stages of awakening in Theravada Buddhism, which few Buddhist teachers say about themselves ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Ingram_(author))).

The readings below show his style: precise instructions, a map of stages that tells a meditator where they are, and open talk about attainments. His main technique is **noting**: naming each experience with a short mental label as it happens. Here are his instructions.

#### Text
content::
A central claim of the book is that insight practice goes through a fixed series of stages, the **progress of insight**. The fourth stage is the **Arising and Passing Away (A&P)**, a peak of clarity and rapture. Stages five to ten he calls the **Dark Night**, a stretch where that clarity drops away and practice can become fearful, miserable or restless. This is how he describes the end of the A&P and the start of the Dark Night.

#### Text
content::
Ingram's map has critics. Bhikkhu Anālayo is a German-born Theravada monk and a scholar of early Buddhism. He is a retired professor of the University of Hamburg and a resident scholar at the Barre Center for Buddhist Studies ([University of Hamburg](https://www.buddhismuskunde.uni-hamburg.de/en/personen/analayo.html)). In 2020 he published a case study of Ingram's book in the journal *Mindfulness*. Here is his summary.

#### Text
content::
Put plainly: a meditator who knows the map and notes hard and fast may produce the very experiences the map predicts, and may then take them as proof of having reached a stage of awakening. Anālayo also rejects the idea that people in ordinary mindfulness courses, who do not practise deep and intensive insight meditation, can suffer from going through these stages. Ingram answered the critique in a podcast interview ([Guru Viking, episode 73](https://www.guruviking.com/podcast/ep73-daniel-ingram-dangerous-and-delusional)).

#### Question: Open
id:: d2f8f7a5-e850-4c4e-ba53-e4e87b018b08
content::
Many people who practise Ingram's way report going through the stages in the order his map predicts. According to Anālayo, why is this weaker evidence for the map than it sounds?
assessment-instructions:: The question is about Daniel Ingram's map of stages of insight meditation (a fixed order of stages, including a peak of clarity and a hard stretch called the Dark Night). Many practitioners report passing through the stages in the predicted order. The critic, Bhikkhu Anālayo (2020), argues that a forceful style of mindfulness with fast mental noting can construct experiences that match the expectations created by such maps, and so warns against overvaluing meditators' own reports.

Score out of 100.
100: The answer says that the reports may be shaped by the map itself: meditators who know what to expect, and who practise in this forceful, fast way, can produce or interpret experiences so that they fit the map, so agreement with the map is what you would see even if the map were not accurate. Any plain wording counts (expectation, self-fulfilling prophecy, the map creates the experiences).
50 instead of 100: the answer only says that self-reports are unreliable, subjective or biased in general, without saying how knowing the map or the practice style could make experiences fit the map.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Because people who know the map expect those stages, and forceful fast noting can produce experiences that fit what they expect. So matching reports could come from the map itself rather than from stages that exist anyway."

At 100, confirm briefly and add one way to test it that the learner could think about, for example comparing reports from meditators who had never read any map.
