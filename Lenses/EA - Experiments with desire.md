#### Text
content::
Each school on the previous page makes claims you can test on yourself in a week. Below are six experiments. Each one gives the instruction in its source's own words, what to record, and what the evidence for it is. Some have little or no controlled evidence. Module 3's rule applies: a practice that is cheap and safe can be worth trying on thin evidence, as long as you know that is what you are doing.

Pick two that test different claims, for example one about noticing wanting and one about which wants to keep. Run each for about a week and write a few lines every day. Module 8 asks what you found.

#### Callout: 1. Noting desire (early Buddhism)
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**What it tests:** the Buddhist claim that craving arises and passes, and that seeing it clearly, without acting on it or suppressing it, weakens it.

**The instruction.** The Satipatthana Sutta (MN 10), an early Buddhist text on mindfulness, describes how a practitioner watches the "hindrance" of sensual desire:

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/dhammatalks-mn-10-the-establishing-of-mindfulness-discourse-satipa-h-na-sutta]]
from:: There is the case where, there being sensual desire present within
to:: no further appearance in the future of sensual desire that has been abandoned.

#### Text
content::
**What to do and record.** Several times a day, and whenever you notice an urge, note "wanting present" or "wanting not present", as the text describes. When it is present, watch what happens to it without acting on it for a minute. In the evening, write down how many urges you noted and whether they passed without being acted on.

**Evidence.** A review of 30 lab experiments on mindfulness and craving found short-term effects that may be explained by distraction, and "tentative support" from a few longer studies ([Tapper 2018](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/18719/)).
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 2. Get curious about a craving (Brewer)
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**What it tests:** whether looking closely at what a habit actually gives you weakens the wanting, which is one way to see the gap between wanting and liking in yourself.

**The instruction.** Judson Brewer, a psychiatrist who studies mindfulness and addiction, describes it in a TED talk (about 6 minutes of it):

#### Video
source:: [[../video_transcripts/ted-a-simple-way-to-break-a-bad-habit-judson-brewer-ted]]
from:: 3:09

#### Text
content::
**What to do and record.** Pick one habit you would like to want less, such as checking your phone, snacking or email. Each time the urge comes, either do it while being "really curious" about what it is like, or notice the urge and the body sensations that make it up, as Brewer says. Each evening, rate how strong the urge felt on a scale from 0 to 10 and how much you actually enjoyed the habit when you did it.

**Evidence.** In Brewer's trial, 88 smokers received either mindfulness training or a standard programme. At 17 weeks, 31% of the mindfulness group and 6% of the other group had not smoked for the past week, checked with a breath test ([Brewer et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21723049/)). This is one small trial. In the talk Brewer calls it "twice as good as gold standard therapy". He founded a company that sells app versions of the method, later acquired by Sharecare ([HIT Consultant 2020](https://hitconsultant.net/2020/06/03/sharecare-acquires-evidence-based-behavior-change-apps-mindsciences/)).
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 3. An Epicurean audit
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**What it tests:** Epicurus's claim that many desires are unnecessary and that dropping them costs little.

**The instruction.** You read both texts on the Epicurus page. Principal Doctrine 29 sorts desires into natural and necessary, natural but not necessary, and those due to "illusory opinion". Vatican Saying 71 says: "Evaluate each of your desires by this question: 'What will happen to me if that which this desire seeks is attained, and what if it is not?'" ([epicurism.info](https://www.epicurism.info/etexts/VS.html))

**What to do and record.** On the first day, list ten things you currently want. Sort each into one of the three classes and ask the question of each. During the week, drop or postpone one desire you classed as unnecessary, and write down each evening whether you missed it and how much.

**Evidence.** We found no study of this audit. It is a cheap exercise in self-observation, nothing more is claimed.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 4. Four reasons for each goal (self-determination theory)
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**What it tests:** how much of what you pursue you pursue for controlled reasons (pressure, guilt) and how much for autonomous ones (valuing, enjoyment), and whether goals with autonomous reasons feel and go differently.

**The instruction.** Researchers in self-determination theory measure this with four ratings per goal. In one study, people listed personal goals and rated each from 1 ("not at all for this reason") to 9 ("completely because of this reason") on four reasons ([Koestner et al. 2002](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2002_KoestnerLekesPowersChicoine_JPSP.pdf), p. 234):

- **external**: "because somebody else wants you to, or because you'll get something from somebody if you do"
- **introjected**: "because you would feel ashamed, guilty, or anxious if you didn't"
- **identified**: "because you really believe that it is an important goal to have"
- **intrinsic**: "because of the fun and enjoyment which the goal will provide you"

The **self-concordance** of a goal is the sum of the intrinsic and identified ratings minus the sum of the external and introjected ratings (same paper).

**What to do and record.** List your three to five main goals for the next weeks. Rate each on the four reasons and compute its self-concordance. At the end of the week, write down how much progress you made on each goal and how the work on it felt.

**Evidence.** In a meta-analysis of seven studies in the same paper, goals with higher self-concordance went with more progress ([Koestner et al. 2002](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2002_KoestnerLekesPowersChicoine_JPSP.pdf)). The research comes from the theory's own community. Rating your own reasons is easy to bias.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 5. Voluntary discomfort (Seneca)
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**What it tests:** the Stoic and Epicurean claim that peace of mind does not depend on comfort, and that fear of losing comfort is larger than the loss.

**The instruction.** The Stoic Seneca wrote to his friend Lucilius:

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/seneca-moral-letters-to-luciliusletter-18]]
from:: Set aside a certain number of days, during which you shall be content with the scantiest and cheapest fare
to:: whether this amount was worth purchasing at the price of great effort.

#### Text
content::
**What to do and record.** For three days, eat plain, cheap food and give up one comfort you are used to, as Seneca describes. Each evening, write down how you felt during the day and answer his question: "Is this the condition that I feared?"

**Safety.** Keep it to plain food and fewer comforts. Do not fast, and skip this experiment if you have or had an eating disorder.

**Evidence.** We found no controlled study of this practice. The closest is the next experiment.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 6. Give It Up (hedonic adaptation)
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**What it tests:** whether a short break from a pleasure makes it more enjoyable again, which tests the adaptation side of wanting.

**The instruction.** This is the practice as the Greater Good Science Center at UC Berkeley describes it, with its evidence and caveats:

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/berkeley-give-it-up]]
from:: Daily for one week. See if you can give up a different pleasure for one week each month.
to:: tend to experience physical and mental health disturbances during periods of abstinence.

#### Text
content::
**What to record.** Rate how much you enjoy the pleasure on the first day, and again when you have it after the week's break, from 0 to 10.
#### End Callout
