#### Text
content::
In early Buddhism, the word for the kind of wanting that causes suffering is **craving** (Pali *tanha*). The first reading is from the Buddha's first sermon as preserved in the Pali Canon. The translator, Thanissaro Bhikkhu, renders *dukkha* as "stress", where others write "suffering" ([Access to Insight glossary](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/glossary.html)). The passage states the problem, its origin and its end.

#### Text
content::
Read on its own, this sounds as if all wanting is the problem. The next reading says it is not. Ajahn Jayasaro, a monk ordained in 1980 by the Thai teacher Ajahn Chah who has lived as a monk in Thailand ever since (see the note at the end of the article), distinguishes craving from **wholesome desire** (Pali *chanda*). The text was adapted from a talk he gave in 2012. The excerpt is about half of the article. Read it with your own work in mind: he talks about work as well as meditation.

#### Text
content::
If wanting is part of the path, can the path end wanting? A brahman (a member of the brahmin caste) puts this objection to the monk Ananda in a short sutta. An **arahant** is a person whose mind is free of defilement, the highest level of the Buddha's disciples. "Mental effluents" are sensuality, views, becoming and ignorance, which the tradition says keep the round of rebirth going ([Access to Insight glossary](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/glossary.html)).

#### Question: Open

content::
\## Learning question
Someone says: "Buddhism teaches that all desire causes suffering. So a Buddhist who wants to reach awakening is contradicting their own teaching." Where does this argument go wrong, according to the readings?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Learning question after SN 56.11, Jayasaro's "Skilful Desires" and SN 51.15.

Key points the learner should reach:
- SN 56.11 names craving (tanha) for sensual pleasure, for becoming and for non-becoming as the origin of stress, not desire of every kind.
- Jayasaro: the Buddha spoke of two kinds of desire. Chanda, wise and wholesome desire, is "absolutely fundamental" to progress on the path. A practical test: tanha is focused on the result, chanda on the action itself.
- SN 51.15: desire is abandoned by means of desire. Like the desire to go to a park, which subsides on arrival, the desire for awakening subsides when it is reached. So wanting awakening is not a contradiction: it is a desire that ends itself.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer precisely in 2 to 4 sentences, name 1 to 3 gaps, and ask 2 answerable follow-ups about why or how. If they reach the answer early, ask whether wanting awakening could itself become craving (for example, meditating only to get peace, as Jayasaro describes) and how one would tell. If stuck after 2 attempts, give the direct answer and move on. After 3 replies, ask whether they want to continue. On close, say what they showed clearly and what is still vague, and whether they are ready for the test after the comparison page. 120 to 200 words. No generic praise. Do not say whether Buddhism is right.
