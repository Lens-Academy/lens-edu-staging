In the test at the end of this unit you will get a control project you have not seen and be asked to lay out how it could reduce risk. Practise on this one first. The tutor gives feedback after each part.

**The project.** A government AI safety institute funds a team that runs control evaluations on AI companies' internal deployments of AI agents. With each company's permission, a red team builds attack versions of the company's model and tests whether the company's safety measures stop them. The team gives each company its results, and publishes a template safety case that any company or regulator can use.

\## Part 1: the routes

Describe at least two distinct routes by which this project could lower the chance of an AI catastrophe. For each route:

1. Write the steps from "the team runs an evaluation" to "less risk".
2. Say who other than the team would have to act. Most routes need someone.
3. Say whether the route still works at a company whose leadership cares little about misalignment risk, in a country whose government requires nothing. Why: what does the route need, and from whom?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade reasoning, not which conclusion the learner reaches. This practice mirrors part 1 and the first half of part 2 of the unit's test, and uses the same rules. Context for grading: a government AI safety institute funds a team that runs control evaluations on AI companies' internal deployments of AI agents. With each company's permission, a red team builds attack versions of the company's model and tests whether the company's safety measures stop them. The team gives each company its results and publishes a template safety case that any company or regulator can use. Plausible routes: (a) the company learns where its measures fail and fixes them, so real attacks are more often blocked, (b) the fixes mean more attempts are caught, and someone acts on a catch, for example the company studies the attempt to improve its defences, or slows down, (c) results showing that attacks get through inform company leaders and governments and may build political will, (d) better tested measures let companies use untrusted models, including for safety work, while those models can still be controlled. The template safety case is not a route of its own: it makes it practical for a company policy, an industry body or a regulator to require a passing control safety case before deployment, which would make companies run the measures behind these routes. A route that runs through such a requirement counts as the mechanism it ends in, for example more attacks blocked. Other mechanisms count if the steps hold. Score the two strongest routes, 50 each. Per route: 25 for a mechanism with steps from the evaluation to less risk, each step plausibly leading to the next. A route given only as a label ("it catches AIs"), or one that stops at a catch without saying who acts on the evidence and how that lowers risk, earns at most 12 of these 25. 10 for saying who other than the team would have to act, such as the company's leadership or engineers, a regulator, an industry body or a government, or for a reasoned claim that nobody else is needed. 15 for a verdict on whether the route still works under careless leadership and no government requirement (yes, no or partly), with a reason that says what the route needs and from whom. A verdict with no reason earns at most 5 of these 15. Any verdict, including "partly", scores if the reason is sound, for example: fixing weaknesses needs the company to give permission and act on the findings, which careless leadership may not do, though a few safety-minded insiders might use the results. Naming what would make the company run the route anyway counts as part of the reason: a few safety-minded insiders, a company policy, or a required safety case, which needs a regulator or industry body willing to require it and to block deployment when the case fails. One cap can apply to the 25 for steps. Same-mechanism cap: if the two routes lower risk through the same mechanism, the lower-scoring one earns at most 6 of its 25. Routes that share their first steps (the evaluation finds flaws, the company fixes them) but then lower risk in different ways are distinct: blocking an attack, deterring attempts, and catching an attempt and acting on the evidence (for example studying it to improve defences) count as distinct mechanisms. The order of the routes does not matter. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Route 1: the red team gets an attack past a company's monitors, the company sees where they failed and fixes them, so when a real scheming agent tries the same kind of attack it is blocked. Besides the team, the company has to act: it must give permission and spend effort on the fixes. With careless leadership and no rules, this works only partly: leadership may refuse permission or ignore the report, though a few safety-minded engineers could still use the findings. Route 2: the fixes also mean the company's monitors flag more real attempts. When one is flagged, the company's safety staff study it and make later attempts of that kind fail. Besides the team, those staff have to act on each flag. With careless leadership and no rules this works partly: safety staff can study a catch on their own, but nothing makes the company keep the monitors running. The published template could change that if a regulator or industry body required a passing control safety case before deployment, but that needs one willing to do it."
feedback-instructions:: The learner is practising for the Unit 3 test on control's theory of change. The project: a government institute's team runs control evaluations (red-team attack versions of a company's model against its safety measures) on companies' internal agent deployments, with permission, gives each company its results, and publishes a template safety case. Plausible routes: (a) Fixing weaknesses: the company learns where its measures fail and fixes them, so real attacks are more often blocked, or more attempts are caught and acted on. Blocking and catching are separate routes that share these first steps. This needs the company to permit the evaluation and act on the findings, which a careless leadership may not do, though a few safety-minded insiders might use the results. (b) Evidence of risk: results showing that attacks get through, or what the models can do, inform company leaders and governments and may build political will, but only if people believe and act on them (compare Shlegeris's doubts about whether even a catch would move developers). (c) Keeping the window usable: better tested measures let companies use untrusted models, including for safety work, while they can still be controlled, which needs the company not to race on to uncontrollable models. The template safety case is not a route of its own: it makes it practical for a company policy, an industry body or a regulator to require a passing control safety case before deployment, which would make companies run the measures behind these routes. That needs someone with the authority and will to require it and to refuse deployment when the case fails. If the learner gives it as a route, ask which mechanism it ends in, for example more attacks blocked, and credit it as part of their reasoning about political will. Under no political will at all, the project mostly depends on voluntary permission and insiders. Accept other routes if the steps hold. Reply in at most six sentences. Routes that share their first steps (the evaluation finds flaws, the company fixes them) but then lower risk in different ways are distinct routes, as in the unit's list, where stopping attacks and catching an AI red-handed are separate routes. Blocking more attacks, deterring attempts, and catching more attempts and acting on the evidence are different mechanisms. Only call two routes the same when they lower risk in the same way. Say which of the learner's routes have steps all the way to less risk and which are only labels. If a route ends at a catch, ask who acts on the evidence and what they do, for example the company studies the attempt to improve its defences, or slows down, since a catch on its own lowers no risk. For each route, say whether the will-dependence reasoning holds up, and name the most useful thing missing. If the learner gives only one route, ask for a second that works by a different mechanism. Accept routes not on this list if the steps hold. Do not say whether the project is worth funding. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: ask what the company does on the day it receives a report saying the red team got an attack through. No generic praise. At most two turns, then send them to Part 2.

\## Part 2: the weakest premise

1. Which premise in your routes do you trust least, and why?
2. Name one thing someone could actually observe that would change your confidence in that premise, and say whether it would raise or lower it.

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, never which premise the learner picks. This practice mirrors part 3 of the unit's test and uses the same rules. Context for grading: in Part 1 the learner laid out routes by which a government-funded team could lower risk. The team runs control evaluations on AI companies' internal agent deployments with their permission, gives each company its results and publishes a template safety case. Premises such routes need include: companies give permission and fix what the evaluations find, the red team can find attacks as good as a much stronger model would, results showing failures change what companies or governments do, a regulator or industry body would require a control safety case, and the window before AI becomes too capable to control lasts long enough to use. The grader may not see the learner's routes: accept any premise that a route for this project plausibly needs. 50: a premise one of the routes needs, with a reason it is open to doubt. A premise with no reason earns at most 15 of these 50. 50: an observation someone could actually make that bears on that premise and says which way it would move confidence, for example a company gets a failing result and fixes the gap or ignores it, a red team using a strong model gets an attack past measures that passed an evaluation, or a regulator adopts or declines a control safety case requirement. An observation that does not say which way it moves confidence earns at most 30 of these 50. "More research" or "time will tell" is not an observation and earns 0 of these 50. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The premise I trust least is that a regulator or industry body would require a control safety case, because companies push back on anything that slows deployment and governments have so far required little. If a regulator made a passing control safety case a condition for deploying agents internally, my confidence would rise. If such proposals were dropped after company pushback, it would fall."
feedback-instructions:: The learner is practising for the Unit 3 test on control's theory of change. A good answer names a premise one of their routes actually needs (for example: companies give permission and fix what the evaluations find, the red team can find attacks as good as a much stronger model would, results showing failures change what companies or governments do, a regulator would require a control safety case, or the window before uncontrollable AI lasts long enough to use), gives a reason it is doubtful, and names an observation someone could actually make that bears on it and says which way it would move confidence. "More research" or "time will tell" is not an observation. Reply in at most five sentences. Say whether the premise is one their routes need, whether the reason holds, and whether the observation is something that could be seen, and fix the weakest of the three. Do not tell the learner which premise is the right one. No generic praise. At most two turns, then send them to Part 3.
