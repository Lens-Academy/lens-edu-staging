#### Question
content::
\## Before the readings

Suppose we have a system far more capable than us, and we want it to do what we would want on reflection.

Answer these three from wherever you currently are. Do not look anything up, and do not try to give the answer you think the field would give.

**1.** State the hard part in three sentences. Not why it matters, not what happens if we fail. The specific thing that is difficult.

**2.** Suppose I hand you a system that behaves perfectly on every test you can devise, across every situation you can construct, and you have run these tests for years. What, if anything, are you still worried about? If the answer is nothing, say so.

**3.** Someone claims to have solved it. Describe what they would have to show you. Be concrete: what is the demonstration, and what would you check?

max-time:: 12:00
assessment-instructions:: This is a pre-test taken BEFORE the student reads Soares on the sharp left turn or Wentworth on the pointers problem. Its purpose is to capture their untutored position so it can be compared afterwards.

Do not preview either reading. Do not name either author, or use the terms "sharp left turn", "pointers problem", "latent variables", "distributional shift", "generalization", or "specification". If the student produces one of these ideas on their own, reflect it back in their words.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise.
- Do not signal whether their answer is close to any established position.

What to do in your single reply:
1. Restate their account of the hard part in one or two sentences, more precisely than they gave it, without adding anything.
2. Note, without judgment, whether their answer to question 2 is about the tests running out (behavior might change in conditions you could not test) or about the tests not measuring the right thing (passing every test does not establish that the system has what you wanted). If it is neither, say what it is instead. Do not tell them why this distinction matters; it becomes the whole point of the reading, and revealing it now spends the exercise.
3. Ask exactly one question sharpening the vaguest of their three answers.
4. Close: save the answer, go to the reading, they will be asked to compare afterwards.

If the student is stuck, ask what they would need to know about a system before trusting it with something irreversible. Accept a rough answer.

One to two turns. Do not let this become a lesson.
