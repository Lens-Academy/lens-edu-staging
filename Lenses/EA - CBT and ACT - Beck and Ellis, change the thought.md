#### Text
content::
Aaron Beck trained as a psychoanalyst. In a 2004 interview he describes the moment he started to doubt the method. A patient spent her sessions talking about her sexual experiences and said she felt anxious. Beck took this as anxiety about forbidden impulses, until he asked what else she had been thinking. Her answer: "I was afraid that I was boring you." She had had this fear all along, in many situations, and never mentioned it. Beck then noticed that other patients also did not report what they thought. "It then occurred to me that there are two types of communication: internal and external. Internal refers to the automatic thoughts people have about themselves which they do not ordinarily share." To get at these thoughts, he stopped using the couch and sat patients up "so that we could talk back and forth" ([Beck Institute interview](https://beckinstitute.org/blog/interview-with-aaron-beck-on-the-history-of-cognitive-therapy/)).

The idea that grew from this is the **cognitive model**: "individuals' interpretations of situations influence their reaction (emotional, behavioral, physiological), more so than the situation itself" ([Beck and Fleming 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9667129/)). If a thought drives the feeling, and the thought is distorted, then checking the thought should change the feeling.

#### Text
content::
The everyday tool for this check is the **thought record**. Here is the version used in the free self-help modules of the Centre for Clinical Interventions, a public mental health service in Western Australia ([CCI module 6](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/-/media/CCI/Consumer-Modules/Back-from-The-Bluez/Back-from-the-Bluez---06---Detective-Work-and-Disputation.pdf), [CCI module 7](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/CCI/Consumer-Modules/Back-from-The-Bluez/Back-from-the-Bluez---07---The-End-Result.pdf)). You write down:

1. **A**, the activating event: what happened just before you felt bad.
2. **B**, the beliefs: what went through your head. You mark the most distressing one, the **hot thought**, and rate how much you believe it from 0 to 100.
3. **C**, the consequences: what you felt, rated from 0 to 100, and what you did.
4. Any **unhelpful thinking styles** you recognise in the hot thought, such as jumping to conclusions, catastrophising, labelling, or "shoulding and musting".
5. **D**, detective work and disputation: the factual evidence for the hot thought and the evidence against it, and answers to questions such as "What other explanations could there possibly be?", "How might someone else view the situation?" and "Is it helpful for me to think this way?"
6. **E**, the end result: a **balanced thought**, which CCI defines as one "that takes into consideration all the evidence, objective information, and alternative viewpoints". Then you re-rate the feeling and how much you still believe the hot thought.

CCI's own example: a man whose partner comes home and says hi without the usual kiss thinks "She probably doesn't care about me anymore" and believes it at 90. Against it he lists that she has said she will support him and made him a special dinner the night before. Other explanations: she had a hard day, or wanted to put the groceries away.

Albert Ellis developed rational emotive behaviour therapy (REBT). CCI's list of thinking styles includes "shoulding and musting", and that is where Ellis put most of the weight. In the essay below he argues that much distress comes from rating your whole self and from absolute demands. Notice how many of the beliefs he lists contain "must" or "absolutely need".

#### Question: Open
id:: 7e27564a-a03e-423b-a412-23352ef8407a
content::
A friend says: "Cognitive therapy is just positive thinking. You swap 'I'm a failure' for 'I'm a great person' and repeat it until you believe it."

What is wrong with this description? Use what this page says about Beck's method and about Ellis's.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100.

50 points: in Beck's method the thought is checked against the evidence (for and against, and other explanations), and the new thought is a balanced one that fits all the evidence. It is meant to be more accurate, not more positive, and it can keep negative facts that the evidence supports. Repeating a positive statement without checking it is not the method.

50 points: Ellis would reject "I'm a great person" as well as "I'm a failure", because both are global ratings of the whole self. He recommends rating only one's acts and traits and not rating the self at all (or, as a second best, accepting oneself just for being alive).

An answer that makes both points in one sentence earns both. Cap at 40 if the answer agrees that the aim is to believe positive things about oneself.
feedback-instructions:: The learner was given the claim that cognitive therapy is just positive thinking, swapping "I'm a failure" for "I'm a great person", and asked what is wrong with it, using Beck's method and Ellis's. They got a score.

In 90 to 140 words: if they missed Beck's point, say that the thought record tests the thought against evidence for and against and ends in a balanced thought that "takes into consideration all the evidence" (CCI), which can still contain unwelcome facts. If they missed Ellis's point, say that he rejects any global rating of the self, positive or negative, and rates only acts and traits. If they asked a direct question, answer it directly. If they are stuck after two attempts, give a brief direct answer and move on. At most 3 replies, then offer to continue or move on. No generic praise.
