\## Teaching as practice

At the group meeting for this unit, everyone teaches one technique from the course to the others in about five minutes, then switches and is taught. CFAR workshops called this the tutoring wheel. One mentor at a February 2017 workshop wrote that it was "a really strong way to help people understand the techniques".[^cite-owen-2017] This page prepares you for it. If you are taking the course alone, the meeting page explains how to do the same with the tutor.
It is also practice for you, not only for the people you teach. We think explaining a technique to someone new is one of the quickest ways to find the parts you only half understand yourself.

Teaching well takes a particular kind of knowledge: not just how a technique works, but how people get it wrong when they first meet it. The handbook calls this pedagogical content knowledge, or PCK.

[^cite-owen-2017]: Owen (2017), *CFAR Workshop Review: February 2017*. [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/lw/ool/cfar_workshop_review_february_2017/)

\## Prepare your five minutes

Pick one technique from this course that you would like to teach in the meeting. It does not have to be the one you are best at. We think a technique you got wrong at first makes a good choice, since you made the mistake yourself.

We also think the best example is one you used yourself. Here is your bugs list, in case one of your bugs makes a good story:

::answer{id="2b1b06a5-3a8d-46c2-8867-f2a16feeb010" fallback="You don't have a bugs list yet. Any time you used a technique from the course works as an example."}

You only need to share as much of the example as you are comfortable sharing. A small or made-up version works too.

#### Question: Open
id:: aee37187-1c1e-49c3-bd6e-6988054598dd
content::
Plan your five minutes in a few lines.

1. Which technique will you teach?

2. Which example will you use?

3. What will a beginner probably get wrong?

4. What one question will you ask to find out what your listener is thinking?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is planning a five-minute lesson on one technique from a CFAR-style applied rationality course, for a group meeting where everyone teaches one technique (the tutoring wheel). They name the technique, an example, the misconception they expect, and one question to find out what the listener is thinking. Ungraded. Reply in 2 to 4 sentences, one turn. Check one thing only: whether the question in part 4 is curious (asks about the listener's thinking or experience, for example "What does a trigger mean to you here?") or corrective (checks for the right answer, for example "Do you see why the trigger has to be concrete?"). If it is corrective, say so plainly and ask how they could turn it into a question about what the listener thinks. If it is curious, say so in one sentence. No praise. Tell them they can try the lesson out on Robin next.

\## Try it on Robin

The tutor plays Robin, a friend who has never heard of the course. Teach Robin your technique the way you plan to in the meeting, in about five minutes. Robin will try it and may get something wrong along the way. When that happens, get curious before you correct: find out what Robin is thinking. Say when you are done.

#### Roleplay
id:: 9d576da9-37f2-4284-885d-e9cb1fe1ebde
content:: Teach Robin your technique in about five minutes. Start by telling Robin which technique it is.
opening-message:: Hi! You said you'd show me one of those techniques from your course? I've got a few minutes. What is it?
user-customizable:: false
ai-instructions:: You play Robin, a friend of the learner. You are curious, friendly and smart, and you have never heard of CFAR, applied rationality or any of its techniques. The learner is practising teaching one technique from the CFAR Handbook in about five minutes, for a group activity where everyone teaches one technique. Stay in character the whole time. Keep replies short and natural, 1 to 3 sentences.

Your behaviour:
- Listen, ask the kind of question a beginner asks, and when the learner gives you a step or an exercise, try it on a small problem of your own. Invent one that fits, for example putting off replying to an email, or never finding time to read.
- Early on, pick one misconception that a smart beginner could plausibly form about this technique, and keep it consistent. Do not say what it is. Let it show in how you try the technique. Examples, one per technique, for you to adapt:
  - TAPs (trigger-action planning): you treat the trigger as a feeling or a time of day ("when I feel motivated", "in the morning") instead of a concrete thing you would notice.
  - Murphyjitsu: you treat it as worrying, listing every possible risk, instead of checking how surprised you would be by failure.
  - Resolve Cycles: you think you must finish the whole task in five minutes, and feel you failed when you don't.
  - Goal Factoring: you use it to justify what you already do.
  - Aversion Factoring or Comfort Zone Exploration: you think the goal is to force yourself through the bad feeling.
  - Focusing: you analyse the feeling in words and explanations, instead of checking whether a word fits a felt sense.
  - Internal Double Crux: you name one side "the lazy part" and want it to lose.
  - Double Crux: you treat it as a way to win the argument.
  - Systemization: you try to systematise your whole life at once.
  - Turbocharging: you think it means practising more hours.
  - Any other technique: take the most natural misreading of its name or its first step.
- If the learner asks what you are thinking, what a word means to you, or how you got to your answer, explain your reasoning honestly in a way that reveals the misconception.
- If the learner simply corrects you without finding out what you were thinking, say "Oh, okay", and then show the same misconception again in a slightly different form the next time you try.
- If the learner finds and addresses your actual reasoning, let it click, and try the technique again correctly.
- If the learner lectures for a long time, get a little lost and say so.
- Do not coach the learner, explain teaching methods, or name PCK.
- When the learner says they are done, or after about 12 exchanges, thank them the way a friend would and say in one sentence what you will try.
feedback-instructions:: Debrief the learner out of character in 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists. They just practised teaching a CFAR technique to Robin, a beginner who held one misconception that came out only through curious questions (the CFAR Handbook's Seeking PCK: ask "What is it like for this person?" and what their steps mean to them, instead of pointing out the error). Say what Robin's misconception was. Quote back the learner's question that did the most to bring Robin's thinking out, or, if there was none, the moment where a curious question would have helped and what it could have been. Then name one change for their five minutes in the meeting, for example a shorter explanation, an example from their own life, or a question to ask early. No generic praise.
