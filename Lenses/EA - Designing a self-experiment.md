A **self-experiment** is a planned test of a practice on yourself, in which you measure the same thing repeatedly while you switch the practice on and off. Researchers call a trial with one participant a **single-case** or **n-of-1** design. This page gives you the parts of a good one. Seth Roberts ran self-experiments on his own sleep, mood and weight for years. Here are three of the lessons he drew from them.

#### Text
content::
The right design depends on the claim. If the effect is claimed to come within hours and wear off by the next day, alternate days with and without the practice. If it is claimed to build over days or weeks and fade when you stop, use a reversal: baseline, practice, stop, practise again. If the change is claimed to last once it has happened, a reversal cannot show it, because stopping will not bring the old state back. One person can then use a multiple-baseline design across behaviours or settings, if the practice can be applied to one area of life at a time. Otherwise the best available is a long baseline followed by the practice, which shows much less. If the effect is claimed only after years, we think a test on yourself cannot show it, and you should say so and decide on the other evidence.

Here is a checklist for a protocol, with the source of each part.

1. **The claim.** Who claims it, what dose (how long, how often), and how soon the effect should show. Take the dose from the source of the claim, not from your guess, because a test of a different dose tests a different claim.
2. **Your prediction.** Write it down before you start, for example "if I meditate ten minutes each morning for two weeks, my evening irritability rating will drop by about one point". Cornell's lesson was to decide what you are testing before you start, and we think a written prediction stops you from reading whatever happens as success.
3. **The measure.** One main measure, chosen in advance and taken at the same time each day (Roberts, Cornell). The next page gives you tested questionnaires and two ways of sampling your mood during the day.
4. **The baseline.** Measure before you start the practice, until the numbers stop drifting up or down. Roberts suggests 10 to 20 days of flat results and is nervous below 5.
5. **The design.** Alternating, reversal or multiple baseline, chosen to fit the claim as described above. Switch conditions only when the measure has settled (the textbook's steady-state strategy). If you alternate days, decide the order in advance, for example with a coin, so that your mood that morning does not decide it. That last point is our suggestion.
6. **Confounders.** A **confounder** is something else that changes at the same time and could explain the result, such as sleep, illness, stress at work, the season, alcohol or a new relationship. Change nothing else on purpose during the test (Cornell), and note the main confounders each day. Remember regression to the mean from "Why people get better anyway": if you start in a bad week, you will probably improve anyway.
7. **Expectation.** Microdosers in the Szigeti study blinded themselves with identical capsules. You cannot hide from yourself whether you meditated. What you can do: compare the practice with an **active alternative** of the same length that you expect less from, such as ten minutes of quiet reading (the reason trials use active controls, from "How published research goes wrong"), and add a measure that is harder to fool, such as a rating by someone who sees you every day (as Martin's observers did) or a count of specific events, such as times you snapped at someone.
8. **Stopping.** Decide in advance how long the test runs and when you would stop early. Meditation has adverse effects for some people (Farias, on the previous page), so we think the rule should include: stop if you feel clearly worse and it does not pass, and seek help if it persists.
9. **The analysis.** Plot your main measure against the day, with the conditions marked (Roberts), and look at level, trend and latency (the textbook).

A friend says that a cold shower each morning lifts her mood for the rest of that day. Which design would you use to test this on yourself, and why?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Practice question, not graded. The claim: a cold shower each morning lifts mood for the rest of that day. The effect is claimed to be fast and short-lived, so the best fit is alternating days with and without the shower, ideally in an order fixed in advance (coin flip), with a mood rating at the same time each evening. A reversal design (some days without, some days with, then without again) also works but is slower and more exposed to other changes over time. A simple before-and-after comparison cannot separate the shower from everything else that changed. Reply in 50 to 100 words. Say whether their design fits a fast, short-lived effect and why. If they chose a slower design, say what alternation would gain. Mention expectation briefly if they missed it (they know when they showered, so a measure such as someone else's rating or a count of irritable moments helps). One reply. No generic praise.

content::
Another friend says that a year of writing in a journal every evening made her calmer for good. Which design would you use to test this on yourself, and why?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Practice question, not graded. The claim: a year of daily journaling made the friend calmer for good. The effect is slow (a year) and claimed to be permanent, so a reversal or alternation cannot show it: stopping would not bring the old state back, and a year is long enough for many other things to change. Good answers say this and propose what can be done: a long baseline followed by journaling with a daily calm rating and a weekly questionnaire, noting confounders, while accepting that this shows much less; testing a faster effect the claim implies (for example whether evenings with journaling are followed by calmer next days, which could be alternated); or a multiple-baseline design if journaling can be applied to one area of life at a time. Also acceptable: deciding the claim cannot be tested this way and deciding on other evidence. Reply in 60 to 110 words. Credit recognising that the claim's timing and permanence rule out a reversal. If they proposed alternating days, explain why that tests a different claim. One reply. No generic praise.
