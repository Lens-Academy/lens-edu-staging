#### Text
content::
\## What counts as a draft

The draft you hand over at the midpoint is the first time someone else reads your work. The goal is not polish; it is that your review partner can read it from the first line to the last without stopping to ask you what something is. That needs four things:

- **It is in order.** The sections appear in the order the final version will have them, even if some are a heading and a sentence.
- **Holes are labelled.** Every gap says what is missing and, if you know, where it will come from: `[TODO: source for the thermal headroom claim]`, `[TODO: third scenario, not yet priced]`. An unlabelled gap reads as a mistake; a labelled one reads as a plan.
- **The first paragraph says what it is.** Who it is for, what question it answers, what the answer currently is. A reviewer who has to reach page three to find out what they are reading gives worse feedback.
- **It is shareable.** A link that your partner can open: a document, a notebook, a repository. Check the permissions before you paste it.

A complete short draft beats a long fragment. If you have two finished sections and four to go, write the four as headings with a labelled hole each and hand that over.

\## The reader's guide

Your partner has about ten minutes at meeting 3 and about an hour in week 4. Tell them where to spend it. A reader's guide is three or four sentences: what this is, what is finished and what is a hole, where to look first, and what you already know is weak. Reviewers who are told where the weakness is do not waste their hour rediscovering it; they spend it on whether the fix you have in mind works.

\## Link and reader's guide

The link to your draft, then the reader's guide: what it is in two sentences (reader, question, current answer), what is finished and what is a labelled hole, and where a reviewer with ten minutes should look first.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 10: a link to the draft. 45: what the draft is, in about two sentences, 15 each: who the reader is, what question the draft answers, and the current answer or position (a topic alone earns none of the last two). 25: what is finished and what is still a labelled hole. 20: one specific place a reviewer with ten minutes should look first, such as a section, table or figure; "the whole thing" earns nothing here. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Link: (shared document link). This is a memo for the treaty secretariat's verification working group on whether rack-level power metering can tell training from inference at a declared data centre; my current answer is that it can only for runs well above the threshold. Sections 1 to 3 and the evasion table are finished; section 4 (costs) is a labelled hole waiting on a vendor quote, and the conclusion is a first-pass paragraph. With ten minutes, read the evasion table in section 3: rows 2 and 3 carry the argument."
feedback-instructions:: If the current answer is missing from the description, say that a draft with no current answer cannot be reviewed for whether the answer is right, and ask what the answer is today, even if it changes. If "look first" is everything, ask which ten minutes would most change the final version. Otherwise, name the most important thing still missing or wrong, if anything, then one sentence confirming the guide is usable and a reminder to check the link's sharing permissions. No praise.

content::
\## Weakest part, and the question you want answered

Name the weakest section and why it is weak. Then write the one question you want your reviewer to answer. Make it a question they can settle by reading: "does the evasion in row 3 actually bypass the countermeasure in row 2?", not "is it good?".
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 20: names one specific section as the weakest. 30: says why it is weak in terms a reader could check on the page, such as thin evidence, an argument that rests on an unsourced number, or a mismatch with the intended reader, not only a quality word like "not strong enough". 50: the one question for the reviewer, 25: the reviewer can settle it by reading the draft alone, without the author's private knowledge, and 25: it has a yes-or-no or short-answer shape, not "is it good?". Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The weakest section is section 3, the evasion table: row 3's claim that masking power draw costs a violator at least 20% of throughput rests on one unsourced estimate. My question: does the evasion in row 3 actually get past the countermeasure in row 2, as the table is written?"
feedback-instructions:: If the question is not answerable from the draft, rewrite it as one that is, in one sentence, and ask whether that is what they meant. If the weakness is a quality word, ask what a reviewer would see on the page that shows it. Otherwise, name the most important gap or weakness in one sentence. Then remind them: share the link with the review partner before meeting 3, not after. Two to four sentences. No praise.

Send your draft link and reader's guide to your review partner today. At the meeting you will each read the other's draft for about fifteen minutes and give first reactions out loud; the written review comes in week 4. Bring the hardest problem you have right now, in one sentence, for the whole group.
