summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky argues that Science says "go test it" but does not say which theory is right before the test, so it would let you waste ten years on a doomed theory. Some questions have large future consequences but cannot be cheaply tested now (macroscopic superpositions, parts of evolutionary psychology, cryonics). Absence of evidence is evidence of absence only to the degree you could reasonably expect the evidence to appear (snake oil versus gaps in the fossil record). Science's verdict without a test is "not proven", and reading that as a rejection is a step outside Science. A clear negative trial (56% versus 57% recovery) is a real verdict, not "not proven". #### Question: Open
id:: 30b6ca42-ed2f-48b2-96de-8179cce3a822
content::
Your friend says: "I treat these two claims the same way. There is no experimental proof for either, so I assume both are false."

1. A herbal tea that has been sold for twenty years, whose sellers say it cures the common cold within a day.

2. A prediction that a space probe launching next year will find liquid water under the ice of a distant moon.

For each claim, say whether the missing proof is strong evidence against it, and why.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The essay's idea: a lack of evidence counts against a claim only to the degree that we could reasonably expect the evidence to exist if the claim were true. Where a test would be easy and expected, its absence is strong evidence against the claim. Where nobody could have run the test yet, the absence says little, and the honest verdict is "not proven", not "false".

Score out of 100.

50: Claim 1 (the tea). The missing proof is strong evidence against it, because if the tea really cured colds in a day, after twenty years we would expect someone to have tested it or to have seen many clear recoveries, so the lack of such evidence is telling. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer says the missing proof counts against the tea but gives only a reason unrelated to whether evidence would be expected (for example "teas don't cure things" or "the sellers are biased").

50: Claim 2 (the probe). The missing proof is not strong evidence against it, because the probe has not flown, so no one could have the evidence yet. The lack of proof tells us little about it. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer says the missing proof does not count against it but gives no reason.

Cap at 40 if the answer says the missing proof counts equally against both claims, or that missing proof never counts against any claim.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The tea: yes, that's strong evidence against it. If it cured colds in a day, twenty years is plenty of time for a simple test or for lots of obvious recoveries to be noticed, so the silence is suspicious. The probe: no. Nobody could have checked yet because the probe hasn't gone, so we'd expect no proof either way. That claim is just unproven, and calling it false is a step beyond what the evidence says."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Yudkowsky's "When Science Can't Help" and judged two unproven claims: a cold-curing tea sold for twenty years, and a prediction about a probe that has not launched. Key ideas from the essay: absence of evidence counts against a claim only to the degree you would expect the evidence to appear (snake oil versus gaps in the fossil record). Without a test, Science's verdict is "not proven", and treating that as "false" is a step outside Science. Some questions with large consequences cannot be cheaply tested yet.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent recall, well done). Below a full score, name the single most important gap, usually that the answer did not tie each verdict to whether we would expect the evidence by now. At full score, confirm briefly and add one sentence linking the tea to the essay's snake oil example. Grade only against what the essay supplied.

If the student says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold: ask them to imagine the tea really worked and say what we would have seen by now. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on where their answer stands.
