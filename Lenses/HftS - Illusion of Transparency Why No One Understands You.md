summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky describes the illusion of transparency. Because we know what we mean, our intended reading falls into place when we read our own words, and we expect others to read them the same way. Evidence from Keysar's studies. Subjects who knew Mark's dinner was bad thought June would hear sarcasm in "marvelous, just marvelous", even when told Mark wanted to hide his opinion. Groups taught opposite meanings of the idiom "the goose hangs high" each thought an uninformed listener would take their meaning. Speakers thought they were understood in 72% of cases but were understood in 61%, and when listeners had not understood, speakers thought they had in 46% of cases. People who only overheard showed no such bias. Chamberlain's letter meant as a warning was read by Hitler as conciliatory. The lesson is not to be quick to blame people who misread your clear sentences. #### Question: Open
id:: a54a87c4-e16e-4ca7-8d0e-0d2fe5afbd0f
content::
A manager messages a designer: "Can you make the sign-up page simpler?" She means cutting the form from three steps to one. The designer removes most of the text and pictures and keeps the three steps. When the manager sees it, she says: "But my message was perfectly clear."

Using the essay, explain why the message seemed clear to her, and why her sense that it was clear tells her little about whether the designer could understand it.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay describes the illusion of transparency. Because we know what we meant, our intended reading falls into place when we read our own words, so we expect others to see it too. The listener has only the words, which are often more ambiguous than the speaker thinks. In a study, speakers believed they were understood far more often than they were, including many cases where the listener had misunderstood, while people who only overheard did not overestimate. In this scenario, "simpler" fits both "fewer steps" and "less clutter".

50: Why it seemed clear. The answer says she read her message already knowing what she meant (fewer steps), so that meaning seemed to be in the words.

50: Why her sense of clarity tells her little. The answer says that this feeling would be there whether or not the words were clear to someone else, because it comes from her own knowledge of what she meant, that speakers in general overestimate how well they are understood, or that the designer had only the words, which could also mean other things (less text and fewer pictures). Any one of these reasons earns full marks for this element.

Cap at 40 if the answer blames the designer for misreading a clear message.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "She knew she meant fewer steps, so when she read 'simpler' that meaning seemed obvious. The designer only had the word 'simpler', which could just as well mean less clutter. Her feeling that the message was clear comes from what she already knew, so she'd have felt it either way. It says nothing about how the words look to someone who doesn't know what she meant."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Illusion of Transparency" and applied it to a manager whose request was misread. The essay's key ideas: we know what we mean, so our intended reading falls into place when we read our own words, and we expect others to see it. The listener has only the words, which are more ambiguous than we think. Speakers overestimate how often they are understood (72% believed against 61% actual), while people who only overhear do not. Do not be quick to blame people who misread your clear sentences. Here "simpler" fits both "fewer steps" and "less clutter".

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap, usually explaining why it seemed clear without saying why that feeling is poor evidence of clarity. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example whether she would have felt any different if her words had been ambiguous. At 100, confirm briefly.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as the two groups taught opposite meanings of "the goose hangs high". If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
