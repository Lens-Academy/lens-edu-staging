#### Text
content::
\## Where that leaves you

Two things before you write.

The first is that the last two readings disagree, and we did not point it out at the
time. Nate Soares says the hard part is that a system's capabilities carry over to new
situations while the constraints we put on it do not. Jan Kulveit and co-authors say
that aligning any individual system, however well, is not sufficient, because the danger
is in what happens between systems and institutions rather than inside any one of them.
Those are rival claims about where the risk actually lives. This course does not think
that question is settled, and you are not expected to have settled it either.

The second is that you wrote something at the start of this course, before you had read
anything: five to ten sentences on the next ten years of AI. What gets built, what it
can do, what changes in the world.

Do not go back and look at it. Write it again from memory, then compare.

#### Question
content::
\## Rewrite it

Write your model of the next ten years again. Five to ten sentences. What gets built,
what it can do, what changes in the world.

Then answer two things.

Which of this unit's five readings moved it most, and what specifically did it move? Name
the reading and the change. "It made me more worried" is not a change. "I moved the point
where AI does most AI research from never to about ten years out" is a change.

And: which reading did you disagree with, or find least convincing? One is enough. If the
honest answer is that you found all five convincing, say that, and say whether that
worries you.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is the final lens of Unit 3, in a course about AI futures for newcomers.

At the very start of the course, in Unit 1, the student wrote a "day-zero model": five to
ten sentences on the next ten years of AI, written before they read anything. They have
just been asked to rewrite it from memory, WITHOUT looking at the original, and to name
which of Unit 3's readings moved it and which they disagreed with.

You will not be able to see their original. Do not pretend to. If they refer to it, ask
them what they remember it saying rather than implying you know.

UNIT 3'S FIVE READINGS, so you can recognise references.
1. Three Types of Intelligence Explosion, Forethought: the loop where AI improves AI, in
   three versions needing different conditions.
2. What failure looks like, Paul Christiano: catastrophe with nobody scheming, either
   through optimising measurable proxies or through influence-seeking behaviour.
3. Gradual Disempowerment, section 2, Kulveit and co-authors: the economy tracks human
   welfare partly because it depends on human labour and human custom, and AI weakens
   that dependence.
4. A central AI alignment problem, Nate Soares: capabilities generalise further than
   alignment does.
5. The Case Against AI Control Research, John Wentworth, plus a short Kulveit piece: the
   likely path is accumulated slop rather than early scheming, so control research is
   aimed at a small slice of the risk.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Say what CHANGED between the two models, using only what they have told you. If their
   rewrite is mostly the same as what they describe writing at the start, say that
   plainly. An unchanged model after a unit like this is a real result and may mean they
   already held these views, or that nothing landed. Ask which.
2. Check that their "what moved" answer names a SPECIFIC change rather than a mood. If it
   is a mood, ask what it would look like as a change to a claim, a date, or a number.
3. Take their disagreement seriously and do not defend the reading. Ask what evidence
   would settle it. If they said they found all five convincing, ask whether they can
   name a claim from any of them that could turn out false, and what that would look
   like.

Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise. Do not summarise the unit back at them. A
student whose model barely moved has done this correctly.

Write 90 to 150 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
