\## Unit 5: Facing the Challenge, Part 2

**Readings:** Chapter 12 ("I Don't Want to Be Alarmist"), Chapter 13 ("Shut It Down"), Chapter 14 ("Where There's Life, There's Hope"), Closing Words

*Definitions reflect how this course uses each term, not necessarily its broader academic meaning. Each term is defined once, where it first comes up. For the authors' full arguments, see the corresponding chapters.*

**The One-Chance Problem**: Humans reliably fail to believe unthinkable disasters can happen (Chernobyl experts denying the failure as it occurred; faith in the "unsinkable" Titanic). With past disasters there was a second time to learn and reform, but with ASI there is no second time. The denial pattern is exactly the barrier to meeting the higher burden of proof a one-shot problem demands.

**Ladder in the Dark**: Competing AI companies climb a ladder without knowing which rung is the last safe one. Uncertainty doesn't protect you, because the problem is structural, not informational: researchers, executives, and world leaders each face locally rational incentives to keep climbing (stopping means ceding the race), so the collective outcome is predictably fatal even though no single rung is identifiable as lethal in advance.

**CERN Comparison**: CERN spent around ten years calculating whether the Large Hadron Collider could destroy Earth before switching it on, a model of appropriate caution under existential uncertainty. The contrast shows AI development has no equivalent threshold, verification requirement, or pause mechanism.

**The WWII Analogy**: The book's reply to "coordinating great powers on AI restriction is impossible." The Allies mobilized on an extraordinary scale (around $6 trillion, tens of millions of personnel, many not personally at risk). Claiming countries "could never" restrict AI amounts to claiming they couldn't care even 1% as much as they cared about WWII. Impossibility becomes a claim about motivation, not fact.

**Coalition Strategy** (the narrow ask): An anti-extinction coalition should limit its ask to preventing human extinction and not bundle it with adjacent causes (killer robots, job loss), because bundling introduces disagreement that can collapse the coalition, and coalition failure risks extinction. The asymmetry: bundling and failing costs everything, while staying narrow just means other issues get addressed separately later.

**Un-Writing Our Fate**: Via the Cold War nuclear precedent, a predicted catastrophe can be actively reversed through sustained, organized effort rather than luck. In 1952 nuclear war looked rationally predictable, yet decades of arms agreements, monitoring, and hotlines (plus decision-makers who would personally suffer the consequences) un-wrote it. Frames hope as an active, rational posture, not wishful thinking.

**Role-Specific Action**: Differentiated calls to action, each group holding a lever no one else has.
- **Government leaders**: Signaling willingness to enter a treaty (not a unilateral halt), so no state is disadvantaged by agreeing.
- **Skeptical politicians**: GPU concentration, keeping compute clusters concentrated and monitorable now to preserve the option of halting later, even without accepting the full risk argument.
- **Ordinary citizens**: Political pressure, voting (especially primaries), writing representatives, lawful protest, and simply talking about it, building the permission structure that lets leaders act.

**The Two Prayers**: The book's closing device. The first prayer asks that the authors be proven wrong, shamed, and forgotten (answered if AI turns out non-dangerous and humanity lives happily without acting). The second, their "true last prayer" and final line, is answered if humanity acts and averts catastrophe. Called "true" because the authors refuse to rely on passively being wrong: hope you're wrong, but act as if you're not.
