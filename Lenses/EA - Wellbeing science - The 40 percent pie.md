The claim: Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade 2005 (Review of General Psychology, p. 116) drew a pie chart in which genetics account for about 50% of the population variation in chronic happiness and circumstances about 10%, which 'leaves as much as 40% of the variance for intentional activity'. #### Text
content::
Many popular books on happiness say that 40% of your happiness is up to you. The figure comes from a pie chart in a 2005 paper by Sonja Lyubomirsky, Kennon Sheldon and David Schkade. To follow the debate about it you need two terms.

- **Variance explained.** People in a group differ in how happy they are. A factor "explains 10% of the variance" when 10% of those differences between people go along with differences in that factor. It describes a group of people, not one person.
- **Heritability** is the share of the differences between people in a trait that goes along with differences in their genes. The happiness estimates come from studies of twins ([Brown and Rohrer 2019, section 3.1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-019-00128-4)).

The original paper says: "existing evidence suggests that genetics account for approximately 50% of the population variation [...] and circumstances account for approximately 10% [...]. This leaves as much as 40% of the variance for intentional activity, supporting our proposal that volitional efforts offer a promising possible route to longitudinal increases in happiness" ([Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade 2005, p. 116](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Pursuing_Happiness-_The_Architecture_of_Sustainable_Change.pdf)). By **intentional activity** they mean things people choose to do and that take effort. Their examples include exercising regularly and striving for important personal goals ([Brown and Rohrer 2019, section 2.1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-019-00128-4)).

In 2019 the psychologists Nicholas Brown and Julia Rohrer traced where the numbers came from. Five passages of their paper follow: how the pie spread, why a split of differences between people says little about one person, why the three slices need not add up, where the 10% for circumstances came from, and their summary.

#### Question: Open
id:: ad6abdb6-246f-4a00-af0f-962c4c17ba24
content::
Without looking back, write down everything you remember about the pie, the critique and the reply. Two minutes, no need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read about the happiness pie (Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade 2005: about 50% of differences in chronic happiness between people from genes, 10% from circumstances, leaving as much as 40% for intentional activity), the critique by Brown and Rohrer 2019, and the reply by Sheldon and Lyubomirsky. They write what they remember. You are a brief, honest mirror, diagnostic not instructional.

Key points: (1) Variance explained describes differences between people in a population, not how much one person can change. The share a factor explains depends on how much people differ in it. (2) The 40% was never measured. It is what is left after subtracting 50 and 10 from 100, and the subtraction assumes the three factors are independent and add up, with no measurement error and no other causes. (3) The 50% heritability figure is uncertain and probably too low. The 10% for circumstances came from demographic variables (age, income, education, race, sex, family stage) in two 1970s US surveys, not from all life circumstances. Brown and Rohrer's reanalysis with a wider set of circumstance variables found 18 to 26%. With other plausible numbers the leftover could be 5%. (4) The pie spread through popular books and coaching. (5) Sheldon and Lyubomirsky agree that 40% was likely an over-estimate and that the numbers were speculative, but hold that what people do can raise wellbeing lastingly, with smaller effects than they first thought, and only with buy-in and effort.

Acknowledge what is right without inflation, name the most important missing point in one sentence, correct errors in one sentence, normalise gaps. 60 to 110 words. No generic praise. One turn, then tell them to move on.

Give the same full credit to an answer that instead explains that the share a factor explains depends on how much people in the group differ in that factor (for example, if everyone did the same helpful activity it would explain little or none of the differences, though it would still help each person), so the share is not a measure of how much an activity can do.

A highly heritable trait can still change, and an activity everyone did would explain no variance even if it helped everyone."

content::
Why is the 10 percent figure for circumstances likely too low?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The question asks why the figure that circumstances explain only about 10% of the differences in happiness between people is likely too low. Reasons from Brown and Rohrer (2019) that count as correct: (a) the figure came from a few demographic variables (age, income, education, race, sex, marital or family stage), not from the much wider range of life circumstances, and a reanalysis of the same 1970s survey data with 15 to 18 circumstance variables found about 18 to 26%; (b) the data came from samples within one country at one time (US adults in the early 1970s), where many circumstances such as political freedom, corruption, basic services or a national economic crisis barely vary, so they cannot show up as explaining differences, although they affect wellbeing (for example the large drop in Greece's average life evaluation during its financial crisis); (c) measurement error in the happiness scores lowers observed associations, so the true share is underestimated.

Score out of 100.
100: the answer gives one of these reasons, in its own words, with enough explanation to show why it makes the figure too low.
60: the answer names one of these reasons without saying why it lowers the figure.
Cap at 20 if the answer's only reason is that circumstances matter a lot in general, with no reason tied to how the 10% was obtained.
feedback-instructions:: The learner gives a reason why the 10% figure for circumstances is likely too low. Below full marks, name the reason closest to theirs and what it needs: the 10% came from a handful of demographic variables in two 1970s US surveys, not from life circumstances in general, and Brown and Rohrer's reanalysis of those data with more circumstance variables found 18 to 26%. Also mention, if they did not, that a sample from one country cannot show the effect of circumstances that barely vary within it, such as Greece's financial crisis, during which average life evaluations fell by almost 1.5 points on a 0 to 10 ladder. At full marks, confirm in one sentence and add one of the other reasons. 60 to 110 words. At most two replies. No generic praise.

content::
Sheldon and Lyubomirsky write that high wellbeing "is like a bicycle tire that needs continued pumping to stay inflated" and that joyful lives "involve more than mere contentment or peacefulness". How does this picture compare with the target this course explores, deep and stable peace that does not depend on circumstances?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner compares Sheldon and Lyubomirsky's picture of high wellbeing (a bicycle tyre that needs continued pumping, sustained by a steady inflow of positive experiences and effortful, need-satisfying activity, joyful lives involving more than contentment or peacefulness) with the course's target state (deep, stable peace that does not depend on circumstances). The course does not decide which picture is right or whether the target is reachable.

Points a strong answer may make: the two describe different things. One is a high level of positive feelings and life satisfaction kept up by ongoing activity, the other a stable calm that does not need any particular input. In the tyre picture wellbeing falls if the activity stops, which is a kind of dependence, though on one's own actions rather than on outside events. Their evidence comes from self-report scales of positive feelings and life satisfaction, which may not register peace. Some may argue the two are compatible, for example that activity matters for joy while peace is a separate dimension.

Reply with the strongest point in their answer and one thing they did not consider, as a question. Accept any well-argued view. 70 to 120 words. At most two replies. No generic praise.
