#### Text
content::
\## Make your current model visible

You have now worked through several reasons for taking catastrophic AI risk seriously and several reasons for caution about those arguments. The risk landscape includes abrupt and gradual failures, extinction, permanent disempowerment, catastrophic suffering, malicious use, competitive pressure, organizational failure, and autonomous loss of control. You have also examined why a precise probability can hide uncertainty about the model, why precaution has costs of its own, and why informed people can understand one another and still disagree.

Now write down the model that currently drives your own judgment. There is no preferred conclusion. You may end up thinking catastrophic AI risk is low and many proposed interventions are premature, or that it deserves unusually high priority. The standard is whether your conclusion is explicit enough that later evidence can change a specific claim, and whether your proposed action follows from the uncertainty you actually have.

Save this map. Unit 4 will return to your assumptions about agency and power-seeking, Unit 5 to evidence about an AI's internal state and intentions, Unit 6 to control and deference, Unit 7 to the institutions you expect to act, and Unit 8 to the kinds of future you are trying to protect.

#### Question: Open
id:: 3bc6038c-37a3-4da6-8fe7-6b103675263c
content::
Take about 20 minutes. Work through all six parts.

**1. Your current conclusion**

In two to four sentences, answer:

**How seriously should society currently take the possibility of catastrophic harm from advanced AI, and why?**

You can give a qualitative answer or a probability if you find one useful. Do not write what you think this course wants you to say.

**2. Your risk landscape**

Name the two or three pathways that currently contribute most to your concern. They can include malicious use, race dynamics, organizational failure, rogue systems, gradual disempowerment, accumulative systemic risk, catastrophic suffering, or another pathway you can explain.

For each one, write one sentence on the mechanism.

**3. Your load-bearing claims**

List three to five claims that do most of the work in your conclusion. Make them specific enough that they could turn out false.

For each claim, mark whether your uncertainty is mainly about:
- evidence or data
- a numerical parameter
- the causal model
- omitted possibilities
- values
- something else

**4. Your strongest challenge**

Write the strongest argument you can against your own overall conclusion. Use an objection that actually creates uncertainty for you, not one that is easy to dismiss.

Then write what evidence would most weaken that objection.

**5. The highest-value question**

If you could get a trustworthy answer to one question before making a much larger decision about AI safety, what would you ask?

Explain what you would do differently depending on the answer.

**6. What action survives?**

Finish this sentence:

**"Given what I currently believe, one action that seems justified even if an important part of my model is wrong is..."**

The action can be research, evaluation, governance preparation, technical work, funding, learning, career exploration, delaying a larger commitment, or deliberately doing nothing yet.

Explain:
- which risk pathways make the action valuable
- one important way your model could be wrong while the action would still make sense
- one finding that would make you stop or change the action

feedback-instructions::
This is an ungraded running artifact. Do not pressure the learner toward greater or lower AI concern and do not supply a preferred probability.

Use at most four tutor replies unless the learner explicitly asks to continue. Focus on the places where the map is vague or decision-irrelevant. Look for claims that cannot turn out false, evidence that would not actually distinguish the claim from its alternative, a "highest-value question" whose answer would not change any action, and an allegedly robust action that depends on one narrow threat model.

When useful, ask one targeted question at a time. Do not rewrite the learner's worldview for them.

On close, summarise in 140 to 220 words:
1. their current conclusion
2. the two most load-bearing claims
3. their strongest skeptical challenge
4. their highest-value information question
5. their proposed action and the condition under which they would change it

End by reminding them that later units will return to this map.
