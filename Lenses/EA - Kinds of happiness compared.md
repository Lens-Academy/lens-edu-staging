\## The kinds side by side

The table collects what you read. The second column matters most: the kinds are not all the same type of thing. Some are feelings, one is a judgement, one is a way of living, and some are described as a changed relation to all experience.

| Kind | What type of thing | In short | Said to depend on circumstances? | Who aims at it | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Positive feeling** (hedonic happiness, emotional wellbeing) | A feeling | Frequent pleasant feelings, few unpleasant ones, moment to moment | Yes. Daily emotions track health, caregiving, loneliness and income | Hedonists (Bentham), much of happiness science | [Diener](https://nobaproject.com/modules/happiness-the-science-of-subjective-well-being), [Haybron 2.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/happiness/), [Kahneman and Deaton 2010](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107) |
| **Life satisfaction** (life evaluation) | A judgement | A favourable judgement of your life as a whole | Yes. It tracks income and lasting life events | Most wellbeing surveys and economists | [Diener](https://nobaproject.com/modules/happiness-the-science-of-subjective-well-being), [Haybron 2.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/happiness/), [Lindqvist et al. 2020](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24667) |
| **Eudaimonia** | A way of living | Living well: exercising your best capacities, pursuing excellence and intrinsic goals by your own choice. "Not a feeling" | Aristotle: partly, it needs some external goods. Stoics: no, virtue is enough | Aristotle, the Stoics, self-determination theory | [Ryan, Huta and Deci 2008](https://www.heartofcharacter.org/wp-content/uploads/Living-Well-Ryan-et-al.pdf), [Haybron 1.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/happiness/), [Aristotle, Book I](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8438/8438-h/8438-h.htm) |
| **Ataraxia** (Epicurus) and **apatheia** (Stoics) | A calm state of mind | Epicurus: freedom from mental disturbance, the best pleasure, reached by limiting desires and removing fear. Stoics: freedom from unhealthy passions (which are mistaken judgements), with healthy feelings such as delight remaining, a byproduct of virtue | Both aim to make it independent of circumstances | Epicureans, Stoics | [IEP Epicurus 5.b](https://iep.utm.edu/epicur/), [IEP Stoicism 4](https://iep.utm.edu/stoicism/) |
| **Sukha**, in three grades | A feeling at the lower grades. At the top, described as a lasting trait | Pleasure from the senses; pleasure in meditative absorption (jhana); the pleasure of a mind released from passion, aversion and delusion. Ekman et al.: an enduring trait from mental balance and insight | The sense grade, yes. The higher grades are said not to, with "reduced vulnerability to outer circumstances" | Buddhist traditions | [Niramisa Sutta](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn36/sn36.031.than.html), [Ekman et al. 2005](https://centerforcontemplativeresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Emotions-and-Wellbeing-1-1.pdf) |
| **Contentment** (santosha) | A state of mind | Happiness from the end of wanting. Yoga Sutra 2.42: "By contentment the acquisition of extreme happiness" | Said not to | Yoga tradition | [Yoga Sutra 2.42 with Vyasa's commentary](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutras-with-commentaries/d/doc1216693.html) |
| **Fundamental wellbeing** (Martin's PNSE) | Described as a lasting change in the sense of self and experience | Ongoing deep inner peace, fewer and shorter emotions. At the far end, no emotion and no sense of agency | Reported as largely independent, but deep triggers can push it down | Martin; he presents it as a secular name for awakening | [Martin](https://nonsymbolic.org/PNSE-Article.pdf), [Life Itself](https://lifeitself.org/learn/fundamental-wellbeing) |

**Equanimity** (upekkha) does not fit in the table, because it is not one more kind of happiness. It is "an even-minded mental state or dispositional tendency toward all experiences or objects, regardless of their origin or their affective valence (pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral)" ([Desbordes et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4350240/)). So it can be present together with any row above, and with pain. It is not indifference. The Niramisa Sutta grades it separately from pleasure.

Three of the rows make a similar claim in different words: Epicurus's calm, the higher grades of sukha and contentment all say that the best happiness is what remains when wanting stops. Vyasa's commentary on Yoga Sutra 2.42 puts it this way: sensual and heavenly pleasures "do not come to the sixteenth part of the joy due to the suppression of desires" ([wisdomlib](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutras-with-commentaries/d/doc1216693.html)). Whether that claim is true is a question for later modules.

#### Question: Open
id:: 8575c859-2aea-476b-8184-3682545d9a6d
content::
Practice. For each quote, say which kind (or kinds) of happiness it is about, and what in the wording tells you.

A. "Gluttons smacking greasy lips are happy, but not eudaimones." (Broadie and Rowe, quoted by Ryan, Huta and Deci)

B. "A year after losing the use of their legs, and a year after winning the lotto, lottery winners and paraplegics are equally happy with their lives." (Dan Gilbert)

C. "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things." (Epictetus)
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Practice before the graded sorting test. The learner sorts three quotes into kinds of happiness and says what in the wording shows it.

Answers:
A. Two kinds in one sentence: "happy" here means positive feeling (pleasure), while eudaimonia is a way of living that the glutton lacks. The point of the quote is that the two come apart.
B. Ambiguous on purpose. "Happy with their lives" sounds like life satisfaction (a judgement). The study Gilbert refers to (Brickman et al. 1978) asked people how happy they were, which mixes feeling and judgement. Credit either reading if the learner notices the wording, and best of all if they notice the ambiguity.
C. About freedom from disturbance, the Stoic calm (apatheia, closely related to ataraxia). Also accept "equanimity" if the learner explains it as an even relation to events, but point out that Epictetus's route is correcting judgements.

For each item, confirm or correct in one sentence. Name the one distinction the learner most needs before the test (usually: feeling versus judgement versus way of living, or equanimity as a stance rather than a feeling). 100 to 160 words. One turn. No generic praise.
