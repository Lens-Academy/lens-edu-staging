#### Text
content::
A lead has arrived and been assessed. This section is about what the regime
does next — and about doing it proportionately, which is the part that decides
whether a verification regime survives its first ambiguous case.

\## Comparing against declarations

The first move is the cheapest: set the observed activity against what was
declared, and ask for clarification. Most anomalies die here, which is the
point. A regime that escalates every anomaly has no capacity left for the ones
that matter.

\## Escalating

When clarification does not resolve it, the ladder runs roughly:

- **Task additional collection** — look again, with a different stream.
- **Seek corroboration** — find an independent source that sees the same thing.
- **Trigger an audit or challenge inspection** — the treaty's own tools, which
  is where identification hands off to resolution.

Each rung costs more, politically and diplomatically, than the one below.

\## Recording what you did not resolve

The step that gets skipped. Whatever the chosen action, the record should carry:

- The **confidence** attached to the assessment;
- Any **dissent** among the analysts;
- The **unresolved blind spots** — what nobody could see, as distinct from what
  was looked at and found clean.

An assessment that hides its own uncertainty is worth less than one that states
it, because the next decision-maker cannot calibrate on it. This is the same
discipline as the evidence standards in the capstone.

:::callout {title="Unfinished writing" tone="amber"}
This section's written output is proposed but not settled. The task is
**red-lining the treaty text**: you work from the verbatim excerpt in 2.3.4 —
item 17 of Article II's definitions, the definition of national technical means
covering satellites, signals intelligence, imagery and so on, plus Article X
¶1(b) and its clauses (i), (ii) and (iii): noninterference, no deliberate
concealment, and cooperation that is encouraged but not obligated —
and go through it with a pencil, as a lawyer preparing for negotiations would.

The questions it asks: what does the Article II definition omit? Whose
satellites count as "national" — is a commercial constellation under
government tasking NTM or not? The word "cyber" inside the definition — is that
legalized intelligence collection, or a license to hack that Beijing strikes
out first?
And what does "deliberate concealment" mean for AI — is behind-the-meter power
generation camouflage, or ordinary industrial practice?

The precedent suggests Article X's noninterference clause (¶1(b)(i)) should be
the easy part, and that drafting dies on the sharing clause. That is the
discovery the task is built to produce.
:::

#### Question: Open

content::
\### Written output, 2.3, Red-line: Red-line the NTM article

Work from the treaty text quoted in 2.3.4: item 17 of Article II's definitions, plus Article X ¶1(b) and its clauses (i), (ii) and (iii). Mark at least three gaps or ambiguities, starting from your four answers if you like, and redraft the one provision whose failure you judge most consequential. Then confront the question the historical record says drafters avoid: write the binding sharing clause the text declines to impose, since ¶1(b)(iii) encourages cooperation but obligates nothing, or defend its absence in three sentences. Test whatever you write against 2.3.4’s rule, that states sign what is symmetric, cheap, and checkable, and finish with the both-capitals test: one paragraph on why Washington accepts your article, one on why Beijing does.

**Reader:** The drafters of a US–China agreement, read in both capitals.

**Goes through peer review.** Reviewed on:

- Fidelity to what signatures can actually establish
- Precedent-consistent language
- Honesty about sharing
- Plausibility in both capitals
- Named blind spots, each with the sibling mechanism that covers it

(about 700 words)
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 20 for each of the five criteria the task lists. (1) Fidelity to what signatures can establish: the gaps and the redraft do not overclaim what imagery, thermal, power, procurement or open-source evidence proves. (2) Precedent-consistent language: the redrafted provision reads like real treaty text (the ABM, INF, CTBT or New START noninterference and no-concealment clauses) rather than invented terms. (3) Honesty about sharing: either a binding sharing clause is drafted and its cost to sources and methods faced, or its absence is defended in about three sentences; building on Article X ¶1(b)(iii), which encourages but does not oblige cooperation, is on target. (4) Plausibility in both capitals: one paragraph each on why Washington and why Beijing would accept the article, tested against the rule that states sign what is symmetric, cheap and checkable. (5) Named blind spots, each paired with the other mechanism that covers it (for example hardware, cloud or human evidence). Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Deduct 20 if fewer than three gaps or ambiguities are marked, and 20 if no provision is redrafted. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Gaps: (1) Item 17 lists means but not whose: a commercial constellation under government tasking is not clearly national, so bought imagery may fall outside the noninterference protection. (2) Cyber inside the definition reads in Beijing as a licence to hack; strike it or limit it to passive collection. (3) Clause (ii) bans deliberate concealment without saying what that means for AI: behind-the-meter power, covered cooling plant and split sites can all be ordinary industrial practice. (4) Nothing links an NTM finding to the challenge-inspection procedure. Redraft of (ii): Each Party undertakes not to use concealment measures, including the shielding of power, cooling or thermal signatures of facilities housing covered chips, that impede verification by national technical means; arrangements in place before entry into force and declared under this Agreement are not concealment measures. Sharing: I would not add a binding sharing clause. No state will commit to hand over collection that exposes its sources and methods; the nuclear record shows sharing has always been voluntary and informal; and a duty both sides would quietly ignore weakens the treaty's credibility. Instead keep (iii) and allow a Party to cite NTM findings as grounds for a challenge inspection without disclosing their source. Test: noninterference and no-concealment are symmetric, cheap and checkable; a sharing duty is none of these. Washington accepts because its satellite and signals advantage gains treaty protection and it keeps control of what it shares. Beijing accepts because cyber collection is struck, commercial imagery is open to it too, and existing facilities are not treated as concealment. Blind spots: NTM cannot see what code runs inside a declared building (covered by hardware and cloud evidence) or training split across small sites (covered by chip accounting and whistleblower channels)."
feedback-instructions:: Go criterion by criterion: name the strongest of the five and the weakest, quoting the learner's own redraft. If they avoided the sharing clause without defending its absence, say so plainly. Ask one follow-up that tests the both-capitals paragraph they found harder. No generic praise.
