#### Text
content::
Kristin Neff is a professor of educational psychology at the University of Texas at Austin. She defined self-compassion as a research construct and built the Self-Compassion Scale, the questionnaire most studies of it use ([Neff 2023](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Neff-2023.pdf), [Muris and Otgaar 2020](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/Muris2020.pdf)). With Chris Germer she later developed the eight-week Mindful Self-Compassion course (same review). Her account draws on Buddhist ideas of compassion, which she says includes oneself as well as others.

In this talk she explains why she thinks chasing self-esteem backfires and what she offers instead. If you already watched it in the tour of the schools, skip to the questions.

#### Callout: Two claims in the talk that others dispute
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
- **The "epidemic of narcissism".** Whether narcissism rose among US students has been argued over for years. A 2025 analysis of student scores on the Narcissistic Personality Inventory from 1982 to 2023 found a small decline over that period ([Oberleiter et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jopy.12982)).
- **The "reptilian brain".** The idea that the brain has a reptile layer with a mammal layer on top is rejected by neuroscientists who study brain evolution ([Cesario, Johnson and Eisthen 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0963721420917687)). Her underlying claim, that self-criticism works like a threat and kindness calms it, comes from Paul Gilbert's model, which you read two pages from now.

Her statement that research supports all of this "unequivocally" is checked in the evidence page of this module.
#### End Callout

#### Question: Open
id:: 1e7ff9c9-1ede-481a-ba31-b592d9f07dca
content::
**Recall.** Without going back to the talk, write down what you remember: how Neff defines self-esteem, what she thinks goes wrong with pursuing it, the three parts of self-compassion, and why she thinks self-criticism does not motivate. Two minutes, no need to organise.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just watched Kristin Neff's TEDx talk "The space between self-esteem and self-compassion" and is writing what they remember without looking back. Key points: (1) self-esteem is a global judgement of one's worth, (2) pursuing it pushes people to feel special and above average, which leads to comparison, putting others down, narcissism, and worth that depends on success in domains that matter to them (she gives women and appearance as an example), (3) self-compassion has three components: self-kindness versus harsh self-judgement, common humanity versus feeling isolated, and mindfulness versus being lost in the self-critic, (4) she says the main reason people criticise themselves is the belief that it motivates them, and she claims it does the opposite: it triggers a threat response and stress, while kindness activates a mammalian caregiving system and makes people feel safe enough to do their best, (5) she says self-compassion gives the benefits of self-esteem without narcissism or social comparison and gives more stable self-worth. Act as a brief, honest mirror: acknowledge what they got right without inflation, name the most important missing point in one sentence, correct any error in one sentence, and say that gaps are normal. Do not re-teach and do not ask follow-up questions. If they say they did not understand something, give one concrete foothold from the talk (for example her father and failing maths grade example). Reply in 60 to 120 words, short paragraphs, no lists, no generic praise. This is a one-turn response. Tell them to move on.
