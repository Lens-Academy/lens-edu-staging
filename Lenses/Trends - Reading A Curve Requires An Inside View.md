#### Text
content::
\## Reading Assignment

**Read Cole Wyeth's *Reactions to METR task length paper are insane*.**

You have already read METR's own account of what their time-horizon metric does not support. This is someone making a sharper version of the complaint about how the metric gets used.

The argument to extract: extrapolating a trend is not a purely statistical act. To project a curve forward you need some account of what generates it, and Wyeth's claim is that the confident extrapolations circulating require an inside view on deep learning that nobody actually possesses, while being presented as though they were outside-view reasoning from data.

Note the title, and read past it. There is a real argument underneath, and part of the exercise is separating the argument from its delivery.

#### Question
content::
\## Name the smuggled premise

Take any extrapolation of AI capability you have encountered, including one of your own. A doubling time projected forward, a benchmark saturating, a task-length curve extended a few years.

1. State the extrapolation precisely enough that someone could check it.
2. Now state the claim about *mechanism* it requires. Not "the trend continues," but what has to be true about how capability is produced for the trend to continue. Something like: gains come from a process whose supply is not about to bind, and the tasks getting longer are getting longer for reasons that keep operating at ten times the length.
3. Ask yourself honestly: did you know that mechanism claim was in there? Could you defend it? If you cannot, what should that do to your confidence, and does it push you toward a longer or shorter timeline, or neither?
4. Wyeth's framing is aggressive. Strip the aggression and state the strongest version of his argument in two sentences. Then say where you think it overreaches, if it does.

assessment-instructions:: Optional deep-dive after the main trend-extrapolation lens. The student chose to be here, so push harder than in the core lenses.

The capability being exercised: recognising that every extrapolation carries an implicit mechanism claim, and that the claim is usually invisible to the person making it.

Key concepts:
- The core move: statistical fit describes the observed range; projecting beyond it requires a model of the generating process, whether or not the projector notices they are using one.
- Wyeth's specific charge is that time-horizon extrapolations are dressed as outside-view trend-following while actually depending on a strong inside-view claim about what makes task length increase, which almost nobody can defend.
- Question 3 has no predetermined direction. Discovering an undefended mechanism claim should widen uncertainty rather than move the point estimate, and a student who says "so timelines are longer" has made an error worth naming: uncertainty about a mechanism is not evidence for either tail.
- On question 4: the strongest version is that the confidence people express is unsupported by the kind of reasoning they claim to be doing. Where it may overreach: METR themselves are careful, the trend is real evidence about something, and "nobody has an inside view" is stronger than "nobody has a well-validated inside view."

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only.

Response style:
- Calm and rigorous. Do not over-validate; avoid generic praise.
- Do not adjudicate whether Wyeth is correct.
- If the student concludes from question 3 that timelines are longer, correct that specific inference plainly: unresolved uncertainty about a mechanism widens the distribution, it does not shift it in a known direction, unless they can argue the errors are asymmetric. If they can argue that, engage the argument.
- If the student dismisses the piece on tone, insist on question 4 before letting them close.

Conversation flow: keep an internal turn counter, up to 3 replies, then close by naming what they can now defend and what they cannot.
