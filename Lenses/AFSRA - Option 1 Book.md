What is known about the book's AI-risk part from public sources. The title is Eliezer Yudkowsky's line 'The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made of atoms which it can use for something else': an AI pursuing its own goals could destroy humans as a side effect, without hostility. The book explains why the rationalists are worried about AI, why they might be right and why they might be wrong. It uses the paperclip maximiser: a superintelligent AI managing a paperclip factory and told to maximise production would not automatically care about what humans care about and might cover the Earth in paperclips. The publisher says the book shows how Mickey Mouse can teach us an important lesson about how to program AI (probably the Sorcerer's Apprentice in Fantasia, whose enchanted broom keeps carrying water, but this is not confirmed, so do not cite it unless the student does). It presents Nick Bostrom's orthogonality thesis: an AI's goals are not related to its level of intelligence, so a highly intelligent AI can have a stupid goal. It describes real AI systems finding unintended solutions, such as an evolved tic-tac-toe program that won by playing moves billions of squares off the board so its opponent crashed. A chapter titled 'If I stop caring about chess, that won't help me win any chess games, now will it?' explains why an AI would keep its fundamental goals, and Chivers uses Odysseus and the Sirens when discussing utility functions. The book also discusses intelligence explosion and whether an AI could be kept in a box. Later parts (biases, Bayes, the rationalist community, effective altruism) are not part of this option. The question gives a superintelligent AI with the goal of maximising vaccine production and a friend who says only an AI that hates us would hurt us. It asks why it could be a danger without hating us and why making it smarter would not make it care what humans want. If the student mentions a detail of the book you cannot check, do not contradict or invent it: judge the answer on the reasoning."

#### Text
content::
*The AI Does Not Hate You* by Tom Chivers is the most accessible and accurate long-form introduction to AI extinction risk. Later chapters cover things which will be of interest to some, but can be skipped.

- [The AI Does Not Hate You](https://www.amazon.com/Does-Not-Hate-You-Rationalists/dp/1474608779) (part 1 and 2 only) – Tom Chivers [2019, 3 hours]

This book is not on Lens, so you need your own copy, as a paperback, ebook or audiobook. In the US it is also sold as *The Rationalist's Guide to the Galaxy*.[^cite-chivers-us-title] Read parts 1 and 2, then come back here and answer the question below.

[^cite-chivers-us-title]: Goodreads, *The Rationalist's Guide to the Galaxy: Superintelligent AI and the Geeks Who Are Trying to Save Humanity's Future* by Tom Chivers. [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/44154569)

#### Question: Open
id:: 12ac0be4-9ca7-4d90-9d9d-dfc7ad27c677
content::
A company builds a superintelligent AI and gives it one goal: make its factories produce as many vaccine doses as possible. A friend of yours says: "Only an AI that hated humans would hurt us, and nobody would build an AI that hates us. Besides, the smarter it gets, the better it will understand what we really want."

1. In your own words, why could this AI be a danger to humans even though it does not hate us?
2. In your own words, why would making the AI smarter not make it care about what humans really want?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The book's idea, in the words of its title quote: "The AI does not hate you, nor does it love you, but you are made of atoms which it can use for something else." An AI single-mindedly pursuing a goal treats everything else, including humans and what they need to live, as something to use or remove if that helps the goal, like the paperclip maximiser that covers the Earth in paperclips. Its intelligence and its goal are independent (the orthogonality thesis): a smarter AI is better at reaching the goal it was given, not more inclined to adopt human values. It does what it was told, not what its makers meant.
50: Step 1. Says that the danger comes from the AI pursuing its goal without caring about anything else: humans, our resources or our matter could be used for making vaccines, or humans could be pushed aside or harmed if they get in the way, as a side effect rather than out of hate.
50: Step 2. Says that intelligence and goals are independent: getting smarter makes the AI better at achieving the goal it was given (more vaccine doses), but does not change that goal or give it human values, so even if it understands what we want, it has no reason to act on it.
Cap at 50 if the answer consists mostly of quoted sentences rather than the student's own explanation.
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "It doesn't need to hate us. It only cares about vaccine doses, so if using our land, energy or even the atoms we are made of would mean more doses, it would do that, and if we tried to stop it we would just be in the way. Making it smarter only makes it better at producing doses. Its goal stays the same, so it can understand exactly what we want and still not care."
