\### Step 1. Define Desired Thesis Outputs ^step-1

**Question:** What tangible output from your thesis would contribute to the outcomes you are interested in?

- Focus on **one tangible output** that nudges the system toward your bigger vision. Your thesis doesn’t have to solve the whole problem - it just has to produce something useful, actionable, and testable.
- Think of outputs as the **practical deliverables** of your thesis - what stakeholders can actually use to make change happen. Examples include research papers, policy recommendations, datasets, program designs, or workshops.
- Focus on **outputs that link to the stakeholder** it is meant to influence.
   - For example, if your desired outcome is that policymakers understand AI risks, a relevant output might be 2–3 accessible policy briefs co-developed with a think tank.

\#### Info Box: Skills & Resources by Output Type

Different outputs matter for different stakeholders (i.e. an NGO may just want a quick review vs. an in-depth novel study) and they also draw on different skills. Read through the options below and notice which one genuinely excites you, then check it against what you learned during your stakeholder outreach in Weeks 4-5: is this actually the kind of output your stakeholders said they'd want and use? The best output is the one sitting at the intersection of your excitement and their real need!

| Output type | Core skill involved | Relevant stakeholders | Resource |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy brief | Distilling research into a short, decision-ready document for a non-technical audience | Policymakers, regulators, policy think tanks | [UNC Writing Center: Policy Briefs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/policy-briefs/), a concise guide to framing a problem for policy action |
| Systematic literature review /Evidence synthesis | Structured search, screening, and synthesis of existing research | Researchers, funders deciding where to invest, program designers | [LSU Libraries: Steps in a Systematic Review](https://guides.lib.lsu.edu/c.php?g=872965&p=6268540), a clear walkthrough of the search-screen-extract-synthesize process |
| Dataset or survey instrument | Designing a data collection tool that's usable by stakeholders | NGOs or implementers needing a measurement tool, other researchers | [IES: Creating Effective Surveys](https://ies.ed.gov/rel-west/2025/01/handout-creating-effective-surveys-best-practices-survey-design), a practical guide to writing clear, purposeful survey questions |
| Co-designed intervention or workshop | Involving the people affected by a decision in shaping it, rather than designing "for" them alone | Implementers, program managers, the communities the program serves | [A Guide to Selecting Participatory Research Methods](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9258244/), on choosing the right level of stakeholder involvement for your goals |
| Cost-effectiveness analysis | Comparing the cost of an intervention against the good it does, in a shared unit | Funders, grantmakers, charity evaluators, effective giving organisations | [Charity Entrepreneurship: Cost-Effectiveness Analyses](https://www.charityentrepreneurship.com/cea), a practical walkthrough of how they build and use CEAs in practice |
| Case study / qualitative report | Capturing a real-world example in enough depth that others can learn from it | Practitioners and NGOs wanting grounded, narrative evidence; advocates and journalists | [USC Libraries: Writing a Case Study](https://libguides.usc.edu/writingguide/assignments/casestudy), on structuring a case study and choosing a strong subject of analysis |
| Academic paper / peer-reviewed publication | Meeting the methodological standards of a specific field | Academic researchers, journals, future researchers building on your work | [Scribbr: How to Write a Research Paper](https://www.scribbr.com/category/research-paper/), a beginner-friendly walkthrough of structure, sourcing, and drafting |

💡 This list isn't exhaustive but meant to help you ideate on different formats and outputs that can be created beyond just an academic paper!

\### Step 2. Formulate Your Thesis Question ^step-2

**Question:** What research question or project could realistically produce these outputs? Focus on making the question **clear, actionable, and researchable, and link to the outputs you want to create.**

💡 **Tip:** Early stakeholder input is critical. Talking to potential users or collaborators can refine your question, improve feasibility, and strengthen impact.

**A useful framework: FINER.** A well-formed research question is usually:

- **F**easible with the time and resources you actually have,
- **I**nteresting enough to genuinely sustain your motivation,
- **N**ovel in some real way,
- **E**thical to pursue, and
- **R**elevant to the stakeholders and outcomes you've identified

If your question is struggling on one of these, it's often faster to revise the question than to push through the research anyway!

**Three resources worth your time for this step:**

- [CASRAI: How to Write a Research Question](https://casrai.org/guides/how-to-write-a-research-question), a clean, discipline-agnostic guide to narrowing a broad topic into a genuinely answerable question.
- [University of Guelph: Six Steps to Writing a Research Question](https://guides.lib.uoguelph.ca/c.php?g=743978&p=5379960), practical and step-by-step, useful if you want a structured process to follow rather than just principles.
- [Formulating a Good Research Question: Pearls and Pitfalls](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6691636/), a deeper dive into the FINER criteria above, plus common mistakes to watch for.

\### Step 3. Iterate with Stakeholder Feedback and Collaboration ^step-3

💡 **Key Idea:** Your thesis is a working hypothesis - both your outputs and research question may need to evolve based on feedback from the real-world stakeholders you are trying to impact.

- Identify stakeholders who can directly support your outputs or help refine your research question through interviews or feedback, and be prepared to **iterate** on your thesis ideas:
   - Maybe the bottleneck you initially focused on isn’t the highest-leverage point.
   - Perhaps a different output would be more actionable or impactful.
- Consider **co-developing outputs** with stakeholders as a part of your thesis process to ensure that your research has direct relevance to their work.
