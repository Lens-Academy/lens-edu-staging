\## 2. Stakeholder Outreach: From Theory Into the Real World

After completing your stakeholder mapping, the next step is to start reaching out to the people and organizations that can actually move the needle on your research problem. This is about bringing your thesis out of the abstract and into the real world. By connecting with experts, implementers, funders, and organizations, you can validate your research questions, get mentorship, and open doors for collaboration that make your work more impactful.

\### Why reach out to key stakeholders?

*Think of stakeholder interviews not just as a fact-finding mission - they are an opportunity to test assumptions and expand the impact of your thesis through partnerships with people who are already working on a problem you care about.*

- **Test and refine your Theory of Change (ToC):** Stakeholders can challenge or validate specific assumptions in your ToC and can help you know whether your thesis question and planned outputs are actually useful or feasible in practice. *For example,* if your ToC assumes that policymakers will act on evidence from AI safety research, an interview with policymakers or policy think tanks can reveal whether that assumption is realistic or if there are other additional bottlenecks that need to be addressed.
- **Identify opportunities for collaboration or co-development of outputs:** Engaging with stakeholders can help you produce research that is more ambitious and impactful than what you could achieve on your own. For example, partnering with a policy think tank could allow you to co-author policy briefs, boosting both reach and credibility. Similarly, collaborating with academic researchers on a paper could give you access to data, methods, or mentorship that strengthens your thesis and enhances its rigor. By working alongside others already active in the field, your research can become more relevant, actionable, and connected to real-world impact.
- **Work directly with the people your research hopes to reach:** Instead of hoping your findings somehow make it to the right hands, engaging with stakeholders lets you understand their needs firsthand. This ensures your research is relevant, actionable, and directly supportive of the people or organizations you hope to impact.
- **Start making a difference now:** Engaging with stakeholders through your thesis means your impact can begin with your research now, not just in theory. By connecting with the people and organizations your research is meant to help, you can contribute to real-world solutions, test ideas in context, and make meaningful progress while you’re still in the research phase - rather than waiting until your thesis is “finished” or after you’ve graduated.
- **Tap into deep expertise and institutional knowledge on a problem you care about:** Even if you have questions about their current approaches, stakeholders have spent years working on the problem you care about. They know what works, what doesn’t, and why certain strategies succeed or fail. Early interviews can save time by helping you identify the most valuable sources of information and the right next experts to consult - rather than depending on your own research to figure the whole field out!
- **Test your fit and build your ideal career path:** Outreach gives you a chance to explore what working in this field would actually be like. You can experiment with different roles, learn what suits your skills and interests, and start building a network and experiences that align with your long-term goals.

\### Key Recommendations for Outreach

1. **Co-create your research question with the people you hope to impact, rather than only finalising it in isolation and presenting it to them afterward.** One really effective way to tackle uncertainty about both your thesis impact and career is to work directly with people who are actively doing the work in your field.
2. **Frame your first outreach as a 30-45 minute expert interview**, not a request for ongoing help. Many experts are far more receptive to this than you'd expect, since it prioritises learning and relationship-building first, with collaboration following naturally if it makes sense later.
3. **Let the relationship develop naturally afterward**, whether that becomes an informal advisor relationship, occasional feedback, or a more substantial collaboration. This could look like:
   1. **Potential formal/informal advisor for your thesis,** ranging from one-off calls, to email feedback, to more structured mentorship: Once you've established initial contact, these relationships can develop into ongoing advisory arrangements where stakeholders provide guidance on your thesis research.
   2. **Potential collaborator -** providing data to work together, more structured internship opportunities in the future: The most substantial form of engagement involves developing collaborative relationships where you contribute meaningfully to their ongoing work.

\### General Strategies for Effective Outreach

- **Direct Contact:** Reach out to experts or academics via institutional email, typically available on their professional or departmental webpages. : Review authors of key publications, speakers at conferences or webinars, faculty conducting related research, and authors of influential books or reviews. Include early-career researchers, postdoctoral fellows, or graduate students who are actively working on relevant projects.
- **Targeted Points of Contact:** For larger organizations, identify project leads or team members who have directly contributed to work relevant to your thesis, rather than senior executives. These individuals are often more reachable, operationally involved, and invested in the outcomes of their projects.
- **Leverage Junior and Near-Peer Positions:** Consider engaging with individuals a few steps ahead in their careers, such as graduate students, postdoctoral researchers, early-career staff, or program managers. These individuals often:
   - Have practical, hands-on experience in the problem space.
   - They are more accessible and receptive to mentorship or collaboration requests.
   - Can provide highly actionable guidance, introductions, or even co-collaboration opportunities.
   - Offer insights into the day-to-day realities of working in the field, helping you evaluate fit and prepare for future career steps.
- **Show alignment:** Make it clear you know their work, why it’s relevant to your research, and why their perspective matters. Specific references to projects, publications, or initiatives make your outreach stronger.
- **Referrals:** Ask each interviewed expert for recommendations of others you should speak with in order to surface new expert referrals you may have overlooked.

\## 3. The Emotional Barriers of Outreach

We wanted to share that for most people, the hardest part of this week isn't finding stakeholders, it's actually hitting send! Oftentimes, a voice may come up, like “*who am I to be emailing this person*, or *they're going to know how unqualified I am”*. And you’re definitely not alone in experiencing imposter syndrome - we all experience it in different degrees and contexts!

A few things we wanted to share to encourage you when this comes up:

- **This feeling is so common, universal, and NOT a sign you're failing!** Luisa Rodriguez, a researcher at Rethink Priorities, has [written really openly](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/gp94EeYgbh5qjfu65/my-experience-with-imposter-syndrome-and-how-to-partly) about feeling this even years into her career, including around this exact kind of outreach. If people deep into their careers still feel it, that tells you something, the feeling just doesn't track reality very well.
- **The experts you're nervous about were once exactly where you are.** Most people you'll reach out to remember being a student cold-emailing someone senior, and many genuinely enjoy being that person for someone else now. You are also giving them the gift of being able to share the work they spend their lives on!
- **People are often way more receptive than you'd expect.** And the people who do reply are almost always genuinely kind about it - most people love being asked about their work.
- **A "no" or silence really isn't a judgement on your worth or value**. Oftentimes, this might mean a busy week or a full inbox, not a judgement on whether your question or your work matters. And **rejection can be reframed as realignment to opportunities and people who are more aligned with your path!**
- **You don't need permission to be curious.** Wanting to learn from someone's work is itself a beautiful and good enough reason to reach out!
- **And honestly, you're on the same team!** You and the person you’re reaching out to both want to see this problem taken seriously, and that shared mission is real common ground that you can build on.

We also recommend grounding and reminding yourself before sending a batch of emails, on your mission, and on what's actually driving you to do this work in the first place. Oftentimes, our nervousness is just a sign for how deeply we care about this going well, and we can tap into being grateful that we have this intention for the world to be a better place!
