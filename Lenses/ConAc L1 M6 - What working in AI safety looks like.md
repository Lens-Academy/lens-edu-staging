She enjoyed writing, research and talking to people, and understood AI safety to be a field that needed technical talent and money. She went to graduate school to study medieval history. In [AI safety needs more than engineers](https://80000hours.org/2026/04/ai-safety-needs-more-than-engineers/), she reflects on how limited her initial understanding of the field was.

Moneer, a former school principal, took a different route. During his career transition, he provided executive coaching to the director of an AI safety organisation, evaluated the quality of BlueDot's course facilitation and applied his background in sociology to coordination problems within the field. He eventually secured a full-time position in AI safety. He describes his experience in [My journey from School Principal to AI Safety Advisor](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/oTCzW6eAFZ6LH6K4E/).

Lucie Philippon came from software engineering but ended up organising a high-level international AI policy event. Her previous involvement in a French AI safety organisation, where she had helped with everything from proofreading documents to developing organisational strategy, led to a personal recommendation for the position. \### What kinds of work are there?

There is no established classification of AI safety professions. The categories below give you a rough map of the kinds of work you will encounter when browsing job boards:

- **Research.** Finding answers to questions whose answers we need. This could mean investigating how neural networks represent information, developing mathematical theories of agency or studying the feasibility of international AI agreements.
- **Engineering.** Building things that researchers or other practitioners need: experimental infrastructure, evaluation frameworks, monitoring systems, security mechanisms and specialised software.
- **Policy and institutional work.** Finding ways to influence how AI is developed and governed. This includes drafting legislation, advising governments, developing technical standards and helping institutions implement safety measures.
- **Fieldbuilding.** Increasing the number and effectiveness of people working on AI safety. If researchers struggle to find qualified collaborators, for example, someone needs to develop training programmes, identify promising candidates and create opportunities for them to work together.
- **Grantmaking.** Figuring out which people, projects and organisations should receive funding. This can involve considerable research of its own, as well as actively looking for people who could undertake work that currently nobody is doing.
- **Communications and advocacy.** Helping particular audiences understand AI-related problems and act on that understanding. Depending on the audience, this could involve scientific writing, journalism, public campaigns or communication with industry leaders.
- **Management and operations.** Making organisations capable of achieving their objectives. This includes helping researchers choose and complete projects, hiring people, resolving organisational problems, managing finances and developing strategy.

These categories are useful for orientation, but the boundaries between them are porous. Many positions in AI safety are essentially **generalist roles: take responsibility for an objective and do whatever needs to be done to achieve it.**

The smaller the organisation, the more likely you are to encounter this kind of work. In a five-person organisation, the same person might interview researchers, write a grant proposal, organise a workshop and develop the strategy for a new project, all within a single week.

For some people, this variety is a substantial part of the appeal of working in AI safety.

\### How to discover career opportunities

Even if you are nowhere near ready to apply for a job, we recommend regularly browsing the [80,000 Hours Job Board](https://jobs.80000hours.org/) and [AISafety.com](https://aisafety.com/jobs). Look at vacancies outside your current specialisation, read the responsibilities and requirements, and try to imagine what a week in each position would look like.

Keep in mind that job boards reveal only part of the professional landscape. In [What 3,654 Job Postings Tell Us About Talent Needs in AI Safety](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/AAGD34wyWHXm5d2gp/), Weronika Mazurek notes that closed hiring rounds are common and that senior and leadership positions may be substantially underrepresented in publicly advertised vacancies.

Some opportunities emerge through personal recommendations, existing collaborations or conversations about problems an organisation is struggling to solve. Others begin as small independent projects and eventually turn into paid positions.

This is another reason to follow the work of organisations, attend events and talk to people in the field. Alongside discovering existing vacancies, you will gradually learn what kinds of work organisations need done, including work for which a formal position has yet to be created.
