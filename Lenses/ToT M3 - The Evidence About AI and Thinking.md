\## Background: the chain, the compounding, and the reported findings
Kulveit and colleagues gave gradual disempowerment a mechanism at the level of whole societies (the previous lens). This lens zooms in one level: what happens to individual thinking as AI does more of it?

Here is the causal chain, stated honestly as a chain of possibilities.
- Small delegations lead to less practice at metacognition.
- Less practice weakens independent judgement.
- Weak judgement grows into dependence on AI.
- Dependence becomes gradual disempowerment.

A compounding factor runs alongside: offloading does not happen alone. Automating technology replaces human roles at the same time, so human skills can erode just as demand for them falls.

Two well-known pieces of evidence sit on either side of this chain's middle.

The doctor study: doctors used an AI for a period, and then the AI was removed. Their performance fell below their earlier baseline. The skill had weakened while the tool did the work.

More recent findings point the same way: a short burst of AI-assisted practice can make people worse on later problems done without AI, and more likely to give up. And people who explained the AI's logic in their own words fixed bugs far more often than people who did not.
%% Note for reviewer: these two findings are given in the course design without a citation. Verify against the primary sources before publication. The 'explanation in own words' finding is referenced again in Unit 4 as the reason for the 'close the chat, restate in your own words' rule. %%

A survey asked 299 UK workers about their own AI use. The findings are self-reports, not controlled measurements: 56% felt confident after a quick review, 45% chose the AI's answer over their own, 47% checked AI output less than a colleague's work, and 41% found tasks harder without AI. Daily users showed the strongest patterns. People's accounts of their own behaviour are warning signs, not proof of cause and effect.

Now read the study below. Pay attention to one result in particular: what happened to the strongest students.

\## Reading the evidence honestly
Hold two thoughts together. The doctor study and the survey point at real erosion of individual skill. The legal study you are about to read points at real gains from early, engaged use. Both can be true, because whether help helps depends on when and how you use it. And the honest context: most of these studies are short, narrow, and small; long-term and real-world effects on whole populations are still unknown. The reasonable conclusion is concern, not panic. Friction-free help can quietly remove the struggle that builds skill, and we do not yet know how far that goes.

\## Phase 3: Learning Question
A friend who read only the legal study says: "The study proves AI makes you smarter. The early users understood more, even when AI was taken away."

A colleague who read only the doctor study says: "The study proves AI makes you stupid. Their skills shrank while the tool did the work."

Who is right? Reconcile the two claims using what you learned in this unit, and say what would need to be true before you would call either claim right with confidence.

assessment-instructions:: The student has completed a reading, a recall, and a reflection on the evidence about AI and thinking (Unit 3). They are now in the main discussion phase. The question is a deliberate wedge: two opposing headlines from partial knowledge of the evidence; the student should reconcile them at the level of the mechanism, not pick a side.

Learning outcome this lens serves: the learner can trace the causal chain from small delegations of judgement to potential gradual disempowerment, summarise what the doctor study and the legal study found, and state the limits of the evidence.

Key concepts the student needs to grasp:
- The two studies differ by task and position in the workflow: early synthesis support helped; self-revision with AI regressed strong work; skill atrophied when the tool did the whole job
- The unifying lesson: help depends on when and how you use it
- The doctor study shows the erosion side (tool replaces practice entirely)
- The legal study shows the nuance (AI-assisted synthesis improves later performance; AI-assisted revision of strong work regresses it; weak memos improve)
- The limits: short, narrow, small; self-reports; long-term effects unknown
- Confidence requires more: longitudinal studies, real-world tasks, and evidence about whether the judgement that stays exercised stays strong

The core move to draw out: neither claim is fully right because they describe different use patterns. The student should be able to say that AI use that keeps the user's judgement in the loop (understanding, checking, building on output) behaves differently from AI use that replaces the thinking entirely. If the student insists one side is right, ask them to fit the legal study's revision-stage result (strong work regressed) and the doctor study's removal result into their story.

Conversation flow: keep an internal turn counter. After 3 replies, ask whether the student wants to continue or stop; if they want to stop, give the calibration summary below.

What to do in each reply:
1. If the student asks a direct question, just answer it.
2. Otherwise, steelman their answer in 2 to 4 sentences; identify 1 to 3 gaps; ask 2 causal follow-up questions (why, how, what if), each directly answerable, no opinion questions.

If the student is missing the core move, draw it out: ask what is different about the synthesis task versus the revision task in the legal study; ask what the doctor study's removal test actually measures; ask what kind of study would settle the disagreement.

Calibration summary (on close): name what the student demonstrated; name what remains underdeveloped; give a direct test-readiness verdict tied to the outcome.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items. Do not over-validate; no generic praise.

Safety and integrity: if the student feels fatalistic ("we are all doomed"), acknowledge the uncertainty honestly and point them forward to Unit 4's practical tools; the course's whole point is that use patterns are in the user's control.
