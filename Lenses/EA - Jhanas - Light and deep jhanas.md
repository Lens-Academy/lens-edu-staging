#### Text
content::
Teachers who say they teach "the jhanas" do not all mean the same states. The disagreement goes back to two kinds of source. The **suttas** are the discourses attributed to the Buddha, like the two you just read. The **Visuddhimagga** ("Path of Purification") is a meditation manual written by the commentator Buddhaghosa around 500 CE, which describes jhana as a much deeper absorption ([Sparby and Sacchet, 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-024-02367-w)). A **nimitta** is a mental image, usually of light, that appears in deep concentration and that the Visuddhimagga style uses as the object to absorb into (same source).

First, Leigh Brasington's own survey of how different teachers interpret the jhanas. He teaches the lighter style himself and calls his list "highly subjective".

#### Text
content::
For the deep camp in its own words: Ajahn Brahm, a Theravada monk in Western Australia, writes that within jhana "all the five senses are totally shut down. One cannot see, one cannot hear, one cannot smell, taste nor feel touch", and that there is no thought, no decision-making and no sense of time, only "bliss that doesn't move" ([Ajahn Brahmavamso, *The Jhanas*, free booklet](http://dhammatalks.net/Books/Ajahn_Brahm_The_Jhanas.pdf)). Brasington places him close to the Visuddhimagga depth, though Brahm says he teaches from the suttas and his own experience ([Brasington](https://www.leighb.com/jhanantp.htm)).

Next, two researchers, the philosopher Terje Sparby ([TIME](https://time.com/7007856/jhourney-meditation-jhanas-retreat-bliss/)) and Matthew Sacchet of the Meditation Research Program at Harvard Medical School, compared eight modern jhana manuals and proposed a common vocabulary. In what follows, "ACAM" is their term for advanced concentrative absorption meditation, and "pīti" is the rapture you met in the suttas.

Without looking back, write down what you remember about how the light and deep styles of jhana differ.
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Brasington's "Interpretations of the Jhanas", a short quote from Ajahn Brahm's "The Jhanas" and parts of Sparby and Sacchet 2024, and wrote a free recall about how light and deep styles differ.

Key points:
- Two camps (Brasington): Visuddhimagga style uses a nimitta (a mental image, usually of light) and very deep concentration; the Visuddhimagga implies at best one in a million who try reach absorption. Sutta style needs no nimitta and is accessible to many people. Teachers tend to call weaker methods "not real jhana" and stronger ones "indulging". Brasington concludes we do not know exactly what the Buddha practised, and that the Buddha treated jhana as a tool for insight.
- Ajahn Brahm (deep camp): in jhana there is no thought, no decision-making, no sense of time, only unmoving bliss, and all five senses are shut off.
- Sparby and Sacchet: the manuals overlap but disagree, for example on whether thought can be present. Three types: light (stable attention, joy after a few minutes), intermediate (attention locks on, factors present then fade, the body still present), deep (full absorption into the nimitta; external perception, thinking and time disappear). "Sutta jhana" and "Visuddhimagga jhana" roughly match intermediate and deep. Differences are largest at the first jhana and smaller by the fourth.

content::
A friend reached what her teacher called the first jhana on a week-long retreat: waves of tingling joy, and she could still hear the birds outside. A monk tells her it cannot have been jhana, because in jhana the five senses shut off. She concludes that either she or the monk must be wrong about what she experienced.

Must one of them be wrong? Explain.
feedback-instructions:: The learner was given a plausible but flawed conclusion: a friend had what her teacher called the first jhana (tingling joy, could still hear birds), a monk says it cannot be jhana because in jhana the senses shut off, so one of them must be wrong about her experience. This is practice for the test at the end of the next part of the module.

Points a strong answer finds (any two are good):
- They are not disagreeing about her experience. They use the word "jhana" for different states: hers fits light or intermediate (sutta-style) jhana, the monk's definition fits deep (Visuddhimagga-style) jhana. Both can be right about what they mean.
- The disagreement is about a definition, which the texts do not settle: Brasington says we do not know exactly what the Buddha practised, and teachers tend to dismiss lighter methods as not real.
- Sparby and Sacchet's three types give a way to describe her state without settling who owns the word.
- What could still be checked: whether her state had the features of the first jhana in the suttas (rapture and pleasure filling the body), and whether either style leads to what the teachers say it leads to.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in one or two sentences, name one gap, and ask one direct follow-up question (for example: "Which of Sparby and Sacchet's three types would her state fit, and which would the monk's definition fit?"). 100 to 160 words. After 2 replies, close with a short summary of what they found. If the learner is stuck after two attempts, give the answer briefly and move on. No generic praise.
