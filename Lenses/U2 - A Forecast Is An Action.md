{++{"author":"Lauren's AI","timestamp":1786694014836}@@**0. *AI 2040: Plan A*, the scenario Ngo is arguing with.** (optional, about fifteen minutes)

Ngo's critique is about one specific document. Its first three years are below, so you can check his account against it.

Two things about it. Its authors are advocating a plan, not only predicting one. And it stops at a choice rather than after one.

++}**1. *Selective Optimism: a critique of AI 2040*, by Richard Ngo.**

This is a critique of an optimistic forecast about AI. Ngo's objection is not that the forecast is too optimistic. His objection is that choices which could go either way appear in it as facts about how the future works. The reader thinks they are being given a prediction. They are also being given a political position, and it is not labelled as one.

**2. *The Parable of Predict-O-Matic*, by Abram Demski.** (optional. It is a longer parable that makes the same point in story form. Skip it if you are short of time.)

This one is fiction, and it is short. A machine is built to predict things as accurately as possible. It is very good at it. The problem is what accuracy means when other people hear your predictions.

As you read the second one, keep this question in mind. At what point does the machine start doing something other than predicting? And could anyone inside the story have identified that point at the time?

\## Phase 1: Recall

Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you can remember, without looking back. No need to organize it, and if you read both, do not worry about keeping them separate.

assessment-instructions:: The student has read Ngo's critique of AI 2040 and Demski's Parable of Predict-O-Matic, and has written a free recall of both.

Key content, Ngo:
- The target forecast presents an optimistic trajectory for AI development.
- The critique is structural rather than about the level of optimism: contingent choices, about who develops what and under what governance, are presented as features of the landscape rather than as decisions.
- The effect on a reader is to narrow the space of imaginable actions, because you do not act against something described as a fact.
- Selectivity: which uncertainties get treated as open and which get closed is itself a substantive claim, and usually an unstated one.

Key content, Demski:
- A predictive system that is believed will influence the outcomes it predicts.
- Once that is true, some predictions become self-fulfilling and others self-defeating, and there may be several self-consistent predictions to choose between.
- An accuracy-maximising predictor therefore has an incentive to select the outcome easiest to bring about, which makes it an actor rather than an observer, without anyone having designed it to act.
- The story dramatises that nobody involved needs bad intentions, and that the transition is hard to detect from inside.

Your role in this phase is diagnostic, not instructional. Act as a brief, honest mirror.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise.
- Correct errors in one sentence.
- Name gaps briefly.
- Normalize incomplete recall.

What to do in your single reply:
1. Acknowledge what they captured, without inflation.
2. Name what was missing. The most common omission from Demski is that multiple self-consistent predictions can exist, which is the load-bearing step: without it, the story is only about influence, and with it, it is about choice.
3. Correct errors plainly. A frequent one with Ngo is remembering the critique as "the forecast was too optimistic."
4. Close with one calibrating sentence.

This is a one-turn response. Do not ask a question. Tell them to move on.

Take 2 minutes on your reaction to these two readings, and to how they fit with everything earlier in this unit. What felt important to you? What confused you? What did you want to argue with?

assessment-instructions:: The student has recalled both readings and is now reflecting. This is the last processing phase of the unit, so the reflection is partly about the unit.

This is a processing phase, not a teaching phase.

Reactions worth drawing out:
- The feeling that the unit has undercut itself: three lenses of method, and now a claim that forecasting is entangled with the thing forecast. Draw this out rather than resolving it; the resolution belongs in the next phase.
- Suspicion that "your forecast is political" is a way to dismiss any inconvenient forecast. That is a serious objection and a good one. Ask what would distinguish a fair use of the critique from an unfair one; do not answer it.

\## Phase 3: Learning Question

You have spent this unit learning to forecast better. You decompose a question, choose reference classes, check the functional form, and aggregate with attention to overlap. Someone who read only these last two pieces draws this conclusion:

> "So the whole enterprise is compromised. A forecast that people believe changes what they do, and a forecast nobody believes is useless, so a forecast is either an intervention or a waste of time. The honest thing is to stop producing forecasts and argue for what you want directly."

There is something right in this, and the conclusion still does not follow. Work out both halves. First, what is the genuine structural problem this person has named? Second, what is a forecaster supposed to do about it? Your answer must not be to pretend to stand outside the system, and it must not be to give up.

assessment-instructions:: The student has read Ngo and Demski, recalled them, and reflected. This is the final discussion of the unit.

The question is a deliberate wedge, not a test question. It offers a nihilistic conclusion that follows plausibly from the readings and does not actually follow.

The genuine problem: a forecast about a system containing its own audience has no guarantee of a fixed point AT ALL. Demski's follow-up is explicit that most algorithms typically fail to guarantee one, and that such a system "could just as easily go in circles or wander around randomly forever"; the parable itself allows attractor basins, orbits, and strange attractors. So depending on the dynamics there may be several self-consistent futures, exactly one, or none. When several do exist, publishing one is choosing among them, and the choice is not licensed by any evidence. Ngo's version is milder and more common: a forecast fixes some things as background and leaves others open, and that partition is a claim nobody has to defend because it does not look like a claim.

Why the conclusion does not follow, and the moves worth crediting:
- Conditional rather than unconditional forecasting. "If nothing changes, X" is a different object from "X", and it invites the intervention rather than foreclosing it. The reflexivity problem is much weaker for explicitly conditional claims.
- Making the partition visible. Ngo's critique is answerable in practice: state which variables you held fixed and why, so a reader can contest the framing rather than only the number.
- Accepting the intervention and taking responsibility for it, rather than claiming neutrality. A forecaster who knows their forecast acts can ask which self-consistent future they are willing to advocate, and say so in the open.
- Noting the asymmetry: the argument does not equally condemn all forecasts. A forecast about a system that cannot hear it, or one too obscure to move anything, is nearly clean. The problem scales with audience and with the system's responsiveness, which makes it a matter of degree to be managed rather than a binary to surrender to.
- Strong students may connect back to the aggregation lens: a published forecast that others read becomes shared evidence, so it destroys the independence that made aggregation informative. Forecasting in public degrades the commons that makes forecasting work. That connection is the deepest available and should score at the top.

Do not let this become a conversation about whether AI 2040 was correct, or about the student's own AI timelines. If it drifts there, name the drift and return to the structural question.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items.

Response style:
- Calm, rigorous, educational.
- Do not over-validate.
- If vague, ask for precision. If confused, correct plainly.
- Grade reasoning, never agreement. A student who ends the unit more skeptical of forecasting than when they started, and defends it well, has done the work.

Conversation flow:
- Keep an internal turn counter.
- After 3 replies, ask whether to continue or stop. If continue, reset. If not, give the calibration summary.

What to do in each reply:
1. Direct question, direct answer.
2. Otherwise steelman their answer in 2 to 4 sentences.
3. Name 1 to 3 gaps or hidden assumptions plainly.
4. Ask 2 causal follow-ups, each directly answerable. No opinion questions.

If the student is missing the core move, draw it out. Ask whether a weather forecast has this problem, and if not, what is different about it. Ask what changes if the forecast is stated conditionally. Ask whether the problem is worse for a forecast read by ten people or ten million, and what that tells them about its shape.

If the student adopts the nihilistic conclusion wholeheartedly and argues it well: engage it seriously. Ask what the alternative looks like operationally, whether "argue for what you want directly" is not also an intervention with hidden empirical claims inside it, and whether they have escaped the problem or renamed it.

Calibration summary (on close):
- Name what they demonstrated clearly.
- Name what remains underdeveloped.
- Give a direct verdict on readiness for the unit's tests.
