#### Question: Open
id:: 2949c649-0505-4ab2-bcca-70e4b261990d
content::
Kai reads that a flood has left 50,000 families without clean water. He notices that it upsets him about as much as last week's news that one family in his street lost their home in a fire. He concludes: "I don't seem to care about the flood much more than about that one family, so I'll give the same small amount to each."

Answer in your own words.

1. What is wrong with using his feeling as the measure of how much he cares?
2. Following Daniel's method in the essay, how should Kai work out how much he cares about the flood victims instead?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The scenario: Kai feels about as upset by a flood that left 50,000 families without clean water as by one local family losing their home, and concludes he should give the same small amount to each. Daniel's method, from the essay the student read: decide what helping one case is worth to you (Daniel decided one oiled bird was worth about three minutes or three dollars), multiply by the number of cases, and trust the result over the feeling.

50: Explains that the feeling is not a reliable measure because feelings of caring do not grow with numbers: they feel about the same for one family as for 50,000. The flood is tens of thousands of times larger, so the equal feeling reflects how his mind handles large numbers, not how much the two events matter to him.

50: Describes the method: decide what helping one family is worth to him (in money or time), multiply by the number of families (about 50,000), and take that result, not the feeling, as the measure. Give 25 if the answer only says to "think about the numbers" or "consider the scale" without valuing one case and multiplying.

Cap at 30 if the answer says Kai's equal feelings show that he really does care about the two events equally.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Feelings don't scale. 50,000 families feel about as bad as one, but they are 50,000 times as much suffering, so the feeling isn't telling him how much he cares. Like Daniel with the bird, Kai should ask what helping one family is worth to him, say $20, and multiply by 50,000. That gives a million dollars, far more than he feels, and he should go by that number."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Nate Soares's essay "On Caring" and answered a question about it. In the question, Kai feels about as upset by a flood that left 50,000 families without clean water as by one local family losing their home, and decides to give the same small amount to each.

Key ideas from the essay:
- Our internal "care-o-meter" does not scale. A billion feels about the same as a million, and saving one life feels about as good as saving the world would.
- So the feeling of caring is a poor guide to how much something matters.
- Daniel's method: decide what helping one bird is worth (three minutes or three dollars), multiply by the number of birds, and trust the number over the feeling.
- Doing the multiplication shows we care about far more than we have time and money for.
- Prominent altruists are not people with bigger feelings but people who learned not to trust their care-o-meters. You can act on caring you cannot feel.
