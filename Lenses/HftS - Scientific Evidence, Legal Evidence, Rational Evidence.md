title: "Scientific Evidence, Legal Evidence, Rational Evidence"
tldr: "Anything that makes a claim more or less likely is evidence for a rational person. Courts and science accept only narrower kinds, by choice, so 'there is no scientific evidence' does not mean 'there is no reason to believe it'."
summary_for_tutor: "Yudkowsky distinguishes rational evidence (anything that shifts the probability of a claim) from legal evidence and scientific evidence, which are narrower categories with extra standards chosen for social reasons. Examples: the police commissioner who privately tells you Wulky Wilkinsen is a crime boss (strong rational evidence, not legal evidence, because jailing people on such statements would invite abuse), the author's white socks (rational and legal evidence, not scientific, because no one can rerun it), Alexander the Great (historical, relies on authorities like Plutarch). Science is the publicly reproducible knowledge of humankind, made of generalizations that anyone can test, and its standards (such as p < 0.05) are pragmatic choices. The question gives four friends who all fell sick after one shared restaurant meal, and one says that without scientific evidence they have no reason to blame the restaurant. The student explains the flaw, then why their experience is not scientific evidence."

#### Question: Open
id:: ca19e575-06d8-4f19-8ad0-425dd3896a0f
content::
Four friends shared a meal at a restaurant. All four were sick that night, and none of them ate anything else that day. One of them says: "There's no scientific evidence that the restaurant made us sick, so we have no reason to think it did."

1. What is wrong with his reasoning, and why is their experience still a reason to suspect the restaurant?

2. Why doesn't their experience count as scientific evidence, according to the essay?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Background for the grader: the essay says rational evidence is anything that makes a claim more or less likely. Legal evidence and scientific evidence are narrower categories with extra standards that society chooses for its own purposes. Science is made of publicly reproducible generalizations that anyone can test for themselves, so a one-time event cannot be scientific knowledge, yet it can still be strong rational evidence. Score out of 100. 50 (part 1): says that having no scientific evidence is not the same as having no evidence: the shared sickness is still real evidence that the restaurant made them sick, because four people falling sick after the same meal is much more likely if the food was bad than if it was not. 50 (part 2): says science accepts only publicly reproducible generalizations that anyone can test for themselves (or: science sets extra standards beyond what makes a claim more likely), and a single past meal is a one-time event that no one can rerun or test, so it cannot meet that standard. Cap at 40 if the answer agrees they have no reason to suspect the restaurant. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "He's treating scientific evidence as the only kind. Four people getting sick after the same meal is far more likely if the food was bad, so it's strong evidence. It just isn't scientific evidence, because science deals in reproducible generalizations that anyone can test, and a one-off dinner can never be rerun."
