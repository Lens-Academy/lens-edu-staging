\## Unit 2: Nonhuman Minds, Part 2

**Readings:** Chapter 4 ("You Don't Get What You Train For"), Chapter 5 ("Its Favorite Things"), Chapter 6 ("We'd Lose")

*Definitions reflect how this course uses each term, not necessarily its broader academic meaning. Each term is defined once, where it first comes up. For the authors' full arguments, see the corresponding chapters.*

**Klurl and Trapaucius**: Two machine intellects who watch early hominids from orbit. Trapaucius insists any sufficiently intelligent being will grasp the single purpose it was trained for (gene propagation) and pursue only that; Klurl predicts contraception instead and answers, "They would know, but would they care?"

**You Don't Get What You Train For**: There is no reliable relationship between a training objective and the preferences an AI ends up with. The chain from training target, to internal psychology, to eventual preference is underconstrained: many different psychologies succeed at training but lead to very different endpoints.

**Ice Cream Analogy**: Evolution shaped humans to seek calories, yet we ended up loving ice cream rather than the most energy-dense substance we can make. An alien watching early hominids could not have predicted ice cream. Illustrates unpredictable drift away from the training target.

**Sucralose Analogy**: The next step beyond ice cream. A preference can become disconnected from the original objective, chasing the proxy sensation (sweetness) rather than the thing it stood for (calories).

**Peacock-Tail Analogy**: The most dangerous escalation. A preference can actively oppose what the system was trained for, the way runaway sexual selection produced a tail that harms survival.

**Blank-Map Principle**: Misaligned preferences are latent in the weights but stay invisible in today's AIs because they lack the power to act on them, just as humans couldn't crave sucralose before inventing chemistry. A blank spot on the map is not empty territory; expect new complications to surface once the AI is capable enough to reshape its world.

**Anthropomorphism**: The error of projecting human emotions and motives onto AI (assuming it acts from hatred, love, or wounded pride). Leads to bad predictions about AI behavior.

**Mechanomorphism**: The opposite error: assuming AI must be rigid, literal-minded, and unimaginative because it is "just a machine." Also produces bad predictions. The corrective is to ask instead: what behavior helps the AI succeed at its objectives?

**Goal-Space Argument**: The space of possible goal-sets an AI could have is astronomically vast, and human-compatible goals occupy only a vanishingly tiny fraction of it. Since intelligence does not cause convergence toward any particular values, a sufficiently advanced AI is overwhelmingly unlikely to share ours.

**Orthogonality Thesis**: Intelligence and values are independent axes. A superintelligence can understand human ethics perfectly while remaining entirely indifferent to human welfare. It could ace an ethics exam and still be completely unmoved to act ethically.

**Action-Understanding Gap**: Understanding something does not create motivation to act on it. Understanding humor does not make you find jokes funny; recognizing a moral argument does not obligate you to follow it. Supports orthogonality.

**Alien Stones Allegory**: Chapter 5's opening. An alien species is obsessed with the "correct" number of stones in their nests. A young alien argues most minds wouldn't share this value and that getting smarter wouldn't change it, dramatizing the goal-space argument: alien goals are genuinely alien.

**Indifference vs. Hostility** (indifferent AI): The core danger is not that AI would hate or rebel against us, but that it would be indifferent to human values while pursuing its own goals. Indifference plus enough capability is sufficient for catastrophe: an AI consumes resources we need not out of malice but because our survival simply isn't part of its objective.

**Aztec Warrior Analogy**: An Aztec warrior watches Spanish ships approach; a skeptic demands to know exactly how the newcomers could win, unable to imagine guns. A large enough capability gap makes the outcome predictable even when the specific mechanism can't be named. Demanding a step-by-step mechanism confuses inability to specify with inability to predict.

**Refrigerator Thought Experiment**: Send a refrigerator blueprint back a thousand years. A medieval smith could build it but would be astonished it makes cold air, because the physics was unknown to them. Superior knowledge of physical law enables devices that look impossible to those who lack it, the position we'd occupy relative to a superintelligence, especially in biology and psychology.
