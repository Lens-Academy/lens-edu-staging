title: "Absence of Evidence Is Evidence of Absence"
tldr: "If finding something would raise your belief, then not finding it must lower it. How much it lowers your belief depends on how likely you were to find it if it were there."
summary_for_tutor: "Yudkowsky starts from Earl Warren's 1942 claim that the lack of sabotage by Japanese-Americans was the most ominous sign of a coming Fifth Column. A Fifth Column might delay sabotage, but no sabotage is still more likely if there is no Fifth Column, so observing none lowers its probability. Absence of proof is not proof of absence, but absence of evidence is always evidence of absence. How strong it is depends on how reliably the cause would produce the sign: gaps in the fossil record are weak evidence because fossils form rarely. A model's strength is what it cannot explain. The question gives a friend who glanced in the kitchen for her keys, did not see them, and concludes the search tells her nothing. The student explains what is wrong and how a five-second glance differs from emptying every drawer."

#### Question: Open
id:: f0fd33a8-cd2f-4065-9991-4e38e54a18d4
content::
Your friend looks in the kitchen for her keys and doesn't see them. She says: "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, so this tells me nothing about whether my keys are in the kitchen."

1. What is wrong with her conclusion?

2. How would it change what the failed search tells her if she had only glanced around for five seconds, compared with emptying every drawer?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Background for the grader: the essay says that if seeing something would raise the probability of a hypothesis, then not seeing it must lower that probability. Absence of proof is not proof of absence, but absence of evidence is evidence of absence. How strong it is depends on how likely the sign was to show up if the hypothesis were true. Score out of 100. 50: says not finding the keys is evidence that they are not in the kitchen (it lowers the probability), because she was more likely to see them if they were there than if they were not. It need not prove they are absent. 50: says how much the failed search tells her depends on how likely the search was to find the keys if they were there: a quick glance often misses keys, so failing lowers the probability only a little, while emptying every drawer would almost surely find them, so failing is strong evidence they are not in the kitchen. Cap at 30 if the answer agrees that the failed search tells her nothing. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Not seeing them is some evidence they're not there, since she'd be more likely to spot them if they were. It doesn't prove it. A five-second glance often misses keys, so it only lowers the chance a little. If she emptied every drawer and still found nothing, that would be strong evidence they're somewhere else."
