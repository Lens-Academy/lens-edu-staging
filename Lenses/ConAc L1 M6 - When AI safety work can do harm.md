#### Text
content::
Suppose you receive a job offer from a frontier AI company. The position would give you access to experienced researchers, substantial computational resources and first-hand knowledge of how advanced AI systems are developed. Some of your work, however, would contribute to making these systems more capable.

Would accepting the offer help reduce AI risk?

In [Anonymous advice: If you want to reduce AI risk, should you take roles that advance AI capabilities?](https://80000hours.org/articles/ai-capabilities/), 80,000 Hours asked several people working on AI risk to address this question. Their answers differ considerably. Some believe that the skills and professional relationships acquired in such a position could enable much more valuable safety work later. Others argue that accelerating AI development before adequate safety measures are available is too serious a risk to justify on these grounds.

You will encounter similar dilemmas even when working explicitly on AI safety:

- **Technical research.** A method developed to understand or control AI systems might also make it easier to build more capable ones. Should you pursue this research? Should you publish all of its results?
- **Evaluations.** An evaluation intended to identify dangerous capabilities might give developers and regulators unwarranted confidence in a system if they misunderstand what a negative result establishes.
- **Governance.** A regulatory proposal might introduce substantial compliance costs while doing little to reduce the risks it was designed to address.
- **Fieldbuilding.** A successful educational programme might attract hundreds of talented people into the field. Its impact will depend on what those people subsequently do. As Aaron Gertler discusses in [Building the Field of AI Safety](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/dwLLSnBcPXga2aasW/building-the-field-of-ai-safety), fieldbuilders can inadvertently steer people towards harmful work or away from more valuable opportunities.

Notice that in each of these examples, the intervention could succeed on its own terms and still have undesirable consequences. The research produces interesting findings, the evaluation gets adopted, the legislation passes, the educational programme attracts participants.

This is where the distinction between outputs, outcomes and impact, introduced earlier in the course, becomes especially useful.

When evaluating a project or a job offer, try to construct two plausible stories: one in which your work contributes to the future you want, and another in which it makes that future less likely. What assumptions distinguish these stories? Can you investigate them before committing to the work? Could you change the project to preserve its benefits while reducing its risks?

For example, before accepting a safety position at an AI company, you might want to investigate whether the safety team can influence deployment decisions, what happens when its findings conflict with commercial objectives, and whether you would have control over how your research is used.

These are reasonable questions to ask during a job interview.
