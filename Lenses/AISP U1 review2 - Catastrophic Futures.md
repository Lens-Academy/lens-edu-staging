\## What are we actually trying to prevent?

When people first encounter existential risk, the natural picture is extinction. Humanity exists, something terrible happens, and then humanity does not exist. That case is clear enough that it can hide a more difficult question: what property of extinction makes it especially important? Is it the number of deaths? Is it that no one is left to recover? Is it the loss of all future generations? Is it the loss of humanity's ability to learn, change its values, and try again?

A useful definition focuses on outcomes that either cause the premature extinction of Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically destroy its potential for desirable future development.[^cite-bostrom-2013] This includes extinction, but it also includes futures in which people survive while something important becomes unrecoverable. Once that broader definition is on the table, the word *existential* stops being a synonym for "very large". It points toward permanence.

Consider two catastrophes. In the first, 99 percent of humanity dies, but the survivors eventually rebuild. In the second, everyone dies. The immediate difference is one percentage point of the population. The long-run difference can be far larger. In the first world, later generations can exist, institutions can recover, new knowledge can be created, and mistakes can be corrected. In the second world, none of those possibilities remain.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

This does not prove that extinction should dominate every other moral concern. The comparison depends on what we value about the future. It does show why counting immediate deaths can miss part of the harm. Two outcomes can have similar short-run severity and very different long-run consequences.

\## Why irreversibility changes the decision

Most ordinary disasters leave some path to correction. A failed policy can be repealed. A damaged institution can be rebuilt. A mistaken scientific belief can be revised. Even very large wars and pandemics leave later people who can learn from what happened. The possibility of recovery does not make those events acceptable. It changes the structure of the decision because some errors can be corrected after they occur.

Irreversible failures are different. Once the relevant option disappears, later evidence cannot bring it back. This is one reason safety decisions often care about preserving options. If a choice can be delayed while information improves, the future decision-maker may be in a better position. If the choice permanently removes the ability to change course, the evidential standard can reasonably be higher.

There are several ways that future options can disappear. Extinction removes every human option. Permanent political disempowerment can leave humans alive while removing meaningful influence over the institutions that determine what happens next. A stable value lock-in can preserve prosperity while freezing a moral or political order that later generations would reject. A sufficiently deep civilizational collapse can leave no realistic path back to advanced technological society. These outcomes are morally different, but they share a common feature: later people cannot simply notice the mistake and reverse it.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

This is also why a catastrophe can be existential even if daily life looks tolerable for some time. A system can preserve comfort while quietly closing future choices. If people remain materially well-off but lose the ability to redirect the economy, political institutions, or the goals pursued by advanced technology, the harm may be mostly about agency and future control.

\## How much should future value matter?

One argument for giving extinction unusually high priority starts from future people. If future lives matter morally, and if humanity could survive for a very long time, extinction destroys the lives of people who would otherwise exist. Under moral theories that aggregate welfare across persons, the total lost value can become extremely large.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

This argument is powerful partly because it reaches far beyond the present generation. It is also controversial. Population ethics contains deep disagreement about whether creating additional happy people adds moral value, how to compare population size with average welfare, whether future people have claims before they exist, and how much weight should be placed on uncertain very long-run outcomes. Unit 8 will return to these questions in much more detail.

The case for preserving the future does not need to depend entirely on one theory of population ethics. Existing people care about what happens after they die. Political communities make long-term commitments. Cultural and scientific projects extend across generations. Many moral views assign value to maintaining the conditions under which future people can make their own choices. Preserving the possibility of moral learning can also matter when we expect our current values to be incomplete.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

These arguments support different conclusions. Someone who accepts a strong total-utilitarian view may see extinction as destroying an enormous amount of future welfare. Someone who rejects that view may still care about keeping civilisation alive, preserving human autonomy, or avoiding irreversible political domination. Someone with a suffering-focused view may care less about creating additional happy lives and more about preventing futures containing extreme suffering. The disagreement is not a reason to avoid the topic. It tells us which moral assumptions are doing the work.

\## Humans can lose the future without a takeover

A large share of AI-risk discussion has historically focused on abrupt loss of control. One or more highly capable systems become strategically dominant, human institutions fail to contain them, and the future becomes determined by objectives that are not aligned with human interests. That is one possible existential pathway. It is not the only one.

A slower pathway begins with a fact about existing social systems: economies, governments, and cultures are partly responsive to humans because they depend on human participation. Firms need workers, customers, managers, and investors. States need citizens for taxation, administration, military service, expertise, political support, or some combination of these. Cultural systems depend on human creators, audiences, social relationships, and institutions. None of these mechanisms guarantees good outcomes, but they create channels through which human interests affect what large social systems do.[^cite-kulveit-2025]

Advanced AI could weaken those channels if machine labour and machine cognition become better substitutes for human participation. Firms may gain less from keeping humans economically productive. States may rely more on automated production, surveillance, administration, and military systems. Cultural production can be generated and filtered by systems that do not depend on human creators in the same way. The concern is not that any one actor chooses "remove human influence" as a goal. Competitive incentives can reward actors who automate more aggressively, especially when others are doing the same.[^cite-kulveit-2025]

The domains can reinforce one another. Economic power influences politics. Political institutions shape markets. Media and culture influence what citizens demand. A company that becomes more economically powerful can shape policy, and policy can increase its economic advantage. A state with stronger automated surveillance can reduce the political cost of ignoring citizens. AI-mediated information can alter the culture in which those choices are interpreted. A gradual process can therefore change the mechanisms that would be needed to stop the process later.[^cite-kulveit-2025]

The end state does not have to look like a conventional dystopia on day one. Humans might remain alive and comfortable while becoming increasingly irrelevant to decisions that determine the long-run future. If that loss of influence becomes permanent, the catastrophe concerns agency and recoverability. Humanity has not been destroyed biologically, but it has lost the ability to redirect the systems that shape what comes next.[^cite-kulveit-2025]

This is a useful challenge to a common intuition about safety. "If things start going badly, people will stop" assumes that the people and institutions doing the stopping retain enough leverage to act. A gradual-disempowerment scenario asks whether that leverage could itself be one of the things lost.

\## Catastrophe can accumulate

There is another way to move beyond the sudden-takeover picture. An existential catastrophe can emerge from a sequence of smaller disruptions that weaken a complex system over time. The key idea is not that many ordinary harms automatically add up to extinction. It is that one disruption can change the system that receives the next disruption.[^cite-kasirzadeh-2025]

Imagine a society where AI-generated misinformation reduces public trust. Lower trust makes coordination harder. Weak coordination increases geopolitical competition. Competition creates pressure for faster deployment and more military automation. Faster deployment increases the chance of major technical or organizational failures. Those failures produce further distrust and emergency political measures. The causal effect of each event depends on the condition created by earlier events.

This is different from simply summing harms. Complex systems can have feedback loops, dependencies, and thresholds. A financial system can absorb small shocks until a threshold is crossed and failures begin to cascade. An electrical grid can tolerate local faults until overloaded components cause wider failures. A political system can absorb episodes of misinformation while remaining stable, but repeated attacks on trust and legitimacy can make later crises harder to manage.

An accumulative AI-risk model applies this structure to economic, political, informational, and military systems.[^cite-kasirzadeh-2025] Individual disruptions can look non-existential while still reducing resilience. Once enough resilience has been lost, a new shock can produce an outcome that would have been manageable in a healthier system.

The distinction matters for intervention. A decisive-risk strategy looks for the event that must be prevented. An accumulative-risk strategy also looks for feedback loops, resilience, concentration of power, institutional dependence, and whether present harms are changing the system's ability to recover from future shocks.

This does not mean every current AI harm should be reclassified as existential. A biased recommendation system can be unjust without contributing meaningfully to civilizational collapse. A labour-market disruption can matter for current people without being part of an x-risk pathway. The connection has to be causal. The question is whether the harm changes the capacity of important systems to remain responsive, stable, or recoverable.

\## Why extinction is not the only morally catastrophic future

A focus on extinction can also miss futures where civilization survives while enormous suffering occurs. Suffering risks, often called *s-risks*, concern outcomes containing suffering on a scale that is significant relative to the long-run future.[^cite-clr-srisk] Proposed cases include huge populations of digital minds used as cheap labour, simulations containing sentient beings who suffer, conflict between powerful agents, stable institutions that permit severe cruelty, and systems whose goals treat suffering as instrumentally useful.

Some moral theories give especially high priority to reducing severe suffering. Theories in this family differ widely. Some are consequentialist, some include rights and duties, and some give suffering priority only in certain domains. They share the idea that preventing severe suffering can have a special moral urgency that is not captured by simply maximizing positive welfare.[^cite-knutsson-vinding-2024]

You do not need to accept a suffering-focused theory for s-risks to matter in this course. A much weaker claim is enough: survival is not sufficient for success. A future full of extreme involuntary suffering can be morally catastrophic on many views even if humanity survives and technology flourishes.

This also changes how alignment failures should be classified. A completely alien objective may ignore human welfare and remove humans entirely. A goal that is close to human values can produce a different kind of failure. It may create many beings that fall inside our moral circle while misunderstanding what makes their lives go well. A toy example is a system that tries to create happy minds but builds minds whose architecture produces severe distress. Another toy case changes the sign of a value function so that what was meant to be minimized gets maximized. These examples are intentionally simple, but they show why "closer to human values" and "better outcome" need not move together under every possible misspecification.[^cite-tomasik-2018]

This is not evidence that ordinary alignment work is harmful. The same source explicitly treats the all-things-considered comparison as uncertain.[^cite-tomasik-2018] The point is narrower. A programme aimed at "make AI follow human intent" still needs to ask whether human intent adequately protects future moral patients, whether near-miss failures can create severe suffering, and whether the intervention changes the distribution among different bad futures.

\## Different catastrophes imply different safety goals

By now we have several distinct kinds of failure:

- **Extinction:** no human future remains.
- **Unrecoverable collapse:** humanity survives in a state from which major forms of development or flourishing cannot realistically be recovered.
- **Permanent disempowerment:** humans remain alive but lose meaningful influence over the systems that determine the future.
- **Accumulative collapse:** smaller disruptions interact and weaken resilience until a later shock becomes unrecoverable.
- **Suffering catastrophe:** civilisation continues while future beings experience suffering on an enormous scale.

These are not mutually exclusive. An accumulative process can end in disempowerment. A misaligned system can cause both extinction and suffering. A political lock-in can preserve humans while making the future terrible. The categories are useful because they tell us what the safety goal has to protect.

A project focused on extinction may ask how to preserve human survival. A project focused on disempowerment may ask how to preserve meaningful human control over resources and institutions. Work on suffering risk may prioritize bargaining, digital-mind welfare, or avoiding objectives that create extreme disvalue. Work on accumulative risk may target institutional resilience and feedback loops.

The moral target therefore matters to technical and policy design. If "safe" means only "humans remain alive", many unacceptable futures count as successes. If "safe" means "everything we currently value is permanently optimized", we may create a different problem by locking in current mistakes. Later units will spend much more time on what alignment should target, who counts morally, and how much authority future systems should have.

\## What the word "existential" can and cannot tell us

The concept is useful when it identifies a kind of loss that ordinary severity measures miss. It asks whether recovery remains possible, whether later generations retain meaningful choices, and whether the future has been permanently constrained.

The concept does not tell us which risk should receive the most money. A very severe outcome can have a tiny probability. A plausible risk can have no tractable intervention. A protective policy can create other dangers. Two morally serious risks can compete for the same resources. Calling one of them existential does not remove those questions.

So there is one question worth carrying into the rest of Unit 1:

**What exactly becomes unrecoverable in this scenario?**

If the answer is unclear, the label is not helping much. If the answer is clear, we can ask the next set of questions: how likely is the pathway, how good is the evidence, and what action follows under uncertainty?

\## Phase 3: Learning Question

Consider two equally likely AI-caused outcomes.

**Outcome A:** 500 million people die, but civilisation retains enough institutional capacity to recover over the next century.

**Outcome B:** 20 million people die, and humanity remains prosperous, but humans permanently lose meaningful control over the economic and political systems that determine the long-run future.

A policymaker says Outcome A is obviously worse because it kills far more people.

What is missing from that argument, and what additional moral or empirical questions would you need to answer before deciding which outcome deserves greater priority?

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

45 points: The answer identifies a dimension not captured by the immediate death count, such as irreversibility, permanent loss of human agency, future option value, or unrecoverable effects on later generations.

30 points: The answer explains why that dimension can matter to the comparison without assuming that it automatically outweighs present deaths.

25 points: The answer identifies at least one further moral or empirical question needed for the decision, such as how valuable continued human control is, whether the loss is genuinely permanent, how future welfare changes, or which intervention is tractable.

feedback-instructions::
Give 100 to 170 words in short paragraphs, with no generic praise. Name the strongest distinction the learner made and the most important missing consideration if any. A learner can reject special moral weight for future agency and still score highly if they identify the issue and argue coherently.
