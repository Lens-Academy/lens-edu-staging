#### Question: Open
id:: c3f2d7b3-c93b-4c8f-8489-8c155f8cfea2
content::
At every quarterly review, Leo's team finds that his project estimates were too optimistic. Each time, Leo says: "I know, I'm overconfident. Everyone is, it's human nature. At least I'm honest enough to admit it." When a colleague suggests he write down his estimates and check them against what actually happened, Leo says: "You can't fix human nature with a spreadsheet."

Answer in your own words.

1. What is wrong with the way Leo treats his admission, and how does it shape his reply to the colleague?
2. What should he do instead?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The scenario: at every review Leo admits his estimates were overconfident, says everyone is overconfident, and adds "At least I'm honest enough to admit it." When a colleague suggests he record his estimates and check them against the outcomes, Leo dismisses it: "You can't fix human nature with a spreadsheet." The idea being applied: confessing a flaw is not progress, taking pride in the confession makes you hold on to the flaw and reject people who offer a way to correct it, and what matters is a plan to do better.

60: Explains that admitting the flaw is not the same as fixing it, and that Leo treats the admission as something to be proud of (or as enough on its own), which leads him to brush off the colleague's way to improve. Give 30 if the answer only says that admitting is not enough, without connecting his attitude to his dismissal of the colleague's suggestion.

40: Says he should do something concrete to become less overconfident, such as taking up the colleague's suggestion or another way to check and correct his estimates, so that he has less to admit at the next review. Give 20 if the answer only says "he should improve" without anything he could actually do.

Cap at 40 if the answer says that honestly admitting the bias is the main thing that matters.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Leo acts as if admitting he's overconfident is the same as dealing with it, and he's proud of admitting it. Because the admission already makes him feel good, he waves away the one suggestion that could make him less overconfident. He should take the colleague up on it: record his estimates, compare them with what happens, and adjust, so there's less to confess next quarter."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "Tsuyoku Naritai! (I Want To Become Stronger)" and answered a question about it. In the question, Leo admits his overconfidence at every review, is proud of being honest about it, and dismisses a colleague's suggestion to track his estimates against the outcomes.

Key ideas from the essay:
- The most important thing is that there is progress. Keep moving forward.
- Confessing that "we are all biased" is like the Yom Kippur litany: it confesses flaws without any plan to have fewer of them.
- Do not take pride in confessing your flaws. If the confession is a source of pride, you become reluctant to give up the flaw, and you may sneer at someone who offers a way to correct it.
- Do not confess you are as flawed as others unless you can say what you plan to do about it. The occasion for rejoicing is having a little less to confess.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Calm and direct. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent answer, well done).

If the score is below 100, name the single most important thing the answer missed or got wrong in what the question asks (what is wrong with how Leo treats his admission, how it shapes his reply, and what he should do instead), and ask one question that helps the student find it. At 100, confirm briefly what the answer got right. Comment only against the ideas above, which the essay supplied.

If the student says they do not understand, do not dismiss it or repeat the question.
