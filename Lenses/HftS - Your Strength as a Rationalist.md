title: "Your Strength as a Rationalist"
tldr: When a story doesn't quite fit what you know, treat that feeling as a clue. Either your picture of the world is wrong or the story is. Your strength is being more confused by fiction than by reality.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky tells how someone in a chatroom reported that a friend with chest pains was sent home by paramedics. The story did not fit what he knew about ambulances, but he forced it into his model ("it must really be nothing"), and the story turned out to be made up. His lesson is that the feeling that an explanation "still feels a little forced" is an important clue, which he threw away. A model that can explain any outcome equally well has zero knowledge. Your strength as a rationalist is being more confused by fiction than by reality. The feeling means "Either your model is false or this story is wrong". He also notes that belief is easier than disbelief. An optional, ungraded reflection asks the student for a time something did not fit what they knew and they explained it away, and what the feeling might have pointed to.

#### Question: Open
id:: 7ebb3445-accf-4ed5-9240-1285b1531dfd
content::
Think of a time something you heard or saw didn't quite fit what you knew, and you explained it away, like Eliezer with the paramedics story. What did you tell yourself, and what might the feeling have been pointing to?
optional:: true
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Your Strength as a Rationalist" and written an optional, ungraded reflection about a time something did not quite fit what they knew and they explained it away. There is no right answer and no score. Key ideas from the essay: Yudkowsky forced a chatroom story about paramedics into his model even though it felt a little forced, and the story turned out to be made up. That feeling of strain is a clue, which means either your model is wrong or the story is. A model that can explain any outcome equally well tells you nothing. Belief is easier than disbelief.

Reply in 60 to 120 words, in short paragraphs, with no lists. Do not over-validate and avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Take their example seriously and do not judge them for it. Name the explanation they used to make the story fit, in plain words, and point out which two possibilities the feeling of strain pointed to in their case: their picture of things was wrong, or the story was. If they have not said what turned out to be true, ask one question about it. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close briefly. If the student says they cannot think of an example, offer one small everyday kind (a rumour that seemed odd, a number in the news that seemed too big) and let them skip if they want.
