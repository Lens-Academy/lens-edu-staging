\## A catastrophe can be enormous without being existential

Suppose a disaster kills 99 percent of humanity. The survivors are scattered and civilisation collapses, but over centuries people rebuild. Knowledge is partly recovered, institutions re-form, and eventually humanity again has a long future in front of it. Now suppose a second disaster kills 100 percent. If we count only the deaths that occur during the catastrophe, the difference between the two cases is one percentage point. Derek Parfit's well-known comparison asks us to notice that this way of counting may miss the most important difference. In the first case, there are survivors who can recover. In the second, every later human life and every later human project disappears as well.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

This is the starting point for Nick Bostrom's account of existential risk. He does not define an existential catastrophe as an event above some casualty threshold. He defines it in terms of what happens to humanity's long-run potential. Human extinction is the clearest case, but it is not the only one. A civilisation that survives while becoming permanently unable to recover, or that reaches enormous technological power in an irreversibly bad form, may also have lost something that no later generation can repair.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

The distinction is therefore not between "bad" and "very bad". It is between losses that leave open the possibility of recovery and losses that permanently remove important possibilities. That is why irreversibility matters. A society that survives a catastrophe can learn from it, compensate some victims, rebuild institutions, or revise its values. A society that no longer exists cannot do any of these things. A permanent lock-in of a bad trajectory creates a related problem: people may continue to exist, but later generations may have no realistic route back to alternatives that were available before the lock-in.

\## Why should the future count?

One argument for giving existential risk unusual weight is aggregative. If future people matter morally, and if humanity could have a very long future containing many worthwhile lives, extinction destroys not only the lives of people alive at the time but also the value those future lives could have contained. Bostrom develops this argument using very large estimates of the possible scale of humanity's future. The exact numbers are not essential to the structure of the argument. What matters is that a long and flourishing future could contain vastly more value than the present generation alone.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

This is where many readers become either very persuaded or very skeptical. The argument depends on substantive moral assumptions. Perhaps possible future people do not count in the same way as existing people. Perhaps adding more happy lives does not simply add value. Perhaps the future would be mediocre, oppressive, or filled with suffering rather than flourishing. Population ethics gives us no uncontested formula that settles these questions.

Bostrom is explicit that the case for avoiding existential catastrophe need not stand or fall with one form of total utilitarianism. He also considers other ways of caring about humanity's continuation. Someone might value the continuation of human culture, science, relationships between generations, political projects, or the open-ended possibility of discovering forms of value we do not yet understand. Existing people also have preferences about what happens after they die, and many moral views treat those preferences as morally relevant. These arguments do not all imply the same priority for existential-risk reduction, but they show that concern about permanent loss can survive rejection of the strongest astronomical-value calculation.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

This matters for the rest of the course. If the only reason to care about existential risk were one highly specific theory of population ethics, then rejecting that theory would undermine the whole programme. That is not the position we will assume. Instead, we will keep the normative questions open and ask which parts of the case survive under different moral views.

\## The category is partly normative

There is another complication. Some existential catastrophes can be described without making many moral judgments. Human extinction is a biological and historical event. Permanent stagnation can also be described largely in terms of what capabilities humanity never develops. Other cases are harder. To call a technologically mature future "flawed" requires some account of what would have made the future better. Bostrom therefore notes that the concept of existential risk is partly evaluative.[^cite-bostrom-2013]

This is not a technical nuisance. It changes how we should reason about AI safety. Suppose an AI system preserves human beings but permanently removes meaningful political agency. Is that an existential catastrophe? What if it preserves freedom but locks civilisation into values that later generations would have rejected? What if digital minds become the majority of morally relevant beings? These questions cannot be answered by measuring capability alone. They depend on what counts as a valuable future and on who counts morally.

Weeks 2, 3, 7, and 8 will return to these questions from different directions. For now, the important point is that "existential risk" is not a morally neutral label that settles what matters. It identifies a family of outcomes in which something important about the future may be lost permanently. The moral significance of that loss still has to be argued for.

\## Does irreversibility always dominate?

No. Imagine a one-in-a-billion chance of irreversible catastrophe and a near certainty of a huge but recoverable disaster. Calling the first risk "existential" does not by itself tell us to prioritise it. We still need to know how likely each risk is, how much a proposed intervention changes it, what the intervention costs, what else those resources could accomplish, and how reliable our estimates are. We may also care about distribution, rights, duties to current people, and harms caused by the intervention itself.

This is why the distinction between catastrophic and existential risk is useful only if it changes the questions we ask rather than replacing them. Irreversibility can add a morally important dimension that a simple death count misses, but it does not turn a complex allocation problem into a label-matching exercise. A policy can target an existential risk and still be ineffective, excessively costly, or dangerous in another way.

There is a further reason to resist treating "existential" as a trump card. Once a category is associated with enormous moral importance, people have incentives to describe their preferred problem as belonging to that category. The right response is not to abandon the concept. It is to ask for the mechanism. What is supposedly lost permanently? Why is that loss valuable? How likely is the pathway? Which intervention changes it? Those questions keep the argument connected to decisions.

\## The decision problem

Return to the two programmes from the pre-question. Programme A addresses a catastrophe that kills 100 million people. Programme B addresses a much more uncertain risk that could permanently end humanity's future. You now have one additional consideration: the immediate casualty count does not capture everything that may matter. Programme B could prevent a loss that is qualitatively different because recovery is impossible.

But you still cannot choose. You need probability, tractability, costs, moral assumptions, and evidence quality. The role of this lens is narrower. It gives you one distinction that a competent decision-maker should not miss: when comparing severe risks, ask not only how much harm occurs immediately, but also what possibilities are removed permanently and why those possibilities matter.

The next submodule takes the harder step. Once the possible loss becomes extremely large, even tiny probabilities can begin to dominate expected-value calculations. That creates a real reason to take uncertain risks seriously, and a real danger of taking bad arguments too seriously.

What is missing from this reasoning? You do not have to agree that the existential risk should receive greater priority.
