tldr: People who call themselves rational are often no more impressive than anyone else. That is because they have had little systematic, tested training, not because thinking cannot be trained. A real art of rationality is still to be built.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky answers the common objection that rational people do not seem any happier. Those people have had very little rationality, he says, all of it ad hoc and self-taught, nothing like the training of a black belt, an Olympic runner or the knowledge of a nuclear engineer. There ought to be a systematic art of thinking that makes its students visibly more formidable. Martial arts became systematic because people had a sense that more was possible and because they could tell when they had hit someone. Rationality lacks such an art first because people lack that sense, second because results are hard to verify (he wants evidence-based training, for example running half the signups through one version of a program and half through another and surveying them years later), and third because rationalists have trouble working in groups. The lack of visible formidability should make us say "we must be doing something wrong". The question gives the student a friend who concludes from a few unimpressive self-described rationalists that studying rationality does little, and asks what is wrong with that conclusion and what evidence would show whether rationality training works.

#### Question: Open
id:: c8701fec-af6c-4820-8418-5dd5dd19ce34
content::
A friend says: "I know a few people who read about cognitive biases and call themselves rationalists. They aren't any happier or more successful than anyone else. So studying rationality doesn't do much."

Answer in your own words.

1. What is wrong with your friend's conclusion, according to the essay?
2. By the essay's standards, what kind of evidence would show whether rationality training works?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The friend's argument: a few people who read about cognitive biases and call themselves rationalists are no happier or more successful than others, so studying rationality does little. The essay's view: such people have had very little rationality training, all of it ad hoc and self-taught, far less systematic than the training of a black belt or an athlete. A serious art of rationality would have to be systematized, trained and tested, and the essay names testing the results of training programs as the main obstacle, with examples like running half of the people signing up through one version of a training program and half through another, and surveying them years later.

60: Explains that these people have had little real training in rationality: what they know is casual, self-taught and unsystematic (for example, unlike the training of a martial artist or an athlete). So their results show what casual study does, not what serious, systematic training could do. Give 30 if the answer only says the sample is small, or that these people are not "real" rationalists, without saying that the problem is the lack of serious, systematic training.

40: Describes a real test of a training program's results: compare people who went through a serious training program with people who had different training or none (ideally assigned at random), and measure how they actually do afterwards, for example years later. Give 20 if the answer only says to "do a study" or "measure it" without any comparison between groups.

Cap at 40 if the answer agrees that rationality cannot really be trained.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The people your friend knows have barely trained at all. They read some books and improvised, which is nothing like how a black belt or an athlete trains. So they don't show what a serious, systematic training could do. To find out whether training works, you'd put some people through a real program and others through a different one or none, picked at random, and see how they do years later."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "A Sense That More Is Possible" and answered a question about it. In the question, a friend concludes from a few unimpressive self-described rationalists that studying rationality does little.

Key ideas from the essay:

- Current rationalists learned ad hoc and alone, with far less systematic training than a first-dan black belt gets in hitting people.

- The lack of visible results should make us say "we must be doing something wrong", not give up.
