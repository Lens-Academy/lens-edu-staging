title: "Taboo Your Words"
tldr: When two people argue over a word, stop using the word and describe what each of you expects to see. You may find there was no disagreement, or a real one hidden under the shared label.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky argues that when you are stuck in a confusing argument, the first move is not to define the problem word but to stop using it and its short synonyms, and to describe observable things and mechanisms instead. Examples are the game Taboo, the dispute over whether a tree falling in an empty forest makes a sound (acoustic vibrations against auditory experience), Albert and Barry using "human" for concepts with different predictions about hemlock, futurists who agree on "AI in thirty years" but would expect different things, and free will. Tabooing a word can make an apparent conflict vanish or reveal a real clash of expectations. The question gives a dispute over whether a colleague is "a good programmer" and asks the learner to taboo the phrase and check whether any disagreement about what they expect to see remains.

#### Question: Open
id:: f2c12c98-aa05-44f4-8ebe-2e712e85f61b
content::
Dana and Lee argue about their new colleague Sam.

Dana: "Sam is a good programmer."

Lee: "Sam is not a good programmer."

Dana has watched Sam finish every task early, with code that passes all its tests. Lee has had to change Sam's code twice and found it very hard to follow.

1. Rewrite each claim the way the essay recommends, without the words "good" or "programmer" or any short synonym such as "skilled" or "competent".

2. After the rewrite, do Dana and Lee still disagree about anything they expect to see? Say why.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay says that when people argue over a word, they should stop using the word and its short synonyms and describe what they actually observe or expect instead. Sometimes the conflict then disappears, because each side used the same word for a different test (one person's "sound" meant acoustic vibrations, the other's meant someone hearing it). Sometimes a real difference in expectations appears that the shared word had hidden. In this scenario, Dana's "good programmer" stands for "finishes tasks quickly with code that passes its tests". Lee's "not a good programmer" stands for "writes code that is hard for others to follow and change".

40: Both claims are rewritten as descriptions of what Sam does or what each person has seen, without "good", "programmer", or a mere synonym (such as "skilled", "competent", "writes good code", "bad coder"). 20 for each claim. A rewrite that only swaps in another evaluative label gets 0 for that claim.

60: The answer says that, once rewritten, the two claims do not conflict about anything they expect to see, because both descriptions can be true of Sam at the same time: they were using one label for two different things. An answer that adds that they might still differ on which of these matters more, or on a decision such as whether to keep Sam, keeps full marks as long as it says this is not a disagreement about what they expect to observe.

Cap at 40 if the answer concludes that one of them is simply right about whether Sam is a good programmer.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Dana: Sam finishes his tasks early and his code passes its tests. Lee: when someone else has to change Sam's code, it takes a long time to understand. These don't contradict each other. Both can be true of Sam, so once the label is gone they don't disagree about anything they'd expect to see. They were just using 'good programmer' for different things."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Taboo Your Words" and applied it to Dana and Lee's argument over whether Sam is "a good programmer". The essay's key ideas: in an argument over a word, do not define the word, stop using it and its synonyms. Describe observable things instead. Then check whether the two sides now expect different things. Sometimes the conflict vanishes (the tree and "sound"), sometimes a real clash of expectations appears that the shared word hid (Albert and Barry on hemlock). Here Dana means Sam finishes fast with code that passes its tests, and Lee means Sam's code is hard for others to follow and change. Both can be true, so nothing they expect to see is in conflict.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap. Usually it is a rewrite that only swaps in a synonym ("skilled", "writes good code"), or a verdict on who is right instead of a check of what each expects to see. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example what Lee would expect to happen the next time he has to change Sam's code. At 100, confirm briefly.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as how Albert's "sound" became "acoustic vibrations". If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
