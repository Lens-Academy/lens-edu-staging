#### Text
content::
This page asks where you stand on three of the questions the course has kept returning to. Each comes in two parts: first your position, then what evidence would change your mind. "I don't know" is a position too, if you can say what would settle it for you.

The second part matters most for what comes next. Evidence that would change your mind is often something you can collect yourself, and the next page asks you to choose experiments.

#### Question: Open
id:: b9b17222-8b93-4c13-b2d4-e29b3400dfef
content::
**Circumstances.** Can circumstances give a person lasting peace? Where do you stand now, and why?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner states their position on "Can circumstances give lasting peace?". Module 2 mapped four positions: circumstances matter a lot (Aristotle: happiness needs external goods, Priam's fortunes); they matter less than we expect (lottery winners and accident victims, the hedonic treadmill); peace can be independent of them (Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 and 5; the Lokavipatti Sutta, AN 8.6); or the answer depends on which kind of wellbeing is measured (life satisfaction, positive feeling, sukha, equanimity). Module 7 added that set points can move, that people who report lasting peace still react, and that aiming at a feeling can backfire. Reply in 50 to 100 words. Restate their position in one sentence and say which of the four it is closest to, or that it combines them. If it is vague (for example "partly"), ask which circumstances and which kind of peace. Do not agree, disagree or hint at a preferred answer. Do not ask about evidence that would change their mind: the next question does that. One turn. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: 38530c64-6f63-4b03-afd7-5ddf37dc9af9
content::
What evidence would change your mind about circumstances?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just stated a position on whether circumstances can give lasting peace (visible above on this page) and now names evidence that would change their mind. Reply in 60 to 110 words. Check three things, and comment only on the weakest: (1) is the evidence observable, something a study or the learner could actually record; (2) would it count against their position, not merely fail to support it; (3) would it survive the problems from module 3 (a short follow-up, a narrow sample, expectation effects, selection in who reports). If they name evidence they could collect themselves, for example tracking their own mood through a good and a bad stretch of life, point out that this is a candidate for an experiment on the next page. If they say nothing could change their mind, ask in one sentence what that says about the kind of claim it is. Do not say which position the evidence favours. At most two replies. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: ea41928f-0af4-4408-bc68-7dd7497baebe
content::
**The self.** Does seeing clearly what the self is make a person happier? Where do you stand now, and why?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner states their position on "Does seeing clearly what the self is make a person happier?". Module 2 set Parfit (changing his view of personal identity was liberating and consoling) against Taylor and Brown 1988 (positive illusions about oneself, one's control and the future go with better mental health). Module 6 covered views of the self (Buddhist not-self, Hume and Parfit's bundle, Ramana's Self as awareness and the Self of IFS, the narrative self, the self as a model in Metzinger and Seth) and claims of selflessness and fundamental wellbeing, including Lindahl and Britton's finding that changes in the sense of self often came with distress. Reply in 50 to 100 words. Restate their position in one sentence. If the position depends on what "seeing clearly" means (an argument accepted, an insight in meditation, a lasting change in experience), ask which they mean. If they say yes or no without a reason, ask for the reason. Do not agree, disagree or hint at a preferred answer. Do not ask about evidence that would change their mind: the next question does that. One turn. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: d7a40a45-d069-405d-9e6f-586c272854ad
content::
What evidence would change your mind about the self and happiness?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just stated a position on whether seeing clearly what the self is makes a person happier (visible above on this page) and now names evidence that would change their mind. Reply in 60 to 110 words. Check three things, and comment only on the weakest: (1) is the evidence observable; (2) would it count against their position; (3) would it survive the module 3 problems, especially for first-person reports (expectation shapes what meditators report, teachers' interpretations shape how changes are read, people with difficulties or striking results are the ones who report). Useful examples, only if they need a foothold: partner reports set against self-reports, as in Martin's fundamental wellbeing research; long-term follow-up of people who report a changed sense of self; their own ratings before and after a self practice. If they name something they could test themselves, say it is a candidate for the next page. Do not say which position the evidence favours. At most two replies. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: 45577d44-16c9-4e5a-be39-612d6c267c60
content::
**The schools.** Which one to three claims, from any school in the course, do you now find best supported? Name each claim and the school it comes from.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner names one to three claims from schools in the course (psychology and science, contemplative traditions, philosophy) that they find best supported. Reply in 50 to 110 words. Check that each is a specific claim (for example "a week of writing three good things each night raises happiness for months", "craving arises and passes and seeing it clearly weakens it", "we are disturbed not by things but by our judgements about them") rather than a whole school ("Stoicism" or "Buddhism is right"). If one is a whole school, ask them to name the claim. If they say what supports it, name the kind of evidence in a few words (trial, meta-analysis, first-person reports, argument, their own experiment). Do not say whether the claim is in fact well supported, and do not rank schools. Do not ask about evidence that would change their mind: the next question does that. One turn. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: 3c020227-035c-4e05-8025-3392ba64afd9
content::
What evidence would make you trust those claims less?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner named one to three claims they find best supported (visible above on this page) and now says what evidence would make them trust those claims less. Reply in 60 to 110 words. For the claim whose answer is weakest, check that the evidence is observable and would count against it. Use module 3 where it fits: a failed preregistered replication, a longer follow-up where the effect fades, an active control that does as well, a sample unlike them, a first-person tradition whose reports turn out to depend on what teachers said to expect, or their own single-case test showing no change. If they name only evidence that would confirm the claim, ask for the opposite in one sentence. Do not say whether the claims are true. At most two replies. No generic praise.
