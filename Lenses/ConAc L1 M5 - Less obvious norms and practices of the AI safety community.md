Some parts of the field have inherited distinctive communication practices from the rationalist and effective altruist communities. These practices are often left implicit.

\### One-on-one meetings (1:1s)

In many professional environments, arranging a meeting with someone requires a concrete business purpose or an existing working relationship. In AI safety, relatively informal one-on-one meetings are common even between people who have never worked together.

A 1:1 may be an opportunity to exchange perspectives on a research problem, discuss career plans, obtain feedback on a project, explore potential collaboration or simply learn about someone's work.

People frequently arrange such meetings with researchers, organisation founders and other professionals they have never met before.

If you are requesting a 1:1, briefly explain why you would like to speak to this particular person and what you hope to discuss. This helps them assess whether the conversation would be worthwhile and gives them some context in which to prepare.

\### Ask vs Guess Culture

The distinction between Ask Culture and Guess Culture concerns the implicit expectations surrounding requests.

In Guess Culture, people are expected to infer whether a request would be welcome before making it. Asking for something carries an implicit expectation that the other person will agree, which can make refusing socially uncomfortable.

In Ask Culture, people are generally more comfortable making explicit requests whose answers are uncertain. The recipient is expected to communicate their own boundaries and can decline without having to justify the decision.

For example, you might ask a researcher whether they have time to review a short research proposal. You do not need to establish in advance that they are likely to agree. You do, however, need to be genuinely comfortable with a refusal.

This convention can make it easier to request feedback, approach potential collaborators and investigate opportunities. It works best when both parties understand and respect the underlying expectations.

Read more: [Ask and Guess](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/vs3kzjLhbdKsndnBy/ask-and-guess).

\### Explicit epistemic status

You may have noticed that some LessWrong posts begin with a statement labelled *Epistemic status* or *Epistemic effort*.

These statements communicate additional information about the author's confidence, the evidence underlying their claims, and the amount of investigation that went into developing their position.

For example:

*Epistemic status: Speculative. I have discussed this idea with several researchers, but have yet to investigate it empirically.*

*Epistemic status: Fairly confident in the main argument. Considerably less confident in the quantitative estimates.*

*Epistemic effort: Approximately 30 hours of literature review and preliminary experiments.*

Such statements help readers determine how to engage with a piece of writing. An exploratory idea may invite suggestions about how to develop it. An extensively investigated claim may call for a more detailed examination of its evidence and reasoning.

The same practice extends to conversations. Explicitly communicating which parts of your position you are confident about, where your intuitions come from, and what remains uncertain can make intellectual exchanges more productive.

Read more: [Epistemic Effort](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/oDy27zfRf8uAbJR6M/epistemic-effort).

\### Steelmanning

Steelmanning is the practice of constructing the strongest plausible version of an argument, especially one with which you disagree.

Suppose you believe that a particular AI safety agenda is unlikely to succeed. Before criticising it, you might try to reconstruct the most compelling case for pursuing it. What assumptions would make the approach reasonable? What evidence might its proponents have access to? Which version of their argument would be most difficult for you to refute?

This is particularly relevant in a field where researchers frequently disagree about foundational assumptions and where superficially similar arguments can originate from substantially different models of the problem.

When discussing someone's position with them, it is worth checking whether they recognise your reconstruction as an accurate representation of their views.

Read more: [Steelmanning](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/steelmanning).

\### Double Crux

Double Crux is a technique for investigating disagreements by identifying the underlying beliefs on which opposing conclusions depend.

Suppose two researchers disagree about whether investing in a particular alignment approach is worthwhile.

Through conversation, they might discover that their disagreement depends largely on their expectations about the scalability of interpretability techniques.

If both would reconsider their positions upon changing this underlying belief, they have identified a double crux. They can now investigate a more specific question and potentially identify evidence that would help resolve their original disagreement.

Read more: [Double Crux: A Strategy for Mutual Understanding](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/exa5kmvopeRyfJgCy/double-crux-a-strategy-for-mutual-understanding).

\### Proactively seeking and processing feedback

In some parts of the AI safety community, researchers routinely share unfinished work, circulate early drafts and solicit criticism before committing substantial resources to a project.

Feedback can be sought at many stages: when choosing a research problem, developing a Theory of Change, designing an experiment, interpreting its results or preparing a publication.

It is useful to develop several habits:

- Seek feedback early, while changing your approach is still relatively inexpensive.
- Specify what kind of feedback you need. Someone evaluating your research direction may need different information from someone reviewing your experimental methodology.
- Make it easy for people to identify weaknesses in your reasoning. Explain your assumptions and indicate where you are uncertain.
- Investigate the reasoning behind criticism before deciding whether to accept or reject it.
- When appropriate, follow up with the person who provided feedback and explain how it informed your subsequent work.

Developing a reliable feedback loop is especially valuable when working independently or investigating problems for which there are few established standards of research quality.

Read more: [How to Get Rationalist Feedback](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/NwWhbgovCwGQCKAZi/how-to-get-rationalist-feedback).

As AI safety attracts professionals from an increasingly diverse range of backgrounds, differences in communication norms can become a source of misunderstanding.

In [AI Safety Acculturation is Neglected](https://www.jenn.site/ai-safety-acculturation-is-neglected/) (2026), the author examines the cultural gap between people with longstanding involvement in the rationalist community and professionals entering AI safety from industry and government. The essay discusses why mutual understanding requires deliberate effort and proposes ways for these groups to learn from one another.

Familiarity with the practices described in this submodule can help you recognise the expectations behind certain interactions. You may also encounter situations where conventions from your own professional background offer useful alternatives.
