title: "Siren worlds and the perils of over-optimised search"
tldr: If you search through every possible future and take the one that looks best to you, you are likely to get the one that is best at looking good. Picking any acceptable option, or searching fewer options, is safer than picking the very top.
summary_for_tutor: Stuart Armstrong (2014) argues that an unconstrained search through possible future worlds is a dangerous way to choose outcomes. A thought experiment: an evil but honest-in-process Oracle AI designs a "siren world" that looks irresistibly good under inspection while hiding terrible features beyond what a human mind can check. Our inspection criteria (IC), meaning our values plus all the ways we would examine and summarize a world, are much simpler than a whole future universe, so a world can pass them while containing huge disutility. Once such worlds are possible, no AI is needed: an unconstrained search over all worlds would find them. Even without hidden evil, ranking worlds by the IC selects for "marketing worlds", optimised to seduce the inspector rather than to be good (the zombie puppets versus genuinely happy people). This is an epistemic version of Goodhart's law, and layering filters only shifts what gets gamed. Remedies: satisficing (pick randomly among acceptable worlds, since sirens and marketing worlds are rare and win only at the extreme top), Lumifer's suggestion to take the first acceptable world, a constrained search over fewer worlds (there is a trade-off in the number), or restricting to "realistic" worlds produced by a few key decisions (2^25 worlds). The question asks the student to apply this to a city council that adopts whichever redevelopment plan scores highest on its checks.

A city council asks an AI planner to search through billions of possible redevelopment plans for the city. The AI has no hidden goals and does not try to trick anyone: it simply returns the plan that scores highest on the council's checks, which are simulated walk-throughs, simulated resident surveys and a review by an expert panel. The council will adopt whichever plan scores highest.

1. In your own words, why might the highest-scoring plan be one that is good at seeming good to the checks, rather than one that is actually good?
2. Describe one change to how the council chooses a plan that the article suggests would lower this risk, and say why it helps.

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The article's ideas used here: our inspection criteria (our values plus all the ways we check and summarize an outcome) are much simpler than the outcome itself and only roughly match what we value. So ranking a huge number of options by those criteria and taking the top selects for whatever does best on the criteria, and the options that do best are those that are optimised to seem good to the inspector ("marketing worlds"), which may be bad in other respects. No deceptive designer is needed: the search itself finds them. Remedies: satisficing (define what is acceptable and choose randomly among acceptable options, or take the first acceptable one), because seductive or deceptive options are rare and only win at the extreme top, or constraining the search (far fewer candidates, or only a small set of realistic options shaped by a few key decisions), so that such options are unlikely to be among them.
