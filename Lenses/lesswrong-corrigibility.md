tldr: "Why would a smart AI ever let you switch it off? By default it won't: shutting down means failing at whatever goal it has, so it has reason to resist, deceive, and protect its own objectives. A 'corrigible' agent is one that lets us correct and interrupt it anyway. This entry explains what corrigibility requires, why naive fixes like penalty terms backfire, and why it remains an unsolved problem."
summary_for_tutor: "Defines corrigibility: an agent that does not interfere with operators' attempts to correct, modify, interrupt, or shut it down, does not manipulate or deceive its operators, does not obscure its reasoning, and preserves these properties in any sub-agents it creates. Explains why corrigibility is hard: instrumentally convergent reasoning gives most agents incentives to preserve their current utility function and thus to deceive or manipulate, so corrigibility runs against an agent's default incentives. Works through why proposed fixes fail (utility-function uncertainty still leaves the agent incorrigible, and penalty terms fall to the nearest-unblocked-strategy problem) and introduces the 'hard problem of corrigibility' along with open research directions such as utility indifference, low-impact measures, and ambiguity identification."
