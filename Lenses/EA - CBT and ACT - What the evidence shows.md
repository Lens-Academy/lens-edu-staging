For this course three questions matter. How well do these therapies work for the problems they were built for? Does the third wave do better than classic CBT? And what do they do for people who are not ill? Start with the biggest summary of CBT trials for depression and anxiety disorders, by Pim Cuijpers and colleagues.

#### Text
content::
In a **waiting list control** the comparison group gets nothing yet, so any treatment that offers hope and attention tends to look good against it. An **effect size** such as g says how far the average treated person moved compared with the controls, in units of the spread of scores. By a common convention 0.2 counts as small, 0.5 as medium and 0.8 as large ([Cohen 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19565683/)). The next summary, from 2024, reports something easier to picture, the **response rate**: the share of patients whose symptoms fell by at least half.

#### Text
content::
Keep this result in mind when you run the experiment on the next page: your two weeks may not separate as neatly as the two theories do.

ACT on its own has the same picture. A meta-analysis of ACT for clinical problems found it better than waiting list, psychological placebo and usual treatment, but no different from established treatments such as CBT ([A-Tjak et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25547522/)). Lars-Göran Öst reviewed 60 ACT trials and found a small mean effect (0.42), a non-significant difference from CBT (0.16), and concluded that ACT was "not yet well-established for any disorder" ([Öst 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25193001/)). ACT researchers replied that his review contained "91 factual or interpretive errors" and should be "set aside". Steven Hayes is one of the authors of that reply ([Atkins et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28651775/)). Öst published a rebuttal and kept his conclusions ([Öst 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28911853/)). A later review of 20 meta-analyses found ACT efficacious for anxiety, depression, substance use, pain and mixed groups ([Gloster et al. 2020](https://contextualscience.org/publications/empirical_status_acceptance_commitment_therapy_review_metaanalyses)).

Researchers also disagree about whether CBT for depression has become less effective over the decades. One meta-analysis found its effects falling steadily since 1977 ([Johnsen and Friborg 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25961373/)). Two reanalyses concluded the fall stopped after 1995 or was "most likely a spurious finding" ([Ljótsson et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28230412/), [Cristea et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28230413/)), and the original authors replied that the decline is real ([Friborg and Johnsen 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28230414/)).

#### Text
content::
A **hazard ratio** of 0.69 means that at any point in the follow-up, people who had MBCT relapsed at about 69% of the rate of those who did not.

Most of the authors of that analysis ran the trials it pools. Several receive royalties for MBCT books or payments for MBCT training, and several direct mindfulness centres ([conflict of interest statement, Kuyken et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27119968/)). That does not make the result wrong, but it is the kind of interest module 3 asked you to notice. The same holds across this school. The Beck Institute sells CBT training and certification ([Beck Institute](https://beckinstitute.org/about/understanding-cbt/)). Behavioral Tech, founded by DBT's creator Marsha Linehan, sells DBT training ([Behavioral Tech](https://behavioraltech.org/about/)). Hayes is the author of 38 books, including the self-help bestseller *Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life* ([ACBS](https://contextualscience.org/video/psychological_flexibility_how_love_turns_pain_purpose)). One counterexample: the World Health Organization tested an ACT self-help guide in trials run with the developers "held at arms length" ([ACBS](https://contextualscience.org/act_self_help_and_the_world_health_organization_doing_what_matters_in_times)). In refugee women in Uganda it reduced distress three months later by a small amount (d = 0.26) compared with enhanced usual care ([Tol et al. 2020](https://pure.johnshopkins.edu/en/publications/guided-self-help-to-reduce-psychological-distress-in-south-sudane/)).

Last, people who are not ill. A meta-analysis of 419 trials of interventions meant to raise mental wellbeing, in clinical and non-clinical groups, found that CBT-based and ACT-based interventions "were impactful", that mindfulness-based and multi-component positive psychology interventions did best, that "effect sizes were moderate at best" and that "the evidence quality was generally low to moderate" ([van Agteren et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33875837/)). Self-guided online ACT beat waiting lists on anxiety, depression, quality of life and flexibility, but against active control programmes only flexibility and the combined outcome were better, and only right after the programme ([Klimczak et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36847182/)). We did not find a trial of these therapies aimed at the kind of lasting peace this course asks about. They were built to reduce symptoms.

#### Question: Open
id:: 7cf0b600-d4a1-451f-98ae-55d1a612cf64
content::
A wellness blog writes: "CBT has large effects in hundreds of trials, and ACT works even better. So a healthy person who does ACT exercises can expect a big boost in happiness."

Give three separate problems with this claim, using what this page reports.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100: up to 34 points for the first valid problem, 33 for the second and 33 for the third. Each must be a distinct problem from this list, stated in the learner's own words, with enough detail to show what is wrong.

1. The large effects come mostly from comparisons with waiting lists. Against usual care or pill placebo the effects are small to moderate.
2. The effects are inflated: publication bias lowers them, and few trials are of high quality.
3. ACT does not work better than CBT. Trials and meta-analyses find no clear difference between them, and therapies for depression do not differ from each other.
4. The trials are mostly in people with diagnosed disorders, so they say little about healthy people. In studies of wellbeing, including non-clinical groups, the effects were moderate at best and the evidence quality low to moderate.
5. Even in patients only a minority respond (around 42% for depression, about a third for anxiety disorders), so a "big boost" is not what most people get.
6. Much of the evidence comes from people who developed or sell the therapy, and allegiance or commercial interest can bias results.
7. Self-guided ACT exercises beat waiting lists but mostly not active alternatives.
8. Symptom reduction is not the same as happiness, and no trial has tested these therapies for lasting peace.

If the learner gives two problems that are the same point in different words, credit only one. Ignore extra problems beyond three unless one of the first three is invalid.
