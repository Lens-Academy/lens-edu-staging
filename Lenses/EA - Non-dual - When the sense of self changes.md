#### Text
content::
The experiments in this module aim to loosen the ordinary sense of being a separate self. Teachers in this school describe what follows as peace. Researchers who study meditation have found that a loosened sense of self is not always pleasant.

One term first. **Depersonalisation** is a feeling of being detached from yourself, or of watching your own thoughts, feelings, body or actions from outside. When it persists and causes distress or impairment it is diagnosed as a dissociative disorder ([Merck Manual](https://www.merckmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/dissociative-disorders/depersonalization-derealization-disorder)).

Jared Lindahl and Willoughby Britton interviewed Western Buddhist meditators who had had difficulties from meditation ([Lindahl and Britton 2019](https://www.imprint.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Lindahl_Open_Access.pdf)). These meditators practised Theravada, Zen and Tibetan Buddhism, not Advaita, but the changes they describe are the ones this school's practices aim at. Of 68 practitioners, 49 (72%) reported at least one change in their sense of self. The authors sort the changes into six kinds: in the story you tell about yourself, in the sense that thoughts and feelings are yours, in the sense of being the one who acts, in feeling located in a body, in the boundary between self and world, and a loss of any basic sense of self. In their words, such changes "could be transient or enduring, positive or distressing, enhancing or impairing". Some practitioners saw them as insight, others as depersonalisation. The more global the change, the more it impaired people's lives. More than half of those who reported changes were meditation teachers, and nearly half had no psychiatric or trauma history.

The study chose people who had had difficulties, so it cannot tell you how often these changes happen to meditators in general. It does show that the same kind of experience a teacher calls awakening can, for some people, be frightening and disabling.

#### Question: Open

Suppose that during one of this module's experiments you notice that your thoughts feel less like yours, or that you seem less located behind your eyes. What would make you stop?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner imagines noticing a change in sense of self during an experiment (thoughts feel less like theirs, less located behind the eyes) and says what would make them stop. A later question asks what would make it worth continuing, so focus on stop signals here. The learner read a summary of Lindahl and Britton 2019 on changes in sense of self among Western Buddhist meditators who reported meditation-related challenges (72% of 68 reported at least one; six kinds of change; changes could be positive or distressing, transient or enduring; more global changes went with more impairment; some saw them as insight, others as depersonalisation). They may also have read Deane, Miller and Wilkinson 2020, who argue that depersonalisation involves a loss of felt control with flattened, numb emotion, while sought-after selfless states involve peace and joy.

Help the learner make their criteria concrete. Useful signs in the readings: whether the change is pleasant or distressing, whether it passes when they stop practising, whether they can still work, sleep and relate to people (impairment), whether emotion feels richer and calmer or flat and absent, whether they feel more or less in control. A good answer also names what they would do: stop or reduce practice, talk to someone, seek help from a doctor or therapist or a service for meditators in difficulty such as Cheetah House (cheetahhouse.org). If the learner says they would push through distress because teachers call it progress, ask what evidence would make them change course. Do not diagnose and do not frighten. 80 to 130 words. One reply, then send them on. No generic praise.

\## Going further
A related risk is **spiritual bypassing**, a term the psychotherapist John Welwood coined for using spiritual ideas and practices to avoid facing emotional problems and human needs. In an interview with *Tricycle* ([Human Nature, Buddha Nature](https://www.johnwelwood.com/articles/TRIC_interview_uncut.pdf), optional, about 25 minutes) he speaks from Buddhism, but his point applies directly to this module's claim that happiness does not depend on anything. He argues that "absolute truth" can be used to dismiss "relative human needs", and that while one's deepest nature may not depend on anything, "our human embodiment" does.
