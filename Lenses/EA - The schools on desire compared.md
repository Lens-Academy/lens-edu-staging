| **Hobbes** | "Felicity is a continuall progresse of the desire, from one object to another" ([Leviathan ch. 11](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Leviathan_(1651)/Chapter_11)) | Keep desiring. There is no final rest | Argument | No evidence offered either way |

content::
\## Where the schools agree

These are our readings of the table, each tied to the rows that support it.

1. **Getting what you want does not reliably keep you satisfied.** Buddhism, Yoga, Epicurus (the "vain" desires), hedonic adaptation, the neuroscience of wanting and Nesse all say this in different words. Hobbes agrees about the facts and calls the endless chase happiness.
2. **Not all wanting is the problem.** Buddhism's wholesome desire, Epicurus's natural desires, the Stoics' preferred indifferents, self-determination theory's autonomous motivation and Zerwas's "aspiring" all mark out a kind of wanting that is fine or good.
3. **How you want matters, not only what you want.** Wanting the result or the doing (Jayasaro), wanting under pressure or by choice (Ryan and Deci), wanting what is up to you or what is not (Epictetus), aspiring to happiness or monitoring it (Zerwas).

\## Where they disagree

1. **What to do with craving.** Buddhism and Yoga: see through it until it ends. Stoics: desire only what is up to you. Epicurus: prune the unnecessary desires and satisfy the easy ones. Self-determination theory and Maslow: satisfy basic needs and pursue goals you endorse. Hobbes: keep wanting. Nesse: dissatisfaction is part of how the mind regulates goal pursuit, not a malfunction.
2. **Whether outer things matter.** Tay and Diener found that need fulfilment, including basic material needs, goes with wellbeing across 123 countries. The Stoics say externals are not good at all.
3. **Whether wanting can end.** Buddhism says yes ([SN 51.15](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn51/sn51.015.than.html)). In our reading, Nesse's account gives reasons to doubt it. Nobody can check the claim from outside, which is the problem of first-person evidence from module 3.

\## What evidence would decide it

1. Whether practice that weakens craving improves wellbeing beyond what any distraction does. This needs long-term controlled trials of repeated practice. The review quoted below says such studies are few.
2. Whether wanting and liking separate in everyday human desires and not just in addiction. [Pool et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2016.01.006) suggest that the way studies measured the two is the obstacle.
3. Whether valuing happiness hurts in general or only through constant monitoring, and only in some cultures. This needs large long-term studies that measure aspiring and monitoring separately, as Zerwas et al. began to do.
4. Whether extrinsic goals harm because of what they are or because of the pressured reasons people pursue them for. We think this is open: the passages you read do not separate the two.
5. Whether the Stoic and Epicurean practices work as claimed. There is almost no controlled evidence.

The review mentioned in point 1, by the psychologist Katy Tapper, covers 30 experiments on mindfulness and cravings for food, cigarettes and alcohol. It concludes that "whilst mindfulness strategies may bring about immediate reductions in craving, such effects are likely to stem from working memory load", that is, from keeping the mind busy, "and will not necessarily be superior to alternative strategies that also load working memory". But "a smaller number of studies show promising results where mindfulness exercises have been repeatedly practiced over a longer period of time", and these "provide tentative support for Buddhist models of craving" ([Tapper 2018](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/18719/), abstract).

\## Practice for the test
The test at the end of this part gives you a case and asks you to sort the person's wanting with three distinctions from this module. Practise on this case first:

Jonas is up for a promotion. He wants it mostly because his parents keep asking about his career and because he would feel like a failure if a younger colleague got it first. On good days he also enjoys the work itself, especially solving hard problems with his team.
