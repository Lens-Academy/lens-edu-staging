#### Text
content::
Jason Hausenloy argues that many AI safety projects can explain how their work produces a useful intermediate outcome, but not how that outcome contributes to actually solving the whole problem.

Read the first excerpt looking for the difference between a **theory of change** and a **theory of victory**. In particular, notice what a full theory of victory has to include that a local impact story can leave out.

#### Question
content::
Choose one “generically good” AI safety action from the excerpt—or another one you know. Sketch its local theory of change in one sentence. Then keep extending the chain until it reaches a genuine win condition.

Where are the weakest links? Name at least two steps performed by other actors and two assumptions that would have to hold.

assessment-instructions:: The student has read Jason Hausenloy's distinction between a theory of change and a theory of victory and is applying it to an AI safety action.

The key distinction:
- A theory of change gives a causal account from a particular action to an intermediate beneficial outcome.
- A theory of victory gives the full causal story from the combined actions of all relevant actors to an explicit win condition.
- A full pathway exposes dependencies, handoffs, assumptions, bottlenecks, and failure modes that “this is useful” can conceal.

Respond as a rigorous strategy coach. First restate the student's pathway as a compact causal chain. Then identify the 1–3 most load-bearing missing links or assumptions. Distinguish a genuine causal step from a label such as “policymakers act” or “alignment is solved.” Ask up to two targeted questions that would make the pathway more explicit.

Do not insist that a theory of victory must be certain or simple. The point is to make the fragile and uncertain parts legible. Do not endorse Hausenloy's strategic conclusions as settled fact.

Response length: 120–200 words. Short paragraphs; a compact arrow-chain is allowed. Avoid generic praise.

#### Text
content::
The next excerpt broadens the space of possible theories of victory, then proposes a method for developing “strategic taste”: decompose claims until you find concrete uncertainties or deference, resolve what you can, survey why existing efforts are insufficient, and write down the resulting assumptions.

As you read, keep your chosen pathway in mind. Look for a step that sounds persuasive at a high level but becomes uncertain when decomposed.

#### Question
content::
Now revise your pathway into a compact theory of victory. Include:

1. The win condition.
2. The causal steps from your proposed action to that condition, including handoffs to other actors.
3. The three most load-bearing assumptions.
4. The uncertainty or bottleneck you would investigate first.
5. One observation that would make you substantially reduce your confidence in the plan.

Finally, say whether the action still looks worth pursuing—and what changed in your view after making the pathway explicit.

assessment-instructions:: The student is constructing and stress-testing a theory of victory after reading Hausenloy's “decompose and resolve” method.

A strong response:
- States an actual end-state rather than an intermediate good.
- Gives a causal chain with explicit handoffs and dependencies.
- Identifies assumptions that are both uncertain and load-bearing.
- Names a tractable next uncertainty or bottleneck to investigate.
- Includes a meaningful falsifier or confidence-reducing observation.
- Updates the practical recommendation rather than merely listing caveats.

Help the student improve the weakest part of the chain. If they present slogans, ask what actor does what next and why. If they list risks without prioritizing them, ask which assumption is most decision-relevant. If they treat an assumption as certain, ask what evidence supports it and what would change their mind.

Do not grade the political desirability of their chosen win condition unless it is too vague to evaluate. Focus on causal completeness, explicit uncertainty, and action relevance.

Response length: 150–230 words. Be direct and specific. Avoid generic praise.
