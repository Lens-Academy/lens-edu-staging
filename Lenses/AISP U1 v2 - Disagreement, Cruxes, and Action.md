\## What if careful disagreement survives?

A tempting picture of rational debate says that informed people should converge if they share evidence, explain their arguments clearly, and have enough time.

AI risk gives us a useful test.

The Forecasting Research Institute brought together generalist forecasters and domain experts who disagreed sharply about AI-caused existential catastrophe.[^cite-fri-roots] The "concerned" group in the 2023 adversarial collaboration put much more probability on catastrophe by 2100 than the mostly superforecaster "skeptical" group. Participants spent substantial time reading, forecasting, discussing, and trying to identify the strongest reasons that could change their minds.

The disagreement barely closed.

That result should not be overgeneralized. The sample was small and selected. Long-run forecasts are hard to validate. Still, the study is useful because several easy explanations for disagreement look less satisfying after it. Participants engaged deeply. They could summarize the other side. Many had strong reasoning or forecasting credentials. Simple exposure to the opposing argument was not enough.

\## A disagreement can hide different questions

The FRI work found that the groups agreed on more than their top-level numbers suggested.[^cite-fri-roots] Both groups put high probability on very powerful AI eventually existing. Their larger disagreements concerned what happens next: how quickly capabilities become far beyond humans, how likely dangerous goals are, how hard human extinction would be to cause, and how well society would respond.

The groups also wanted different evidence. Concerned participants cared more about alignment progress and alignment failures. Skeptical participants put more weight on real-world demonstrations of dangerous capabilities and power-seeking behavior.

That matters because "find the crux" is not as simple as asking for one fact both sides would update on. People can disagree about which evidence should count.

The study's short-term cruxes did not explain most of the long-run gap. One of the strongest identified cruxes was expected to close only a small fraction of it.[^cite-fri-roots] The disagreement appears to involve longer-run expectations and deeper views about how much weight to put on theoretical arguments before strong empirical validation exists.

\## Worldviews enter earlier than we think

The FRI report describes a broad contrast. Skeptics tended to anchor more on a world that changes gradually and on skepticism toward long chains of theoretical reasoning. Concerned participants were more willing to treat large capability differences and theoretical models of misalignment as strong evidence even before those models had direct real-world confirmation.[^cite-fri-roots]

This is not a neat division between "empirical people" and "theoretical people". Both sides used both. The difference was about evidential weight.

AI safety contains many claims for which decisive evidence is unavailable by design. If we wait until a superintelligent system attempts takeover, the experiment is no longer very useful as a warning. Theoretical reasoning therefore has to carry some weight. Yet long chains of plausible steps can also create persuasive stories that are badly calibrated.

There is no universal formula for how much theory should count. A productive disagreement identifies where that judgment enters.

\## Cruxes are still useful when they do not create convergence

The FRI project defined cruxes as near-term questions whose resolution would cause substantial updates to long-run AI-risk beliefs.[^cite-fri-roots] This is a powerful idea even though no crux eliminated the whole disagreement.

A good crux has at least three properties. The outcome is observable. Different possible outcomes lead to meaningfully different updates. The update matters for a decision or belief we care about.

Consider dangerous-capability evaluations. If increasingly capable models repeatedly fail to show autonomy, replication, shutdown avoidance, or other dangerous properties, some threat models should become less plausible. If those capabilities appear earlier than expected, other views should update upward.

The goal is not to manufacture consensus. It is to create contact between abstract beliefs and future evidence.

\## Value of information

Cruxes connect directly to value of information.

Imagine two research projects. Project A would produce a beautiful measurement, but every plausible result leaves your policy choice unchanged. Project B is less academically exciting, but one result would make you support deployment and another would make you support a pause.

Project B can have higher decision value.

This helps prioritize research under uncertainty. Ask: **what result could actually change what we do?**

The FRI report explicitly develops metrics for the value of forecasting questions and invites researchers to find better cruxes.[^cite-fri-roots] Even when the strongest short-term indicators explain only a small part of the total disagreement, they can reveal which parts of the model are exposed to evidence and which parts rest on deeper assumptions.

\## When should you defer?

Disagreement also creates a personal problem. You may not be an expert on every step.

Deference makes sense when a field has demonstrated competence on the target question, experts have access to relevant information, incentives are reasonably aligned, and there is some track record that tells us what expertise predicts. AI existential risk is awkward because the question spans machine learning, economics, security, institutions, philosophy, and forecasting, while the final event has no direct historical frequency.

A machine-learning researcher can know more than you about current model training and still have no special authority over population ethics. A superforecaster can have a strong short-term track record and still face a horizon far outside the domain where that track record was measured. A philosopher can clarify the argument while knowing little about current capabilities.

This does not imply "trust nobody". It suggests decomposed deference. Defer locally where the expertise is real, then inspect how the pieces are combined.

\## Averaging is sometimes useful and sometimes meaningless

Suppose two groups give 2 percent and 20 percent. Taking 11 percent can be reasonable if the estimates behave like independent, similarly informed measurements. That condition often fails.

The groups may use the same data. One may have much better domain information. The disagreement may be about values rather than facts. The numbers may compress different causal models. A simple average can hide all of this.

The first question is therefore not "what is the midpoint?" It is "why are these numbers different?"

\## Action without consensus

A decision-maker eventually has to stop analysing and choose.

One practical response is to look for actions that survive several plausible views. Better incident reporting can help under organizational-risk and rogue-AI models. Capability evaluations can inform both skeptical and concerned views. Better biosecurity can matter under malicious-use scenarios independent of whether strong AI agency appears. Preserving institutional capacity can matter under gradual disempowerment and accumulative-risk models.

Robustness is not enough by itself. Some robust actions are tiny. Some high-impact actions depend on taking a stand on a disputed model. The purpose of robustness is to identify places where disagreement need not block progress.

Your final exercise in this unit asks you to write down your own model, the claims carrying most of its weight, the evidence that could change it, and one action that still looks reasonable if an important part of the model is wrong.

That is the standard we will carry into the rest of the course. We are not trying to finish philosophy before acting. We are trying to make the connection between uncertainty and action explicit.

Think of one claim in your current AI-risk model. What near-term observation would be most useful for changing your belief about it? Would both a skeptic and a concerned person agree that the observation is informative?

\## Phase 3: Learning Question

Two groups disagree about a long-run AI risk. They understand each other's arguments, share most current evidence, and still differ by an order of magnitude. A policymaker proposes averaging their probabilities and moving on.

Explain why averaging can lose important information. Then describe one better way to use the disagreement for a decision.

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

50 points: The answer explains that top-level probabilities can compress different causal models, evidential standards, priors, values, or correlated evidence, so a midpoint can hide the source of disagreement.

50 points: The answer gives a defensible alternative use of the disagreement, such as decomposing the model, identifying a crux, gathering discriminating information, deferring locally to relevant expertise, or choosing an action robust across the live views.

feedback-instructions::
Give 100 to 170 words. State whether the learner preserved the information inside the disagreement. Below full credit, identify the missing reason or the weakness in the proposed alternative.
