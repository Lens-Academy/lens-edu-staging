summary_for_tutor: "Optional lens of the early Buddhism library module, on a debate about anatta. (1) Thanissaro Bhikkhu, 'No-self or Not-self?': the Buddha refused to answer whether there is a self; holding either view is wrong view; the question should be put aside because any notion of self involves clinging and stress. Anatta is a not-self strategy: ask 'Is this really me, myself, or mine? If it's stressful but not really me or mine, why hold on?' (2) Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 'Buddha' (Mark Siderits): some (e.g. Albahari 2006) point out the Buddha never categorically denies a self beyond the five aggregates, but all of classical Indian philosophy, Buddhist and orthodox, understood him to deny the self outright. Others read non-self as practical advice not to identify with transitory things, not metaphysics. Section 3 reconstructs the discourse the learner read (SN 22.59 = S III.66-8) as two arguments: from impermanence (if there were a self it would be permanent, none of the five aggregates is permanent, so there is no self) and from control (if there were a self one could never desire it be changed, each aggregate can be desired to change, so no self). The arguments give reason to deny a self that grounds identity over time or serves as a locus of control, given the assumption that the person is no more than the aggregates. (3) Bhikkhu Sujato's 2019 SuttaCentral essay 'On not-self, existence, and ontological strategies': the not-self versus no-self distinction is not found in Pali; Thanissaro relies heavily on SN 44.10 (Vacchagotta); Sujato quotes Bhikkhu Bodhi's footnote in his Connected Discourses rejecting both a transcendent self and the 'strategy of perception' reading: the Buddha did not say 'there is no self' (i) because annihilationists used that expression and (ii) to avoid confusing those attached to self, and 'all phenomena are nonself' precludes a transcendent self. Sujato's tone is polemical. Question: wedge."

content::
In the second lens, the Buddha says of each part of body and mind: "This is not mine. This is not my self. This is not what I am." The Pali word for this teaching is **anatta**, usually translated "not-self" or "no-self". Does it mean there is no self at all? Teachers within Theravada disagree. First, Thanissaro Bhikkhu, who says the question itself should be put aside.

#### Question: Open
id:: 43bd49cb-9818-4348-a11c-2dbafa4e5ad9
content::
Someone says: "Thanissaro and the Stanford Encyclopedia just disagree about the facts, so one of them must have misread the text." Is that right?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Thanissaro Bhikkhu's "No-self or Not-self?" and two passages from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Buddha" (the debate over whether the Buddha denied the self, and the arguments from impermanence and from control, reconstructed from SN 22.59). The claim to examine: the two simply disagree about the facts and one misread the text.

Points a strong answer finds (two are enough):
- They disagree less about the words than about what the discourse is for. Thanissaro: the Buddha put the question "is there a self?" aside, and anatta is a strategy for letting go of what causes stress. SEP: the discourse contains philosophical arguments whose conclusion is that there is no self, and all of classical Indian philosophy read the Buddha that way.
- The SEP itself reports both readings: some point out the Buddha never categorically denies a self beyond the five aggregates, and others read non-self as practical advice. So it is not simply Thanissaro against the texts.
- The SEP notes the arguments only work given the assumption that there is no more to a person than the five aggregates. The discourse does not state that assumption, which is exactly where Thanissaro's reading has room.
- Both can be partly right: the teaching can be used as a strategy and still rest on, or imply, a claim about what exists. Bhikkhu Bodhi's note, quoted by Sujato, rejects the pure 'strategy of perception' reading but also says the Buddha avoided the words 'there is no self' for two reasons: the annihilationists used that expression, and it would confuse people attached to the idea of self. Bodhi adds that 'all phenomena are nonself' rules out a transcendent self.
- What would decide it: how other discourses use the term, how the earliest commentators read it, and, for the learner, whether the strategy works whatever the metaphysics.

Per reply: answer direct questions directly. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in one or two sentences, name one gap, and ask one direct follow-up (for example: "Which premise of the argument from impermanence would Thanissaro say the Buddha never asserted?"). 90 to 150 words. After 2 replies, close with a brief summary. If stuck after two attempts, give a short answer and move on. Do not say which reading is correct. No generic praise.
