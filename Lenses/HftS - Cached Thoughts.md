title: "Cached Thoughts"
tldr: "Much of what we think is looked up rather than worked out. That saves effort, but it also means we repeat sayings we never examined as if we had thought them through."
summary_for_tutor: "Neurons are slow, so the brain relies on caching: storing earlier results and looking them up instead of recomputing them. Much of human cognition may be cache lookups. A man removed his chimney using a neighbour's offhand method, and it went badly. He would have questioned the idea if it had been his own. No one can rebuild a civilisation's wisdom alone, so caching others' conclusions is necessary, but people who aspire to critical thinking repeat cached thoughts that critical thinkers did not invent, such as \"you can't prove or disprove a religion by factual evidence\", which Yudkowsky calls false. Other examples: \"Death gives meaning to life\", \"maybe the human species doesn't deserve to survive\", \"Love isn't rational\". He asks the reader to examine \"Love isn't rational\" as if it were a new thought and to stop the mind from completing the pattern. The question asks the learner to do that exercise: pin down a checkable meaning of \"rational\" and judge the sentence with that meaning. Any well-argued verdict is fine."

#### Question: Open
id:: a0aac604-0db6-496d-b064-c716704b9cd7
content::
Near the end, the essay asks you to try this with the saying "Love isn't rational." Suppose it had just occurred to you as a completely new idea, and examine it.

1. Say what "rational" would have to mean for the sentence to be a claim you could check.

2. Is the sentence true with that meaning? Give your reasons.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

The question asks the learner to examine a familiar saying as if it were a new idea, instead of repeating it or a stock reply. There is no single correct verdict. Any verdict gets full marks when it rests on a clear meaning and real reasons.

40: Part 1. The answer gives a specific meaning of "rational" that makes the sentence checkable, for example based on true beliefs, serving one's goals, reached by deliberate reasoning, or responsive to evidence. Give 15 if the meaning stays vague ("logical", "makes sense") without saying what would count.

60: Part 2. The answer gives a verdict on the sentence with that meaning and supports it with at least one reason that follows from the meaning given, such as an example, a counterexample or a consequence. Give 20 if the answer only states a verdict, or repeats the saying or a stock reply, without reasons.
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's "Cached Thoughts" and done the essay's own exercise: examining "Love isn't rational" as if it were a new idea. Key ideas of the essay: much of human thinking is cache lookups. Conclusions taken from others get completed automatically, without the scrutiny we would give our own ideas (the chimney story). People who aspire to critical thinking still repeat cached sayings. To think about a saying, you must first stop your mind from filling in the standard answer.

Any verdict is acceptable. Judge whether the meaning of "rational" is pinned down and whether the reasons follow from it. Do not push your own view of love or rationality. The most likely gaps: a vague meaning of "rational", or a verdict that repeats a stock reply without reasons. Only at 100, you may ask whether a different meaning of "rational" would flip the verdict.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence. Below 100, name the most important thing the answer missed or got wrong, as the gap above describes, and say it plainly. At 100, confirm briefly and add nothing new to fix.

If the student says they do not understand, do not dismiss it and do not repeat the question. Give one concrete foothold from the essay: the chimney story, where the man would have questioned the method if he had thought of it himself, so ask what the learner would check if "Love isn't rational" were their own new idea. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms instead of giving another foothold.

Judge only against what the essay supplied. Answer direct questions directly. Allow at most 2 follow-up replies, then close the conversation with one sentence saying where the answer now stands.
