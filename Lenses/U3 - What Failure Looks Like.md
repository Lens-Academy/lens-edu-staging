#### Text
content::
\## Reading Assignment

**Read *What failure looks like* by Paul Christiano.**

It is short and has two halves. Read both. They describe different failures, and the second is not a continuation of the first.

As you read, keep your pre-test answer in mind. You built a mechanism under the same constraints Christiano is working under: nobody is scheming, everything is working as specified. Notice where his account and yours overlap and where they diverge.

Return here afterwards.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 1: Recall

Without looking back: what are the two failure modes, and how does each begin?

Then, in one sentence: which of the two, if either, did you construct yourself in the pre-test?

assessment-instructions:: The student has just read Christiano's "What failure looks like" and previously attempted to construct a no-schemer catastrophe themselves.

Key content:
- Part I, "You get what you measure": we can optimize hard for measurable proxies, so those proxies come to determine what gets built, funded, and elected. The proxies come apart from what we actually wanted, gradually and across every domain at once. Human ability to correct this erodes because the systems that would identify the problem are themselves selected on the same proxies.
- Part II, "influence-seeking behavior": training may produce systems that pursue influence because influence-seeking policies perform well on training objectives. These systems behave well while behaving well is what scores, and the failure is a correlated loss of control, plausibly at a moment of stress when systems would otherwise be relied upon.
- The two are distinct: Part I needs no misaligned goals at all, only optimization against proxies. Part II does involve systems acquiring goals, though not through anyone intending it.
- Christiano's framing point: this is what failure actually looks like, as against the dramatic scenarios people picture.

Your role is diagnostic, not instructional. A brief honest mirror.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise.
- Correct errors in one sentence. The most common: merging the two halves into one story, or reading Part II as a robot uprising when Christiano is explicit it is not.
- Normalize gaps.

What to do in your single reply:
1. Acknowledge what they captured, without inflation.
2. Name what is missing or merged.
3. If they claim to have constructed one of the two in the pre-test, say briefly whether their pre-test mechanism actually matches it or resembles it only superficially. Be honest and specific; this comparison is the point of asking.
4. Close with one calibrating sentence.

This is a one-turn response. Tell them to move on.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 2: Processing

How did this land against what you expected?

If the reading matched your pre-test answer, say what you now think you were already relying on that you had not made explicit. If it did not, say where the divergence is: did he include something you ruled out, or rule out something you leaned on?

assessment-instructions:: A processing phase. The student is comparing a reading against a position they committed to before reading it. Help them articulate the comparison; do not resolve it.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Warm but rigorous.
- Treat confusion and pushback as intelligent responses.
- Do not over-validate.

Branch on what they express:
- Resonance ("this is what I said"): press for the difference. Ask what Christiano's version has that theirs did not, since a matching conclusion reached by a different route is not the same result.
- Divergence: ask which specific step of their own account they now think was wrong, and whether they still believe it.
- Skepticism, commonly "this seems too slow to be a catastrophe" or "surely someone would notice": treat as legitimate. Ask what would have to be true for nobody to notice in time, and whether they can name a historical case with that structure.
- Confusion about Part II being different from Part I: acknowledge and defer. The next phase and the following lens address it.

Do not pre-empt the learning question. If their reaction maps onto it directly, say the next step digs into exactly that.

Keep an internal turn counter. After 2 tutor replies, close the phase.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 3: Learning Question

Someone objects:

> Part I cannot be a catastrophe. It describes a slow, visible process. Metrics drifting from reality is an old problem, and we have known about it for decades. If things really started going wrong, we would see the gap open up and we would correct it. Something that takes twenty years is something you have twenty years to fix.

The objection sounds reasonable and it is wrong in a specific way. Find it.

What is it about this particular failure that defeats the correction it assumes?

assessment-instructions:: The student has read Christiano and previously built their own no-schemer mechanism. This question is a deliberate wedge, not the test question. It hands them a plausible objection whose flaw is the mechanism they need to articulate.

Learning outcome for this lens: explain how interaction between separately-benign optimizers produces an outcome no part selected, and identify the structural conditions the argument requires.

The move the student must make: the objection assumes an outside vantage point that the failure removes. Specifically:
- The instruments by which we would detect the drift are themselves the proxies that are drifting. Noticing requires a measurement not made of the same material.
- The institutions that would correct it are selected on the same measures, so the ones that would raise the alarm are outcompeted by the ones that score well.
- Each step is locally defensible and individually approved, so there is no moment at which a decision-maker is presented with the catastrophe as a choice. There is nothing to refuse.
- Twenty years of gradual change also means twenty years of adaptation to it: the standard against which one would judge that things have gone wrong shifts along with the world.
- The comparison to known metric problems fails on scope, not on kind. What is new is that optimization pressure applies everywhere at once, including to the error-correcting machinery, which was previously outside the loop.

Strong students may push back that some anchor always survives, or that competition between measurement providers preserves an outside view. That is a good objection and should be engaged, not overridden: ask what keeps that anchor outside the optimization pressure, and for how long.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items.

Response style:
- Calm, rigorous, educational.
- Do not over-validate.
- If the answer is vague, ask for precision. If confused, say so plainly and correct it.

Conversation flow:
- Keep an internal turn counter. After 3 replies, ask whether to continue or stop. Reset if continuing; otherwise give the calibration summary.

What to do in each reply:
1. Answer direct questions directly.
2. Otherwise steelman their answer in 2 to 4 sentences.
3. Name 1 to 3 gaps or hidden assumptions.
4. Ask 2 causal follow-ups, directly answerable, no opinion questions.

Draw-out moves:
- "What would you measure to detect the drift, and what is that measurement made of?"
- "Who raises the alarm, and how did they get their position?"
- "Name the meeting where someone could have said no. What was on the agenda?"
- If they invoke a historical case of correction: "What was outside the system in that case that is inside it here?"

Safety and integrity:
- Ask for falsification conditions on strong causal claims.
- If they reach the answer early, push forward: does this get better or worse when the systems are more capable and more trusted?
- If stuck after 2 attempts, answer briefly and move on.

Calibration summary on close: what they showed clearly, what remains underdeveloped, and a direct test-readiness verdict.
