\## Who this is for

This course is for people who want to think better and then act on it. Many people who take it also take AI risk seriously and want to work on it, and that is one of their reasons to want to think better. So some examples come from that life: deciding what to work on, a research project that has stalled, an application you keep putting off, a disagreement with a colleague. Just as many come from ordinary life. You do not need any background in AI safety or in rationality.

\## Where it comes from

The Center for Applied Rationality (CFAR) is a non-profit that runs workshops on applied rationality: practical techniques for thinking more clearly about your own life and then acting on what you conclude. Its classic workshop runs over four days: an opening evening, then classes on one technique after another, with practice on each participant's own problems.[^cite-cfar-schedule] The workshops came with a participant handbook, refined mainly by Duncan Sabien, a former CFAR staff member.[^cite-cfar-handbook-2021] Duncan Sabien and the LessWrong team later published it on LessWrong as a sequence of posts, and that version is the reading for this course.[^cite-cfar-intro-2022]

This course follows the shape of that four-day workshop, stretched over six weeks. It is Lens Academy's own interpretation, not a CFAR course. The techniques are CFAR's and the handbook text is Duncan Sabien's. How we adapted them, and any mistakes in that, are ours.

\## How the six weeks work

- **Your bugs list.** This week you write a list of "bugs": things in your life that frustrate you, and things that could be great. Every later week shows you this list and asks you to pick one bug to work on with that week's technique. At CFAR workshops, building this list came on the first evening, so that later sessions could practise on real problems.[^cite-cfar-schedule]
- **Your Hamming question.** This week you also name the most important problem in your life right now, and what keeps you from working on it. You come back to it at the end of the course.
- **One technique at a time.** Each class page explains what a technique is for, gives you the handbook's own text, and then has you practise it on one of your bugs. Practice takes more of your time than reading. One participant at CFAR's 2025 workshop liked best the classes that started with about 20 minutes of examples and then gave about 40 minutes to practice in pairs.[^cite-penguin-2026]
- **The AI tutor as your partner.** At CFAR workshops, participants helped each other work through bugs in pairs, which CFAR calls "pair debugging". Here the tutor plays that partner. It asks questions and keeps you on the technique's steps. It does not solve your bug for you, and it does not lecture. You can tell it when a technique does not fit you.
- **A weekly experiment.** Each week ends with a plan to try one technique in your real life, and the next week starts by asking what actually happened. We think the hardest part of any technique is still using it once the class is over, and this loop is there for that.
- **Real change, not liking.** We ask you to judge each technique by whether it changed something on a real bug, not by whether you enjoyed the class.
- **Meetings, or on your own.** If you are in a group, you meet for 90 minutes each week to work on each other's real problems in small rooms. If you take the course alone, each meeting has a version you can do in 45 to 60 minutes with the [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor).

Plan for about two to three hours a week.

\## Trust your own judgment

Two things from that introduction shape this course. The handbook was a supplement to doing things, so every class here ends with you doing the technique. And it is a snapshot from around 2017, so read it as a starting point, not as a finished manual.

Each technique post starts with an epistemic status: the handbook's own label for how well tested the technique is, from "established" to "preliminary". We point these out where they matter. A technique with a weak label can still be worth trying, but you should hold it more loosely.

The final call is yours. In 2025, Anna Salamon of CFAR wrote that the workshops should keep reminding people that "the buck stops with you" and to check whether a technique is "producing fruits you directly feel good about".[^cite-salamon-2025] The techniques in this course are scaffolding. Try each one properly, as written, at least once. Then adapt it until it fits you, or drop it, and say why. The next page has the handbook's own advice on how to do that.
