title: "Mind Projection Fallacy"
tldr: Some properties you see in things are facts about your own mind. Old science fiction covers showed aliens carrying off human women, as if attractiveness lived in the woman and not in the mind of whoever looks at her.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky uses old science fiction magazine covers of bug-eyed aliens carrying off women in torn dresses. The artists did not ask whether a non-human alien would find a human woman attractive, because they experienced sexiness as a property of the woman herself (Woman.sexiness, like height or weight) rather than as something in the mind of whoever looks at her. E. T. Jaynes called this the Mind Projection Fallacy, projecting properties of your own mind onto the outside world. Jaynes was mostly concerned with probabilities treated as properties of objects instead of states of knowledge in a mind. The essay says the same error appears in the argument over the meaning of "sound". The question gives Maya's claim that spiders are creepy "as a fact about spiders", so birds must avoid them too, and asks where her reasoning goes wrong.

#### Question: Open
id:: d579564b-016f-4db8-8a6e-68caacb92b8b
content::
Maya says: "Spiders are creepy. That's just a fact about spiders. So the birds in my garden must stay away from them too."

Using the essay's idea, explain where Maya's reasoning goes wrong.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay describes the Mind Projection Fallacy, treating a property of your own mind as if it were a property of the thing in the world. Its example: artists drew aliens attracted to human women because they saw attractiveness as part of the woman, not as a reaction in a particular kind of mind, and so never asked whether an alien with a different evolutionary history would react the same way.

60: The answer says that creepiness is not a property of spiders but a reaction in Maya's (or people's) minds when they see spiders. She treats her reaction as a feature of the spider.

40: The answer says that because of this, nothing follows about birds: birds have different minds, so whether a bird reacts to a spider the way Maya does depends on the bird, not on the spider being "creepy".

Cap at 40 if the answer's only objection is that Maya lacks evidence about birds, without saying that the creepiness is in her mind rather than in the spider.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Creepiness isn't something spiders have, the way they have eight legs. It's how Maya's mind reacts to them. She treats her own reaction as a fact about the spider, so she assumes any creature will react the same way. But a bird has a very different mind, and nothing about spiders being 'creepy' to her says anything about how a bird sees them."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Mind Projection Fallacy" and applied it to Maya's claim about spiders and birds. The essay's key ideas: we often represent a reaction of our own mind as a property of the object (Woman.sexiness, like height). The pulp artists never asked whether an alien with a different evolutionary history would find a human woman attractive, because to them she simply was attractive. Jaynes named this the Mind Projection Fallacy. Here Maya treats her own reaction as a property of spiders and so expects birds to share it.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap, usually saying only that birds are different without saying that the creepiness is in Maya's mind and not in the spider. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example where the creepiness is when no one is looking at the spider. At 100, confirm briefly.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as the alien and the woman in the torn dress, and ask what the artist forgot to ask. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
