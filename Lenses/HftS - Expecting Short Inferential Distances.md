title: "Expecting Short Inferential Distances"
tldr: An explanation that feels complete to you may skip many steps your listener lacks. Go only one step back, and a listener several steps away will think you are wrong or arrogant, and you will think they are not listening.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky argues that our ancestors lived in small bands without writing, where everyone shared the same background knowledge, so explanations were rarely more than one inferential step from what everyone knew. Our instincts still expect this. A claim with no obvious support looks like a lie or stupidity, and someone who does not see an obvious point looks stupid or obstinate. Today, expert knowledge can be many steps from shared premises, so explainers take one step back when they need several, and listeners expect things to be visible in one step. His example is a biologist who says evolution is "the simplest explanation" to someone who does not share the history behind Occam's Razor. The listener thinks the biologist is arrogant, and the biologist thinks the listener is an idiot. A clear argument lays out a path from what the audience already knows. The question gives a doctor and a patient who misjudge each other over antibiotics and asks what went wrong on each side and how the doctor should explain instead.

#### Question: Open
id:: 6ed9f642-1246-4bf5-8eb2-047abd4f3221
content::
A doctor tells a patient: "Take all the antibiotics, even after you feel better, or you'll breed resistant bacteria." The patient has never heard that bacteria can change over time. To him, stopping a medicine once you feel fine is common sense, so he decides the doctor is just being bossy. The doctor, in turn, decides the patient is not listening.

1. Using the essay, explain what went wrong on each side.

2. Say how the doctor should change the explanation.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay argues that people instinctively expect short inferential distances, as if everyone shared their background knowledge. Explainers take one step back when the listener needs several. Listeners who hear a claim without visible support conclude the speaker is wrong, crazy or arrogant, and speakers whose obvious point is not accepted conclude the listener is stupid or obstinate. A clear argument starts from what the audience already knows and lays out every step from there. In this scenario the doctor's reason ("resistant bacteria") depends on background the patient does not have.

35: The doctor's side. The doctor gave a reason that relies on knowledge the patient lacks, going only one step back when the patient needed several, and then blamed the patient instead of seeing the gap.

35: The patient's side. The patient heard a claim with no support he could see and, instead of thinking the doctor might know something he does not, concluded the doctor was being bossy.

30: The fix. The doctor should start from what the patient already knows or accepts and go through the missing steps one at a time. The answer does not need correct biology for the steps, only the approach. An answer that only says "explain more clearly" or "be more patient" without starting from what the patient knows gets 10.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The doctor took one step back when the patient needed several. 'Resistant bacteria' only makes sense if you already know bacteria can change. The patient heard an order with no reason he could follow, so it sounded bossy, and the doctor read his doubt as not listening. Both acted as if the gap were small. The doctor should start from what the patient knows, for example that feeling better doesn't mean all the bacteria are gone, and build up from there one step at a time."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Expecting Short Inferential Distances" and diagnosed a doctor and patient who misjudge each other. The essay's key ideas: in the ancestral environment everyone shared background knowledge, so we still expect explanations to be one step from what everyone knows. Explainers go one step back when they need several. A claim without visible support looks crazy or arrogant to the listener, and a listener who does not see an obvious point looks stupid to the speaker. A clear argument starts from what the audience already knows. Here the doctor's reason depends on knowing that bacteria change.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap, often blaming only one side, or a fix like "explain better" that does not start from what the patient knows. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example what the patient would need to know first for "resistant bacteria" to make sense. Do not grade the medical details. At 100, confirm briefly.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as the biologist who says evolution is "the simplest explanation". If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
