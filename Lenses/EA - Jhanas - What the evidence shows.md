There are three kinds of evidence about the jhanas. **First-person reports** come from the texts, teachers and practitioners you have read so far. **Brain studies** record what happens in the brain while experts are in jhana. **Outcome data** would show whether learning the jhanas changes people's lives afterwards. This lens covers the last two.

The first brain study of jhana, published in 2013, recorded a single meditator.

Both studies look at experts while they are in jhana. Neither tests whether learning the jhanas makes people happier, or less dependent on sense pleasures, in the weeks and months afterwards. In our searches (October 2026) we found no controlled study of that question. Jhourney's CEO writes that the company cannot yet make "causal claims about long-term benefits" ([Jhourney, 2026](https://jhourney.io/blog/what-are-the-jhanas)). The only outcome data we found is a survey Jhourney ran among its own past participants, below. Module 3 covers how to weigh evidence like this.

#### Question: Open
id:: 90bfc29f-7560-4097-a3e7-0b071e36fe52
content::
Without looking back, write down what you remember from the three readings.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read parts of Hagerty et al. 2013, Yang et al. 2025 (preprint) and Jhourney's "Life in HD" survey, and wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- Hagerty 2013: one meditator (53, about 6,000 hours over 17 years, trained by Ayya Khema), fMRI and EEG. Brain changes in regions matching the reported features of jhana (less activity in visual, auditory and language areas, more in attention areas), appearing promptly after entering jhana, and activation of the nucleus accumbens, part of the reward system. The authors call it self-stimulation of a reward system by internal mental processes. They advise caution with voluntary reward stimulation, note that meditators report needing less effort over time and no withdrawal, and that reward activity fell below rest in the fifth jhana.
- Yang 2025, preprint: 20 experts (about 17 years and 20,500 hours of practice on average, 18 intermediate style and 2 deep), 7T fMRI across eight jhana states. Reproducible changes across people, linked to equanimity, attentional stability and behaviour. Limitations: scanner setting, no EEG, may not generalise, deep states hard to hold in the scanner, longitudinal training studies needed.
- Life in HD: 61 self-selected alumni and 10 interviews. Those who accessed jhana were twice as likely to report changes in relationships and lifestyle, 1.5 times as likely in thoughts and beliefs, nearly 8 times as likely to meditate more. The afterglow faded in days to weeks. 6.5% (4 people) reported negative changes, nearly all among those who did not access jhana. The authors call their sample small and self-selected.
- No controlled study of lasting effects on wellbeing was found.

#### Question: Open
id:: 977de924-9ecf-4adb-b68f-607e445240a7
content::
A friend says: "Brain scans show that jhana switches on the brain's reward system without any outside stimulus. So it is now scientifically proven that learning the jhanas makes you lastingly happier and less dependent on outside pleasures."

What is wrong with this argument?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner was given a plausible but flawed argument: brain scans show jhana activates the reward system without outside stimulus, so it is proven that learning the jhanas makes you lastingly happier and less dependent on outside pleasures. This is practice for the test at the end of this part of the module.

Points a strong answer finds (any two are good):
- State versus lasting change: the scans record experts during the state. They say nothing about happiness outside the state or months later. No controlled study of lasting effects was found.
- Who was studied: one expert (Hagerty) and 20 experts with thousands of hours (Yang). Not people learning the jhanas, so the results may not apply to a learner.
- Reward activity during a state is not the same as being less dependent on outside pleasures. That claim would need a measure of behaviour or craving over time.
- The only outcome data is a small, self-selected survey by a company that sells the retreats, with self-reported change and no random assignment, and its own authors say effects faded in days to weeks.
- Hagerty's own authors advise caution about voluntary reward stimulation, so the paper does not present it as simply good.

Do not say the claim is false: say it is not shown. Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in one or two sentences, name one gap, and ask one direct follow-up question (for example: "Who was in the scanner, and were they learners?"). 100 to 160 words. After 2 replies, close with a short summary and a verdict on whether they are ready for the test at the end of this part. If the learner is stuck after two attempts, give the answer briefly and move on. No generic praise.

\## If you want more
The same Harvard group has published several single-practitioner studies. The most detailed scanned one meditator with over 23,000 hours of practice many times with fMRI and EEG, and linked the brain data to his ratings of bliss ([Chowdhury et al., 2025, *NeuroImage*](https://meditation.mgh.harvard.edu/files/Chowdhury_24_NeuroImage.pdf)). It is one person (n = 1), so it has the same limits as Hagerty's study.
