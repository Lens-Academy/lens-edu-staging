The essay opens with one student's record: 4-6-2 No, 4-6-8 Yes, 10-12-14 Yes. Subjects test triplets their hypothesis says should fit, hear Yes, and confidently announce rules like \"increasing by two\" or X-2X-3X. Only about 20% find the real rule, which is any three numbers in ascending order. Finding it requires testing triplets that should not fit, such as 20-23-26. Yudkowsky calls this positive bias and separates it from trying to preserve a belief. Phlogiston could explain a flame going out and also its not going out. The strength of a hypothesis is what it cannot explain. The bias is below the level of words, so knowing about it is not enough: you have to learn to look toward the zero. #### Question: Open
id:: 3d53574d-507b-4b36-af3e-962967fdc70b
content::
Go back to the student's record at the start of the essay: 4-6-2 No, 4-6-8 Yes, 10-12-14 Yes. Suppose the student's guess was "each number is two more than the one before."

1. Why were 4-6-8 and 10-12-14 weak tests of that guess?

2. Give one triplet the student should have tested next, and say what a "Yes" and what a "No" would each have told them.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

The facts: the real rule is any three numbers in ascending order. A test only tells you something about a guess if a plausible alternative rule predicts a different answer. Triplets the guess says should fit also fit many broader rules, so a Yes was expected either way.

50: Part 1. Both triplets fit the guess and also fit other rules (for example the real one, any ascending numbers), so a Yes was expected whichever rule was true, and the answers could not tell the guess apart from the alternatives. Give 25 if the answer only says the student tested examples that fit their guess, without saying why that cannot tell the guess from other rules.

50: Part 2. A triplet that the guess says should get a No but that a broader rule such as ascending numbers would accept, for example 1-2-3, 20-23-26 or 2-4-100 (25). Its two outcomes explained: a Yes would show the guess is wrong (the rule is broader), a No would support the guess over that alternative (25). A triplet that both the guess and ascending order reject, such as 6-4-2, gets at most 10 of the first 25, because its answer cannot tell those two rules apart.
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's "Positive Bias: Look Into the Dark" and returned to the 2-4-6 record from its opening. Key ideas of the essay: people test triplets their hypothesis says should fit, hear Yes, and announce wrong rules with confidence. The real rule is any ascending numbers. Finding it takes triplets that should not fit, such as 20-23-26. The strength of a hypothesis is what it cannot explain (phlogiston explained a flame going out and not going out). Knowing about the bias is not enough, you have to learn to look toward the zero.

The most likely gaps: not saying that a Yes was expected under other rules too, or a proposed triplet that the guess and ascending order would treat the same.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence. Below 100, name the most important thing the answer missed or got wrong, as the gap above describes, and say it plainly. At 100, confirm briefly and add nothing new to fix.

If the student says they do not understand, do not dismiss it and do not repeat the question. Give one concrete foothold from the essay: the triplet 20-23-26 from the essay, and what the "add two" rule says it should get. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms instead of giving another foothold.

Judge only against what the essay supplied. Answer direct questions directly. Allow at most 2 follow-up replies, then close the conversation with one sentence saying where the answer now stands.
