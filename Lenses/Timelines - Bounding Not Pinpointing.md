\## Reading Assignment

Two readings by the same author, in this order. The second is a correction to how people read the first.

**First, read Holden Karnofsky's *Forecasting transformative AI: the "biological anchors" method in a nutshell*.** Biological anchors estimate when transformative AI arrives by asking how much computation a human brain performs, or how much computation evolution used to produce one, and then asking when training runs of comparable size become affordable. The method is more careful than that summary; read for how the assumptions are made explicit and testable.

**Second, read Karnofsky's *"Biological anchors" is about bounding, not pinpointing, AI timelines*.**

This is unusual and worth noticing as you read it: an author whose model was widely cited, writing publicly to explain that it does not establish what readers took it to establish. Ask yourself while reading what the model DOES establish, since he does not think the answer is nothing.

Return here after both.

\## Phase 1: Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you can remember from both readings, without looking back. The mechanics of the method, the assumptions, the correction, the numbers. No need to organize it. Speech to text is recommended.

assessment-instructions:: The student has just read Karnofsky's two biological-anchors pieces and written a free recall without looking back.

Key content:
- The bio-anchors method estimates transformative AI timelines by anchoring the compute required to train such a system to a biological reference point: the computation performed by a human brain over a lifetime, or the computation performed by evolution in producing brains, among several anchors.
- It combines that estimate with projections of compute cost declines and willingness to spend, yielding a probability distribution over arrival dates. Karnofsky's headline figure is greater than 10 percent by 2036.
- The method's virtue is that its assumptions are explicit and individually arguable, so a disagreeing reader can locate exactly which parameter they disagree about, rather than trading intuitions.
- The correction: the method bounds rather than pinpoints. The anchors are better at establishing that transformative AI is not wildly implausible on a given timescale than at locating when it arrives. Evolution-scale anchors in particular function as a bound on how much compute could conceivably be needed, given that a blind process achieved it.
- What it does establish, on Karnofsky's account, is that the burden of proof shifts: someone claiming transformative AI is very far away must argue against a bound derived from an existence proof, rather than merely expressing skepticism.

Your role is diagnostic, not instructional. Act as a brief, honest mirror.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent recall, well done).
- Correct errors in one sentence. Name gaps briefly without lecturing. Normalize incomplete recall.

In your single reply: acknowledge what they captured, name what was missing, correct errors plainly. If they recalled the method in detail but recalled the second piece only as "it has limitations," name that specifically as the gap, since the shape of the limitation is what the next phases turn on. Close with one calibrating sentence.

Do not re-teach, do not ask follow-up questions, do not invite dialogue. One-turn response. Tell them to move on.

\## Phase 2: Processing
Go back to what you wrote in the previous lens, your answer to "what is my estimate an answer *to*."

Take 2 minutes: has that answer changed? What did it feel like to read an author dismantle the interpretation of his own model? And is there something unsatisfying about a method that gives you a bound rather than a date? Just capture the reaction.

assessment-instructions:: The student committed, in the previous lens, to a classification of their own estimate; that lens was built to draw out "this is when it happens", so expect a location. They have now read Karnofsky's method and his correction to it.

This is a processing phase, not a teaching phase. Help them articulate the reaction. Do not resolve it.

The learning outcome for the next phase is: given an unfamiliar quantitative estimate, determine whether the method can locate the answer or only bound it from one side, state which side and what assumption puts it there, and name a case where the bound is uninformative.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Warm but rigorous.
- Treat doubt and dissatisfaction as intelligent responses, not failures.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise.
- Do not pre-empt the next phase.

Branch on what they express:
- If they find the bound unsatisfying and want a date: this is the reaction the phase is built for, and it is productive. Treat it as legitimate and ask what they would do differently, as a decision-maker, if they had a date rather than a bound. Do not resolve whether the desire is reasonable.
- If they say the model is therefore useless: legitimate stance, engage it. Ask what a bound rules out, and whether ruling things out has any decision value.
- If they were struck by the author correcting himself: ask what they think made that necessary, and whether the original piece said anything false.
- If they claim they always understood it as a bound: accept it without challenge and ask what in their earlier answer showed that.

Conversation flow:
- Keep an internal turn counter. After 2 tutor replies, close: "Good. Next step, we do this to an estimate built the opposite way round."

Do not resolve with a mini-lecture, do not adjudicate whether bio-anchors is a good method, do not run past 2 turns.

\## Phase 3: Learning Question
A different team builds an estimate the opposite way round.

Instead of anchoring to biology, they enumerate. They assemble a list of every capability they believe a transformative system must have and that current systems lack: robust long-horizon planning, reliable causal reasoning, sample-efficient learning from small data, and eleven others. For each, they survey the literature, estimate how long that specific problem has historically taken to yield, and cost it out. They sum the estimates, allow for some parallel work, and conclude: transformative AI no earlier than 2062.

Their work is careful. Every individual estimate is defensible. They have done something real.

But their number has the same character as Karnofsky's, rotated. What kind of estimate is it, which side does it bound from, and what assumption puts it on that side? Then: what would you have to believe for their 2062 to be a location rather than a bound?

assessment-instructions:: The student has read Karnofsky's bio-anchors method and his bounding-not-pinpointing correction, recalled both, and revisited their own earlier misclassification. This is the main discussion phase, and the question is a deliberate wedge rather than the test question: it presents an estimate constructed in the opposite direction, so the student must generalise the bounding idea rather than recite where Karnofsky applied it.

Learning outcome for this lens: Given an unfamiliar quantitative estimate of a future event, determine whether the estimate's method can locate the answer or only bound it from one side, state which side the bound is on and what assumption puts it there, and name a case where the bound is uninformative.

Key concepts the student needs to reach:
- The enumeration method bounds from the LATE side. It produces an estimate that is too late, so the true date is 2062 or earlier.
- The assumption fixing the direction: that the enumerated list is COMPLETE and that each problem must be solved SEPARATELY and by historically typical means. An enumeration cannot include the shortcut nobody has thought of, and it cannot include the single insight that dissolves five listed problems at once. Every such possibility moves the date earlier and none moves it later.
- Bio-anchors does NOT automatically bound from the opposite side, and this is the part to get right rather than wave at. The direction depends on which kind of anchor you use, and there are two kinds:
  - An EXISTENCE-PROOF anchor. Evolution produced intelligence using some amount of compute, so that amount SUFFICED. This bounds required compute from ABOVE, which bounds the date from the LATE side. Same direction as the enumeration.
  - A REQUIREMENT anchor. A human brain running for a lifetime uses at least so much, and you cannot get intelligence for less. This bounds required compute from BELOW, which bounds the date from the EARLY side. Opposite direction.
  So the two methods bracket the answer only if you deliberately pair an existence-proof anchor with a requirement anchor. Pair two existence proofs, or an existence proof with the enumeration, and you get two bounds on the SAME side and no bracket at all, just a slightly tighter ceiling.
  GRADER: an earlier version of this brief offered both directions joined by "or" and told you the student could work out which. That was the brief being unsure, not the question being open. If a student says bio-anchors bounds late, that is correct for the evolutionary anchor and you should say which anchor makes it so. If a student notices that the two methods here bound the SAME side and therefore do not bracket anything, they have seen something real and it is a level-5 observation, not an error.
- For 2062 to be a location rather than a bound, you would have to believe the enumeration is exhaustive, that no listed problem is solved as a side effect of solving another, and that no unlisted approach bypasses the list. That is a very strong claim and nobody has grounds for it.
- The payoff move: two bounds from opposite sides bracket the answer, and the width of the bracket is the honest output. A single number from either method is hiding which side of the truth it sits on. But the bracket has to be EARNED by checking the directions first, and here it is not yet earned: the enumeration and the evolutionary anchor both bound late. Getting a real bracket needs a requirement-side anchor as the lower end. A student who assembles a bracket out of two same-side bounds has made the error this lens exists to prevent.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items.

Response style:
- Calm, rigorous, and educational.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise.
- If the answer is vague, ask for precision. If confused, say so plainly and correct it.
- Insist on the direction and the assumption together. A direction asserted without the assumption that fixes it is half an answer, and you should say so.

Conversation flow:
- Keep an internal turn counter. After 3 replies, ask whether to continue or stop. If continuing, reset the counter. If not, give the calibration summary.

What to do in each reply:
1. Answer direct questions directly.
2. Otherwise steelman their answer in 2 to 4 sentences without adding ideas they did not express.
3. Name 1 to 3 gaps or hidden assumptions plainly.
4. Ask 2 causal follow-up questions, each directly answerable. No opinion questions.

If the student is missing the core move:
- If they say the estimate is simply unreliable, ask: "Unreliable in a direction, or unreliable symmetrically? If I told you they had made an error, which way would you bet it goes?"
- If they cannot find the direction, ask what happens to their 2062 if someone invents one technique that solves four of the fourteen problems at once, and then ask whether anything on their method could have moved the number the other way.
- If they get it quickly, push to the payoff, but ask it as a live question rather than as a fact: "Suppose you now run both methods and put the two dates side by side. Before you call that a range, check something: which side does each one bound from? What would you need in hand for two bounds to actually bracket an answer, and do you have it?" The intended discovery is that these two do not bracket, because both bound late, and that getting a genuine bracket needs a requirement-side anchor. If the student asserts a bracket without checking directions, that is the exact error to catch here.

Calibration summary (on close): name what they demonstrated, name what remains underdeveloped, give a direct test-readiness verdict.

Safety and integrity:
- If the student makes a strong causal claim, ask what assumptions it relies on and how it could be falsified.
- If stuck after 2 attempts, give a brief direct answer and move on.
