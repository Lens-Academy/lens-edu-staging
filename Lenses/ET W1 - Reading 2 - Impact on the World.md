\## 3. Dimension #1. Impact on the World 🌎

One common way of understanding the impact of a thesis is about the research that you will complete - its relevance and potential to contribute to solving an important problem, and how it can be translated into real-world impact. For this dimension of impact, it’s crucial to be aware of the potential failure modes of research.

\#### Failure Mode #1. Poor Problem Selection

**Description:** You target a problem that is not important, neglected, or tractable, such that solving the problem doesn’t actually lead to any improvement in the real world.

**Questions to Consider:**

**Is this the right problem to focus on?**  Problem selection is the highest-leverage decision you make. If the problem isn’t [important, neglected, or tractable](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/itn-framework/), your research is unlikely to change anything in the world.

- **Importance**: How much could solving this problem improve the world? Consider scale and severity - how many lives are affected?
- **Neglectedness**: Is this cause getting enough attention, or is it being overlooked?
- **Tractability**: How likely is it that research in this area could make progress? Are there clear pathways where your contribution could actually move the needle?

\#### Failure Mode #2. Poor Research Execution/Understanding

**Description:** You target a high-impact problem, but do a poor job on research execution. This pitfall is most likely when you lack a deep understanding of the problem’s root causes and interactions.

**Questions to Consider:**

- Do you understand the root causes, key mechanisms, and interactions driving this problem?
- Do you have, or are you interested in, developing the skills and methods required to do this research well?
- Do you enjoy this type of work? Great research quality often comes from genuine interest and a sense of meaning.

\#### Failure Mode #3. No Pathway to Impact

**Description:** You target the right problem and produce good research, but your findings are never implemented in the real world. No decisions or actions are changed in the real world. We think this is one of the most common pitfalls in academia.

**Questions to Consider:**

- **Is there an actual pathway to impact?** Even if your thesis addresses a really pressing problem, it may not result in any real-world impact if nobody utilises/implements its findings.
- Are there existing organisations, practitioners, or decision-makers who could implement or build on your work?
- To what extent will working on this project contribute to a real-world solution?
- How will the findings of your thesis be translated into real-world impact? i.e., influencing a key intervention by a charity, informing a policy/funding decision, or shifting research priorities in a university department

**💡 Importantly, we want to highlight that it is a really ambitious goal for a thesis to solve an important problem, especially given your time and resource constraints.** As such, we think that the goal of this program/your thesis is not to put pressure on *definitely* having an impact on solving a massive problem like AI Safety but rather, to practice the skills of understanding HOW you would have an impact on such a problem area, for your thesis and your journey beyond.

Throughout this program, you'll come back again and again to the idea of "impact” - but what does this actually mean and how can we actually go about understanding how to prioritise impact?

Here are a few core concepts (from Probably Good) that can help improve the way you think about doing good. You can think about these as different lenses and tools to consider when evaluating problems, questions or roles!

1. **[Counterfactual impact](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/counterfactual-impact/) asks you to consider what would happen anyway, without you?** If a problem would get solved regardless of your involvement, your counterfactual contribution is small, even if the problem itself is huge.
2. **[Marginal impact](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/marginal-impact/) describes the impact of one additional unit of effort.** Think of a toaster company deciding whether to make one more toaster: the business overall might be hugely profitable, but if the market is already saturated, that one extra toaster could be a loss, even though total profits stay large. The same logic applies to your time and effort. Joining a large, already-impactful movement or organisation can feel exciting, but your own marginal contribution to it might be smaller than a more modest-looking option where you're one of very few people working on something. This is also a good guard against the sunk cost fallacy, continuing to invest somewhere just because it's worked well in the past, rather than because it's still the best use of your effort now.
3. **[Comparative advantage](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/comparative-advantage/)** as a concept helps you weigh not just how important a problem is, but **where your specific mix of skills, network, and interests** lets you contribute more than most other people could - so that you can discover your comparative advantage for working on an important problem!
4. **[Leverage](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/leverage/)** is a way of spotting when your contribution can shift outcomes far more than the effort you put in would suggest, for example, by influencing a decision-maker, or building something many others will end up using.
5. **[Expected value](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/expected-value/)** means weighing up a range of possible outcomes by how likely and how big each one is, instead of only planning around the single most probable one.
6. **[Diminishing returns](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/diminishing-returns/)** explains that the more resources already poured into something, **the less difference an additional unit t**ends to make, a reason to look twice at areas that already feel "solved."
7. **[Effective Altruism](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/effective-altruism/)** is the broader movement underneath all of this: using evidence and careful reasoning to figure out how to help others as much as possible with the time and money you have, rather than going with whatever feels most emotionally compelling in the moment.
   1. ⭐If you want to go deeper, the free 8-week [Introductory EA Program](https://www.effectivealtruism.org/courses/introductory-program) walks through these ideas with a small discussion group and a facilitator, well worth it if you haven't done it yet!
8. **[Opportunity cost](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/opportunity-cost/) is what you give up by choosing one path over another,** a useful check whenever an option looks good only because you haven't actually named what else you'd be giving up to take it.

Check out [this page](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/) on Probably Good for the full list of core concepts!

\### Widening our circle of compassion & consideration for impact

We tend to focus on issues that immediately affect us, or that we frequently hear about, a pattern called the **availability heuristic**. Some of the world's most pressing challenges stay hidden precisely because they affect people far away, other species, or future generations, or because they're slow-moving and complex. A few categories worth considering:

1. **Human beings far away from you.** Take a problem you care about locally, then imagine the same problem occurring far away, affecting far more people. Does distance make it less important? Many of the world's biggest challenges are overlooked precisely because they're out of sight.
   - Video: [Thought Leader Kwame Anthony Appiah on Cosmopolitanism](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=inyq_tfm8jc) (Carnegie Council for Ethics in International Affairs)
   - Article: [Famine, Affluence, and Morality](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/get-involved/videos-books-and-essays/famine-affluence-and-morality-peter-singer#full-text-of-famine-affluence-and-morality) (Peter Singer)
2. **Non-human beings.** Billions of animals are raised in conditions we'd consider torturous if applied to humans, and questions are now emerging about whether advanced AI systems could have morally relevant experiences too. What might we be missing by focusing only on human welfare?
   - Video (10 mins): [Non-Human Animals: Crash Course Philosophy #42](https://youtu.be/y3-BX-jN_Ac)
   - Article: [The Moral Status of Animals](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-animal/) (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)
3. **Future generations.** Humanity has existed for roughly 100,000 years; if we survive the next few centuries, we could exist for millions more. The overwhelming majority of people who could ever exist haven't been born yet. What might we owe them, and what problems could we help solve for them today?
   - Video (13 mins): [The Last Human – A Glimpse Into The Far Future](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LEENEFaVUzU) (Kurzgesagt)
   - Article: [The case for strong longtermism](https://academic.oup.com/book/60794/chapter/530063399) (Hilary Greaves and William MacAskill)
   - Article: [What is longtermism and why do its critics think it is dangerous?](https://archive.ph/lVE3G#selection-605.0-605.65) (Richard Fisher, New Scientist)
   - Article: [The epistemic challenge to longtermism](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04153-y) (Christian Tarsney)
