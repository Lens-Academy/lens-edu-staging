#### Question: Open
id:: 015b0607-82a0-4ec2-a8ba-0f022b523276
content::
A statistics lecturer wants to show how a small sample can mislead. Her example: "A poll of 20 people found that most supporters of [the main governing party in the students' country] can't read a simple graph."

1. Using the essay, why is this a bad example to choose?

2. What kind of example should she use instead?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

The essay's idea: political topics make people take sides, and then they support every argument for their side and attack every argument for the other. A political example used to make a point about reasoning rouses those emotions, distracts from the point, and makes people on the targeted side feel unwelcome. Unless the point is about politics, choose an example from outside current politics, and if it must be political, a historical one.

60: Part 1. The example brings in political sides: students react to the dig at the party (defending or cheering it), which distracts them from the statistical point, and students who support that party may feel attacked or unwelcome. Either the distraction or the alienation, clearly explained, earns full marks. Give 30 if the answer only says the example is biased or offensive, without saying what it does to the students' thinking or to the class.

40: Part 2. An example with no current political charge (for example about a product, sport, weather or health), or a historical one, that still shows a small sample misleading. Give 20 if the answer only says "a neutral example" without saying what makes it neutral or giving one.

Cap at 40 if the answer says the example would be fine if the claim about the party were true.
