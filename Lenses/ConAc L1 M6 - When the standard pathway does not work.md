In September 2026, Alex Chalk published [An AI Safety Career Switch Postmortem](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/45L9wqZuquANFci22/). Having worked as a software developer, he decided to specialise in AI governance. He completed a master's degree in international affairs, studied machine learning, built relationships with government officials and secured a government internship.

After graduating, he spent a year applying for relevant positions. He did not get a single interview.

One of the things Chalk identifies in retrospect is that he had correctly identified a problem that needed solving: the difficulty of communication between ML researchers and policymakers. However, he had overestimated the extent to which institutions recognised this problem and were prepared to hire someone to address it.

His experience illustrates an uncomfortable feature of the AI safety job market: there can be a genuine need for certain work, considerable funding in the field, and very few realistic employment opportunities for someone willing to do that work.

The situation is especially difficult for junior researchers. In [Why It's Hard to Get Hired](https://safetyapprentice.com/hard-to-get-hired.html), David Williams-King describes an imbalance between the growing number of junior researchers coming out of fellowships and the relatively small number of permanent research positions. Organisations often need researchers who can independently develop and execute a research agenda. Hiring junior researchers creates additional demand for senior researchers' already scarce time.

So what can you do if you have been following the recommended pathway and it is getting you nowhere?

- Find out what is going wrong
- Start doing the work without getting hired
- Reconsider what you are trying to achieve

\### Find out what is going wrong

A rejection contains surprisingly little information about your abilities. You might have been the second-best candidate for a position, or your application might have failed to meet a basic requirement. In both cases, you may receive exactly the same email.

In [Germany's Got Talent. How do we get it to work on AI safety?](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/EenKC8JRRBTFvXhch/germany-s-got-talent-how-do-we-get-it-to-work-on-ai-safety), Melanie interviewed 14 people who had successfully entered AI safety. One of the most common difficulties they reported was receiving rejections from jobs, fellowships and grants without knowing what they could have done differently.

Melanie describes how a ten-minute conversation with her mentor sometimes saved her months of pursuing unsuitable opportunities.

If you find yourself repeatedly getting rejected, we would recommend investigating several possibilities:

- **You lack a particular professional skill.** Another general AI safety course is unlikely to help if what employers need from you is proficiency in PyTorch or experience managing a budget.
- **Your abilities are difficult to assess.** You may have the necessary skills, but your CV and portfolio give employers little reason to believe it.
- **You are targeting the wrong positions.** You may be a competitive candidate for research engineering positions and an uncompetitive candidate for research scientist positions.
- **There are simply too few vacancies.** Further improving your qualifications may have little effect if only three organisations employ people with your specialisation.

Ask someone familiar with hiring for your intended role to look through your CV, application answers and previous work. Give them an actual vacancy you applied for. This makes it much easier for them to identify what might be missing.

\### Start doing the work without getting hired

One of the unusual features of AI safety is how much professional activity takes place outside formal employment.

Matt Beard gives the example of Luca, who independently built the [AI Safety Talent Network](https://safetytalent.org/). When 80,000 Hours began developing similar recruitment tools, someone recommended that they speak to him. He eventually joined the organisation.

You can approach an organisation with a proposal to solve a specific problem it has, find a researcher who would benefit from your assistance, or apply for funding to pursue your own project.

\### Reconsider what you are trying to achieve

Imagine that you want to reduce AI-related risks by improving the quality of government decisions. You have been applying exclusively to AI governance research organisations for six months without success.

There are other places where you could acquire influence over those decisions: government departments, regulatory bodies, conventional think tanks, standards organisations and political institutions.

The distinction between having an AI safety career and contributing to AI safety becomes quite consequential here. If you become too attached to the former, you may overlook promising ways of accomplishing the latter.

It is also perfectly reasonable to keep your current job and contribute to AI safety part-time. You can continue developing relevant expertise, collaborating on projects and exploring opportunities without putting your financial stability or the rest of your life on hold.

A career transition is an uncertain undertaking. We recommend setting a point at which you will reassess your strategy, especially if you are supporting yourself from savings. Decide in advance what evidence would justify continuing along your current path and what would persuade you to try something different.
