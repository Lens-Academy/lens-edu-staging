\## Steelmanning the skeptic
Here is the skeptical position, stated as fairly as it can be.

**The self-report problem.** Most of the "AI makes people think less" evidence is people describing their own thinking. Self-reports inflate and miscalibrate. The most-cited survey (Gerlich, 2025) used self-reports; its author noted the real effect could be worse than measured. A 2025 correction to that paper replaced a duplicated table and did not change its conclusions, so the correction is a bibliographic note, not a reason to distrust it.

**History suggests reorganisation, not decline.** Humans have offloaded thinking for as long as there were tools: writing, arithmetic tables, calculators. Whole civilisations offloaded memory to paper and arithmetic to machines, and human thinking did not shrink. It reorganised and moved to harder problems. The extended-mind view says the tool becomes part of the thinking, and the question is whether we practise "cognitive hygiene", not whether we offload at all.

**The speedup illusion.** A large preregistered study (Yu et al., 2026) found that AI assistance did not actually make simple tasks faster, but people *perceived* them as faster and less effortful. Some of what looks like "atrophy" may be miscalibrated self-perception: people feel they no longer need the skill even when their performance on the task is the same.

**Mixed results exist.** Some studies find cognitive *benefits* from strategic AI use. The field's findings are genuinely mixed, and much of the "for" evidence is short, narrow, and small.

\## What a fair skeptic concedes
The skeptic is right that the evidence is thinner than the headlines, that self-report is weak, and that offloading by itself is ancient and often benign. That is a real correction to the alarmist story.

But the skeptic's case has a hole. The new thing about generative AI is not that it takes over a narrow step. It performs the whole epistemic labour: it explains, synthesises, evaluates, and recommends. The risky mode is not "using a tool". It is treating an AI as an authority and skipping the review. That is exactly the pattern the strongest real-world evidence (the endoscopist study, the law-student study) and the survey work (Microsoft, 2025) point at: skills erode when the judgement is handed over, not when the task is shared. The novice-programmer study adds direct observation to that side of the ledger.

So the fair conclusion is the same as the evidence lens reached: concern about specific patterns of use, not panic about AI in general.
