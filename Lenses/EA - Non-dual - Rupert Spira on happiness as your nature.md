#### Text
content::
Rupert Spira is a living teacher of non-duality who has written several books on it ([his site](https://rupertspira.com/introduction/)). The title of one of them states the school's happiness claim: [*You Are the Happiness You Seek*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/59605034-you-are-the-happiness-you-seek). At the end of this talk he says the inward path he teaches is best explained in the Vedantic traditions.

In this talk at the Science and Nonduality conference he first guides the audience to notice the awareness in which every experience appears. Then he argues from what they noticed: awareness never changes and nothing adds to it or takes from it, so its nature is peace and happiness. Do what he asks while you listen, rather than only following the words. Pause the video where you need more time.

#### Question: Open
id:: 2fe5836f-b7d7-438c-9b99-92d013e89746
content::
Spira's argument runs roughly in four steps. (1) Every experience is known, and that knowing is what you call "I". (2) Thoughts, feelings and perceptions keep changing, but being aware stays the same. (3) Nothing that happens adds to it or takes anything from it. (4) So its nature is peace and happiness.

When you did what he asked, what did you find for steps 1 to 3? Then say which step you find weakest, and why.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner watched the first 17 minutes of Rupert Spira's talk "Your Nature is Happiness" (Science and Nonduality conference). Spira guides the audience to notice that all experience is known, to become interested in the knowing itself (which he says is what "I" refers to), says being aware is the only element of experience that is ever present and "in the same condition" since childhood, that no experience adds to or removes anything from it, and concludes: "thus its nature is peace", it is "innately fulfilled", "thus its nature is happiness", "a peace that is prior to all experience". The learner reports what they found when they tried steps 1 to 3 and names the weakest step.

The course explores and never says whether a school is right. Your job is to help the learner state their own finding and objection precisely, not to defend or refute Spira.

Useful points, to use only where they fit what the learner wrote:
- Step 4 is the biggest jump. From "unchanging and unaffected" it moves to "happy". Something unaffected by experience could also be called neutral. Spira bridges the gap by describing happiness as being "innately fulfilled", that is, lacking nothing. The learner can ask whether that is the same happiness people seek, or a redefinition.
- Steps 2 and 3 rest on first-person observation. Someone could ask whether awareness seems unchanging because it is, or because we cannot observe it while it is absent (for example in dreamless sleep), and whether memory supplies the sense of continuity.
- Step 1 identifies "I" with the knowing. Other schools identify the self with the body, a narrative or a model built by the brain. Module 6 compares these.
- If the learner found that steps 1 to 3 matched their experience and step 4 felt true, take that seriously as data, and ask what would show the peace is there when they are, for example, anxious.

Per reply: restate the learner's finding or objection more precisely in one or two sentences, add one point they did not make, and ask one direct question. 80 to 140 words. At most two replies, then close by saying the evidence lens and the experiments later in this module let them test this. If the learner says they did not understand the exercise, give one concrete foothold: "Right now, is the sound you hear known? Is the knowing of it itself a sound?" No generic praise.
