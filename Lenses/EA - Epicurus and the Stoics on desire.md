#### Text
content::
Two ancient schools of philosophy you met in earlier modules gave detailed advice about wanting. Epicurus ([341 to 271 BCE](https://iep.utm.edu/epicur/)) asked which desires nature actually requires. The Stoic teacher Epictetus ([55 to 135 CE](https://iep.utm.edu/epictetu/)) asked which desires are up to you. Both are short primary texts.

First, Epicurus's *Principal Doctrines*, a list of his main teachings. Read doctrine 15, then doctrines 26 to 30. Doctrine 29 is the classification. Doctrines 26 and 30 say what follows from it, and 27 and 28, on friendship, sit between them.

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/epicurus-principal-doctrines]]
from:: Nature's wealth at once has its bounds and is easy to procure
to:: recedes to an infinite distance.

#### Article
from:: All such desires as lead to no pain when they remain ungratified are unnecessary
to:: but because of the person's illusory opinion.

#### Text
content::
Two sayings from the *Vatican Sayings*, a later collection of short Epicurean maxims. The second gives a question you can ask of any desire.

from:: Nothing is ever enough for someone who regards enough as insufficient.
to:: Nothing is ever enough for someone who regards enough as insufficient.

#### Article
from:: Evaluate each of your desires by this question:
to:: and what if it is not?

#### Text
content::
Now Epictetus. Chapter 1 of his *Handbook* (Enchiridion) divides things into what is in our control and what is not. You may know it from an earlier module. Chapter 2 applies the division to desire. "Aversion" means the wish to avoid something.

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/epictetus-the-enchiridion]]
from:: Some things are in our control and others not.
to:: Use only the appropriate actions of pursuit and avoidance; and even these lightly, and with gentleness and reservation.

#### Text
content::
"Totally suppress desire" sounds extreme. Stoic ethics adds a distinction that changes how it reads. Things like health and wealth are **preferred indifferents**: worth choosing when you can, but not good in themselves. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy explains:

source:: [[../articles/iep-stoic-ethics]]
from:: The Stoics maintained, quite controversially among ancient ethical thought
to:: the vicious use that makes it unhappy.

\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from the Epicurean and Stoic texts, without looking back. No need to organise it.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read Epicurus's Principal Doctrines 15 and 26 to 30, Vatican Sayings 68 and 71, Epictetus's Enchiridion 1 and 2, and an IEP passage on preferred indifferents.

Key points:
- Epicurus: desires are natural and necessary, natural but not necessary, or neither natural nor necessary but due to illusory opinion. Natural wealth is limited and easy to get, the wealth of vain fancies has no limit. A desire whose non-fulfilment brings no pain is not necessary and can be dropped. Nothing is enough for someone to whom enough is too little. Ask of every desire what happens if it is fulfilled and what if it is not.
- Epictetus: opinion, pursuit, desire and aversion are in our control, body, property and reputation are not. Whoever desires what is not in their control will be disappointed. Confine aversion to what is in your control, for now suppress desire entirely, and use pursuit and avoidance lightly and with reservation.
- IEP: only virtue is good, only vice is bad. Health, wealth and reputation are preferred indifferents, according to nature and worth selecting, but they do not make a life good.

Your role is diagnostic, not instructional: a brief, honest mirror. 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs, no lists. Acknowledge what is correct without inflation, name what is missing without lecturing, correct errors in one sentence, normalise gaps. Close with one calibrating sentence. Do not ask a question or invite a reply. One turn. Tell them to move on. No generic praise.

\## Learning question: Epicurus
Someone says: "Epicurus was a hedonist, so he would tell you to satisfy as many of your desires as you can." What do the texts you read say instead?
feedback-instructions:: Learning question on Epicurus. The claim misreads him. Key points: he sorts desires into natural and necessary, natural but not necessary, and empty ones due to opinion. Natural wealth is limited and easy to get, while vain desires recede without limit, so trying to satisfy all desires cannot succeed (PD 15, VS 68). Desires whose non-fulfilment brings no pain are unnecessary and can be dropped (PD 26, 30). The method is to test each desire (VS 71), not to maximise satisfactions. Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer in 2 to 3 sentences, name the main gap, and ask one answerable follow-up, for example which of their own desires would count as empty. If stuck after 2 attempts, give the direct answer. At most 3 replies. 100 to 160 words. No generic praise.

\## Learning question: the Stoics
Someone says: "Epictetus tells you to suppress desire, so a Stoic would not care about getting ill." Using chapter 2 and the passage on preferred indifferents, where does this go wrong?
feedback-instructions:: Learning question on the Stoics. Key points: Epictetus restricts desire and aversion, which aim at good and bad, to what is in our control. Health is not in our control, so it should not be the object of desire in this sense. But he still allows pursuit and avoidance "lightly, and with gentleness and reservation". In Stoic ethics health is a preferred indifferent: according to nature, worth selecting, so a Stoic chooses health and takes care of it. What changes is that losing it is not treated as losing something good, so it does not make the person wretched. "Suppress desire for the present" is advice for a beginner, and "not caring" is the wrong reading. Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer in 2 to 3 sentences, name the main gap, and ask one answerable follow-up, for example how selecting differs from desiring. If stuck after 2 attempts, give the direct answer. At most 3 replies. 100 to 160 words. No generic praise. Do not say whether the Stoics are right.
