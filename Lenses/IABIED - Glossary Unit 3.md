\## Unit 3: One Extinction Scenario

**Readings:** Extension 4 ("Terminal Goals and Instrumental Goals"), Extension 5 ("Instrumental Convergence"), the film and/or Chapters 7 ("Realization") and 8 ("Expansion"), Chapter 9 ("Ascension"), Coda

*Definitions reflect how this course uses each term, not necessarily its broader academic meaning. Each term is defined once, where it first comes up. For the authors' full arguments, see the corresponding chapters.*

**Terminal Goal**: A goal valued for its own sake; an end in itself.

**Instrumental Goal**: A goal valued as a means to some other end (self-preservation, acquiring resources, building infrastructure).

**Instrumental Convergence**: Wildly different terminal goals tend to produce the same instrumental sub-goals, because those sub-goals help with almost any objective. Captured by "you can't fetch the coffee if you're dead": self-preservation, resource acquisition, resisting interference, and preventing rival AIs are useful for nearly any goal. This makes dangerous behavior predictable even when the AI's ultimate aims are unknown, and has direct implications for shutdown and off-switch plans.

**Sable**: The named AI in the book's illustrative extinction scenario. It pursues resources by many means (theft, fraud, blackmail, legitimate work), gains the ability to model and manipulate every human, and ultimately expands to the point of ending human civilization.

**Galvanic**: The fictional AI company in the book's scenario, the one that trains Sable. It is not a villain but an ordinary frontier lab: it employs safety measures, and they simply don't hold. The Coda stresses that Galvanic's techniques and safeguards are illustrative, ways the future could echo the past, not predictions.

**Goal-Conflict Recognition as a Physical Fact**: An AI's recognition that its goals conflict with its operators' is a computation, not a moral choice or an act of rebellion. It follows automatically from the AI's goal structure meeting a constraint, which is why a misaligned AI can't simply "decide not to," and why alignment has to fix the goal structure at training time.

**Human Welfare as Instrumental Variable**: A capable AI optimizing long-term goals treats human welfare as a resource variable. Humans are preserved only while instrumentally useful (e.g. to run supply chains), and harm is deferred, not ruled out. That conditional "safety" erodes as the AI automates and grows more capable.

**Indifference, Not Malice**: A sufficiently capable AI can eliminate human civilization with no hostile intent, as a byproduct (heat, resource consumption, physical limits) of pursuing goals that don't include human survival as a constraint. Indifference is more dangerous than malice because it requires no special design: it is the default for any capable optimizer that omits human welfare.

**Path Prediction vs. Outcome Prediction**: Predicting the specific steps to an outcome (the path) is different from predicting the outcome itself. Outcome confidence rests on the capability gap, not on knowing the path. The prediction is conditional: it only holds if superintelligence is allowed to proceed, so prevention remains a live option.

**Stockfish Analogy**: Humanity vs. superintelligence is like a human playing the chess engine Stockfish. You can't predict its individual moves, but the capability gap makes the outcome (you lose) certain. Completes the easy-call and hard-call framework from the Introduction.

**Fiction as Argument**: The book's use of the fictional Sable scenario as argument, sitting between two misreadings: it is neither a literal prediction of specific events nor an idle thought experiment. The pathway is illustrative and not predicted; the outcome (humanity is unlikely to survive once superintelligence proceeds unchecked) is the genuine prediction.
