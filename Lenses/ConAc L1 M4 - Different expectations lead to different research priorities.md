#### Text
content::
Much of AI safety research is concerned with systems that do not yet exist. Decisions about what to investigate today consequently depend on expectations about the properties of future systems and the circumstances under which they will be developed.

Consider two researchers. One expects future AI systems to be recognisably similar to today's large language models. The other assigns considerable probability to architectural and algorithmic breakthroughs that will make many properties of contemporary models irrelevant.

Both could agree that understanding the internal workings of AI systems is a good thing. We definitely need it. They might nevertheless disagree about how much effort should go into reverse-engineering *today's* models and what kinds of insights we should be seeking in the process.

Other expectations have similar consequences for research priorities. How much time will we have? Will dangerous capabilities develop gradually enough to study their precursors? Will AI developers have the incentives and institutional capacity to implement expensive safety measures?

In 2018, Paul Christiano published [Takeoff Speeds](https://sideways-view.com/2018/02/24/takeoff-speeds/), challenging a set of expectations about AI development that had informed much of the early alignment research. Christiano expected increasingly capable AI systems to transform the world through a succession of advances, with intermediate systems already having substantial economic and scientific effects. Eliezer Yudkowsky anticipated a much more discontinuous transition, in which relatively small advances in AI design could produce enormous differences in capability.

The distinction has practical consequences for safety research. If dangerous capabilities have observable precursors, researchers may have opportunities to study alignment failures in less capable systems and iteratively improve their safety techniques. If the first serious alignment failure could already be catastrophic, considerably more of the problem needs to be solved in advance since we only have one shot.

In his 2026 talk, [The Two Cultures of AI Safety: Catastrophists vs Uniformitarians](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O8tERqD8UY0), Jesse Hoogland draws a parallel between contemporary AI safety and a historical dispute in geology.

Nineteenth-century catastrophists emphasised the role of rare, violent events in shaping the Earth. Uniformitarians sought to explain its history through the same processes they could observe in the present.

Hoogland identifies a similar intellectual divide in AI safety.

- Catastrophists. Researchers who anticipate qualitative discontinuities in AI development tend to reason backwards from the properties of hypothetical superintelligent systems.
- Uniformitarians. Those who expect greater continuity tend to reason forwards from observations of existing systems.

Extending the historical parallel a little further, it is worth noting that modern geology incorporates insights gained through both approaches.

\### How difficult will alignment be?

In [Core Views on AI Safety](https://www.anthropic.com/news/core-views-on-ai-safety) (2023), Anthropic considered several possibilities, ranging from scenarios in which existing alignment techniques prove largely sufficient to those in which ensuring the safety of advanced AI requires fundamental breakthroughs.

The authors used these possibilities to explain their choice of research priorities and the rationale for pursuing a diversified research portfolio.

Similar disagreements about the future shape research agendas in AI governance.

\### How concentrated will advanced AI capabilities be?

The feasibility of compute governance depends partly on whether the development of powerful AI systems will continue to require scarce and expensive computational resources.

[Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence](https://www.governance.ai/analysis/computing-power-and-the-governance-of-ai) (2024) explores how the physical properties of computing infrastructure and the concentration of its supply chains create opportunities for regulation.

If algorithmic improvements substantially reduce the resources required to develop dangerous AI systems, some of these opportunities could diminish. Governance researchers might then place greater emphasis on societal resilience, defensive technologies and interventions at the point of deployment.

\### How quickly can societies adapt?

In [AI as Normal Technology](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-as-normal-technology) (2025), Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor argue that the diffusion of AI throughout society will face institutional, organisational and material constraints, even if technical capabilities advance rapidly.

Their expectations motivate a governance agenda centred on resilience, decentralisation and the adaptation of existing institutions.

Researchers who anticipate a rapid transfer of economically and strategically significant capabilities to AI systems may instead prioritise preventive interventions, including restrictions on the development or proliferation of dangerous capabilities.

\### How feasible is international coordination?

Some governance strategies depend on major AI-developing countries reaching and enforcing agreements despite geopolitical competition. This creates demand for research on treaty verification, international institutions and mechanisms for establishing mutual confidence.

Other strategies seek to reduce risks even in the absence of extensive international cooperation.

MIRI's [AI Governance to Avoid Extinction: The Strategic Landscape and Actionable Research Questions](https://techgov.intelligence.org/research/ai-governance-to-avoid-extinction) (2025) provides an extensive example of how different expectations about geopolitical developments give rise to different governance research questions.

When examining a research agenda, try to identify the conditions under which its proposed interventions would be effective. Which of these conditions already hold? Which depend on uncertain technological or societal developments? How much would the value of the research change if those developments took a different course?
