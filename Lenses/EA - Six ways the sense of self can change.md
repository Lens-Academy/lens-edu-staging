#### Question: Open
id:: 591ac4f9-0fe7-4bbe-bdad-a283ef002c25
content::
\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from the reading, without looking back. No need to organise it.
feedback-instructions:: The learner read excerpts from Lindahl and Britton (2019) on changes in sense of self among Buddhist meditators, then wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- The study (Varieties of Contemplative Experience) deliberately recruited Western Buddhist meditators who reported meditation-related challenges. So it shows what can happen, not how often it happens to meditators in general.
- Six changes: narrative self (one's story of who one is), sense of ownership (thoughts, body, feelings not felt as mine), sense of agency (no one doing the actions), embodiment (self located elsewhere or outside the body), self-other or self-world boundaries (expansion such as "this is all me", or painful permeability), basic self (feeling of not being there at all).
- Changes could be brief or lasting, welcome or distressing, enhancing or impairing.
- 49 of 68 (72%) reported at least one change. Boundary changes were the most common (69% of the 49). Of the 49, 55% reported distress and 45% impairment. Basic-self changes most often came with distress and impairment, boundary changes least often. More kinds of change at once predicted more impairment. Tradition, gender, retreat versus daily practice and psychiatric or trauma history did not predict distress or impairment.
- The same change was read as Buddhist insight by some and as depersonalisation by others. Expectations, teachers and context shaped the reading, and readings changed over time.

Be a brief, honest mirror: say what they got right without inflation, name what is missing, correct any error in one sentence (especially any claim that the 72% applies to meditators in general), and treat gaps as normal. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. No follow-up questions and no generic praise. One reply, then tell them to move on.

#### Question: Open
id:: 52f88b21-bd42-41c5-8cff-06d7a6298fd3
content::
\## Learning question
A friend who meditates says: "Losing the sense of self is the goal. If it ever feels bad, that just means you are still clinging, so you should push through."

Using the reading, what is wrong or missing in this?
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Lindahl and Britton (2019) and got a plausible but flawed claim: "Losing the sense of self is the goal. If it ever feels bad, that just means you are still clinging, so you should push through." This is practice, not a test.

Points a strong answer finds (any two are good):
- "The sense of self" is not one thing. The study separates six changes (narrative self, ownership, agency, embodiment, boundaries, basic self). Which one is "the goal" is unclear, and they carry different risks: basic-self changes most often came with distress and impairment, boundary changes least often.
- Distress and impairment were common in this sample (55% and 45% of those with changes), and more kinds of change at once predicted more impairment. Some changes lasted into daily life for years, for example nine years of agoraphobia after a no-self retreat.
- Whether a change counts as insight or pathology was itself a matter of appraisal, shaped by expectations and teachers. "It means you are clinging" is one such appraisal, and some practitioners rejected it.
- The sample was selected for challenges, so it cannot say how often this happens, but it shows that it does happen, including to people with no psychiatric or trauma history.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in 2 to 3 sentences, name one gap, and ask one or two direct follow-ups (for example: "Which of the six changes do you think your friend means?" or "What does the study say predicted impairment, and what did not?"). If they are stuck after two attempts, give two points briefly. Do not tell the learner whether loss of self is a good goal. 100 to 180 words per reply. After 3 replies ask whether they want to continue. No generic praise.
