#### Text
content::
Each experiment below tests a claim from this module, using the instructions of a teacher or a text. Take a baseline before you start, fix how much you will do, and decide what you will measure before you begin, as in module 3. Where the dose or the measure is our own suggestion, the text says so.

Pick one. Experiments 1, 3 and 5 fit into ordinary days. Experiments 2 and 4 need a daily sitting.

#### Callout: 1. Is there a second arrow?
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**Claim.** In the Arrow discourse, an untrained person who feels pain also "sorrows, grieves, & laments" and so "feels two pains, physical & mental", like a man shot with a second arrow. The trained person feels only the first. The discourse adds that the untrained person, touched by pain, reaches for sensual pleasure because he sees no other escape ([SN 36.6](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn36/sn36.006.than.html)).

**What to do.** For one week, after each unpleasant event (a headache, a criticism, a delay), write two lines: what the first arrow was, and what you added to it: resistance, worry, inner complaint, a story about what it means.

**Measure.** Rate each arrow from 0 to 10. Note whether, soon after, you reached for something pleasant (food, your phone, a show) to get away from the feeling. At the end of the week, look at whether the second arrow was usually there, how big it was compared with the first, and whether it got smaller once you started noticing it. The logging and the one-week length are our suggestions.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 2. Two weeks of breath meditation
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**Claim.** Thanissaro Bhikkhu: sensitivity to the breath can "give the mind a handle for dealing with pain", and "true happiness has to be sought within" ([Basic Breath Meditation Instructions](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/breathmed.html)).

**What to do.** Follow his short instructions, below. They start with good will for yourself and for all beings, then a comfortable breath at one spot, then a survey of how the breath feels through the body, then awareness of the whole body at once.

**Dose.** "In the beginning, twenty minutes is usually about right", Thanissaro writes in his longer guide ([With Each and Every Breath](https://www.dhammatalks.org/Archive/Writings/Ebooks/WithEachAndEveryBreath_210603.pdf)). Sit 20 minutes a day for two weeks. The two weeks are our suggestion. His guide also says: if focusing in the head gives you a headache, avoid it until you can focus with less pressure.

**Measure.** Before the first sit and after the last one, rate your usual calm on a typical day from 0 to 10. After each sit, rate from 0 to 10 how calm you are and how pleasant the breath felt (our suggestion). If you have a mild ache, note whether paying attention to the breath changed how much it bothered you.

#### Callout: 3. Watching a craving instead of obeying it
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**Claim.** The Arrow discourse says that seeing a feeling's "origination, passing away, allure, drawback, and escape" frees you from being driven by it ([SN 36.6](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn36/sn36.006.than.html)). Brewer's smoking trial, from the previous lens, turned this into a technique.

**What to do.** Pick one craving that comes often: a snack, checking your phone, a cigarette. Each time it comes, use RAIN, the technique from Brewer's trial: "Recognize, Accept, Investigate and Note what cravings feel like as they arise" ([Brewer et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3191261/)). Notice where you feel the urge in your body and how it changes while you watch it. You are free to act on it afterwards.

**Dose.** Four weeks, the length of Brewer's training. His participants also did about 18 minutes of formal meditation on 4.6 days a week, and informal practice such as RAIN about five times a day (same source). If you want the formal part, use experiment 2.

**Measure.** Each time, rate the urge from 0 to 10 before and after RAIN, and note whether you acted on it. Count how often you gave in each week. If the claim holds, urges should fall while you watch them, and you should act on fewer of them by week 4.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 4. Loving-kindness toward a widening circle
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**Claim.** Practised in order, from yourself out to people you dislike, loving-kindness "breaks the barrier" of likes and dislikes, until you can regard an enemy "with the same amount of goodwill that one has for a very dear friend". So writes **Acharya Buddharakkhita** (1922 to 2013), the Indian monk who founded the Maha Bodhi Society in Bangalore ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acharya_Buddharakkhita)), drawing on the classical commentary, the *Visuddhimagga*.

**What to do.** His first method, below. A note on the reading: he also writes that the practice can cure diseases at a distance. That is a further claim, and this experiment does not test it.

#### Text
content::
**Dose.** 15 minutes a day for three weeks (our suggestion). In a review of trials, Galante and colleagues note that the practice "may initially be challenging for some people" ([Galante et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24979314/)). If turning to a disliked person brings up more anger than you can work with, stay with the earlier circles for a while, as Buddharakkhita himself suggests for people you have quarrelled with.

**Measure.** Before the first session and after the last, think of one person you dislike and rate your goodwill toward them from 0 to 10. Each evening, rate from 0 to 10 how warm you felt toward people that day (our suggestions).
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 5. Is this really me or mine?
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**Claim.** Thanissaro Bhikkhu: asking these questions helps "chip away at the attachment and clinging" that cause stress ([No-self or Not-self?](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/notself2.html)). They come from the optional lens on not-self, and they apply the second discourse you read, which says each part of body and mind is not "my self".

**What to do.** For one week, each time you are upset (hurt pride, worry about how you come across, anger at a loss), ask his three questions slowly: "Am I suffering stress because I'm holding onto this particular phenomenon? Is it really me, myself, or mine? If it's stressful but not really me or mine, why hold on?"

**Measure.** Rate how upset you are from 0 to 10 before the questions and again a few minutes later. Write one line on what you were holding onto: a view of yourself, a feeling, your body, a possession (our suggestions). Compare with a few upsets that you leave alone, to see how much the rating falls without the questions.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: If it goes badly
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
In Lindahl and colleagues' study of Buddhist meditators with difficult experiences, 72% of the difficulties came during or right after a retreat, but a quarter of the people were practising only 30 to 60 minutes a day when their difficulties began ([Lindahl et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5443484/)). Do not take any of these practices to retreat length on your own. In Britton's research, lasting bad effects went with signs of hyperarousal (for example agitation or poor sleep) and dissociation (for example feeling unreal or detached) ([Britton et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8845498/)). If you notice these, cut back or stop. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, stop and contact a doctor or a crisis line.
#### End Callout

#### Question: Open
id:: 86a20413-c40c-4e6f-9e80-225a836eedaf
content::
Choose one experiment, or adapt one, and write your protocol in a few lines: the claim, what you will do, how often and for how long, what you will measure and when, what result would count against the claim, and when you would stop.
feedback-instructions:: The learner read five experiment protocols from the early Buddhism module: (1) a week of logging the first and second arrow after unpleasant events, 0 to 10 each, and whether they reached for a pleasure to escape (SN 36.6); (2) Thanissaro's breath meditation, 20 minutes a day for two weeks, rating calm and the pleasantness of the breath; (3) RAIN for a frequent craving for four weeks, rating the urge before and after and logging whether they acted on it (Brewer et al. 2011); (4) Buddharakkhita's loving-kindness Method 1, 15 minutes a day for three weeks, goodwill toward a disliked person rated before and after; (5) a week of Thanissaro's not-self questions during upsets, rating distress before and a few minutes after, compared with upsets left alone. They now write their own protocol.

Check the protocol for six parts: the claim, the practice, the dose (how often and for how long), the measure and its timing, a result that would count against the claim, and a stopping rule. Name the parts that are present in one sentence, then name the most important missing or vague part and suggest a concrete fix taken from the lens (for example 'rate the urge from 0 to 10 before and after RAIN'). If they have no baseline or comparison, point out that a before-measurement or a few untreated occasions are needed to see a change. If they choose experiment 1 or 5, mention that noticing alone may change the numbers, which is part of what the tradition claims but also an expectation effect. Reply in 90 to 150 words. No generic praise. At most two replies. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: ask them which number they would write down first, and when.
