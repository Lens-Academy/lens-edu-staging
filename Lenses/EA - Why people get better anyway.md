#### Text
content::
A **testimonial** is a person's report that something helped them: "after the retreat, my anxiety was gone." Most practices reach us this way, through friends, teachers and books. A testimonial can be completely sincere and still tell you little about whether the practice caused the change. Two readings show why.

\## Getting better anyway

David Colquhoun is a pharmacologist and a strong critic of alternative medicine. In this blog post he argues that most of the improvement people credit to a treatment would have happened anyway.

#### Text
content::
**Regression to the mean** is the tendency of an unusually bad (or good) measurement to be followed by a more ordinary one. We think it applies to moods as much as to back pain: if you start a practice in a bad week, the following weeks will probably be better whatever you do. Notice the limit of Colquhoun's argument too. The review he quotes says placebos can influence outcomes that patients report about themselves, and almost every measure of wellbeing is self-reported. The next study shows how large that influence can be.

\## Expecting to feel better

The **placebo effect** is an improvement caused by the expectation of a treatment rather than by the treatment itself. To separate them, a trial is **blinded**: participants do not know whether they receive the real treatment or a dummy. A team at Imperial College London tested psychedelic microdosing (taking very small doses of LSD or psilocybin) in people who were microdosing anyway. Participants blinded themselves with a setup that mixed real doses and empty capsules, so they did not know which they were taking.

#### Text
content::
Meditation cannot be blinded in this way: you always know whether you meditated. The design page later in this module comes back to what you can do about that.

\## Who tells the story

**Survivorship bias** is the error of drawing conclusions only from the cases that made it through some selection ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivorship_bias)). We think it applies strongly to stories of practice: the people who write books and teach are mostly those for whom the practice worked. Those who practised for years without result, or were harmed, rarely become teachers. Martin's study of persistent non-symbolic experience in module 2 interviewed 50 people who volunteered and described themselves as having it.

Think of a practice, habit or book that you, or someone you know, believe really helped. Using this page, list the reasons the improvement might have happened even if the practice did nothing. Then say what you would need to know to tell the difference.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Colquhoun on regression to the mean (people seek help when at their worst and are likely to improve anyway), the abstract of Szigeti et al. 2021 (self-blinded microdosing: wellbeing improved as much on placebo as on microdoses), and our paragraph on survivorship bias. They now apply this to a practice they or someone they know believe helped.

Good answers name several alternative explanations that fit their own case: they started when things were bad (regression to the mean), they expected it to help (placebo and expectation, especially for self-reported feelings), other things changed at the same time (a holiday, a new relationship, the season, sleep), they remember the hits and forget the misses, and the people who recommended it are those for whom it worked (survivorship). What would tell the difference: a baseline measured before starting, a comparison (stopping and restarting, or comparing with a similar activity), measuring over a longer time, a measure less open to expectation.

Reply in 80 to 140 words. Name the strongest alternative explanation for their specific case, especially one they missed. Do not tell them the practice did not work. The point is that the story alone cannot tell. If they named good ways to tell the difference, say that the second half of the module turns these into an experiment. No generic praise. One turn.
