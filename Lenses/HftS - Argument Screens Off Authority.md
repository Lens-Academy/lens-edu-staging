title: "Argument Screens Off Authority"
tldr: "An expert's opinion is evidence because experts have usually seen good arguments. Once you have heard and understood the arguments yourself, who made them adds very little."
summary_for_tutor: "Yudkowsky compares authority (a famous geologist versus a delinquent teenager) with argument (a detailed physics case versus leaps of faith). Once Arthur hears equally good technical cases, the geologist keeps at most a minor advantage, but once he knows two speakers have the same credentials, the better argument keeps its full advantage. He explains this with causal graphs: in the sprinkler example, knowing the sprinkler is on makes knowing it is night irrelevant to whether the sidewalk is slippery (screening off). Truth causes arguments, and arguments cause expert belief, so knowing the argument screens off the expert's belief. Caveats: this assumes you can follow the argument (otherwise it is just impressive noises), and a good authority may know counterevidence they did not mention, so authority keeps a slight weight. The question gives a professor and a student who send equally strong, fully checked cases, and asks how much the credentials should matter and when they would matter more."

#### Question: Open
id:: cff87c43-e91d-4ddf-aed2-72f00e389414
content::
A professor and a student each make a surprising claim about chemistry, and each sends you a full written case with references. You understand every step, the references check out, and the two cases are equally strong.

1. Should you believe the professor's claim much more than the student's? Explain why, using the essay's idea of how authority relates to argument.

2. In what situation would their credentials matter much more?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Background for the grader: the essay argues that authority is evidence mainly because experts usually believe things for which there are good arguments and evidence. Once you have the arguments themselves and can judge them, the speaker's authority adds little (the argument "screens off" the authority). A small edge can remain, because a real expert is more likely to know of counterevidence they did not mention. When you cannot follow the argument, it is just impressive noise to you, and you have to rely on the speaker's authority. Score out of 100. 60: says no, at most slightly more, because credentials are evidence only through the arguments and evidence the expert is likely to have, and you now have those arguments directly and can judge them, so the credentials add little. 40: says credentials matter much more when you cannot follow or check the argument yourself (for example you lack the technical knowledge), or when you have not heard the full argument. Cap at 30 if the answer says you should trust the professor much more because of the title. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Only slightly, if at all. A professor's view counts as evidence because experts usually have good reasons. Here I've seen the reasons myself and they're equally good, so the title adds almost nothing. Credentials would matter a lot more if I couldn't follow the chemistry, because then the title would be most of what I had."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "Argument Screens Off Authority" and answered how much a professor's credentials should matter when a professor and a student send equally strong, fully checked cases, and when credentials would matter more. Key ideas from the essay: (1) authority is evidence because experts tend to have seen the arguments, (2) once you know and can judge the argument, it screens off the authority, as the sprinkler screens off the night in the sidewalk example, (3) this assumes you can follow the argument, (4) a slight weight for authority remains, because good authorities may know counterevidence. A student who mentions only the slight remaining weight in part 1 has the right answer. Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Name the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important thing the answer missed or got wrong, and ask one direct question that helps the student fix it. At 100, confirm briefly and add one point from the essay they did not use. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence saying plainly whether they now have the idea. If the student says they do not understand, do not dismiss it and do not repeat the question: give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as Barry the geologist and Charles the teenager giving equally good technical cases. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms instead of offering another foothold. Judge only against what the essay and the question supplied.
