title: "Faster Than Science"
tldr: When there are millions of possible answers, most of the work goes into finding the one worth testing, not into the test. Science does not say how to do that part, so good scientists must know things before Science confirms them.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky argues that academic science is a slow, inefficient processor of evidence, moved by prejudice, intuition and herd behaviour (Planck's "opponents die" quote, the gradual acceptance of many-worlds, decades-long debates). Science tests ideas but does not say where they come from. In a large answer space, such as all equations of 32 bits or less (about 4 billion), it takes far more evidence to promote one hypothesis to your attention than to confirm it afterwards. A robot-controlled Ouija board that kept producing confirmed hypotheses would not bother Science but would be impossible for Bayes. So at the frontier, scientists who pick the right hypothesis to test have already done most of the epistemic work, and they rationally know something before Science does. It is easier to train people to test ideas than to have good ideas to test. The question gives a manager's claim that a researcher who picked the right formula out of millions "just guessed" and asks where most of the work was done and why a guess cannot explain her success.

#### Question: Open
id:: 200b1bf0-f000-4b2b-aab2-b41354a5f153
content::
A lab manager says: "Our researcher had millions of possible formulas to choose from. She picked one, tested it, and it fit the data almost perfectly. Before the test she knew nothing. She just guessed, and the experiment did all the real work."

Answer in your own words.

1. According to the essay, where was most of the work of finding this formula done: in choosing which formula to test, or in the test?

2. Why can "she just guessed" not explain her success?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The essay's idea: when the space of possible answers is very large, it takes far more evidence and reasoning to pick out one hypothesis worth testing than it then takes to confirm it with an experiment. Picking hypotheses at random in such a space would almost never hit a good one. So a scientist who picks the right one to test has already, before the experiment, done most of the work, even if she cannot yet show it.

Score out of 100.

50: Part 1. Most of the work was in choosing which formula to test, not in the test. Give 20 of these 50 if the answer says the choice mattered but still says the test did most of the work, or does not say which did more.

50: Part 2. With millions of options, a blind guess would almost never land on a formula that fits, so her success shows she had real knowledge or reasoning behind the choice (even if not yet confirmed or not fully conscious). Give 20 of these 50 if the answer only says "she must have been smart or experienced" without saying why luck is ruled out.

Cap at 40 if the answer agrees that she knew nothing before the test and the experiment did all the real work.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Mostly in choosing. Out of millions of formulas, narrowing down to one worth testing takes far more evidence than the test needs to confirm it. 2. If she had guessed blindly, the odds of hitting a formula that fits out of millions would be tiny. Getting it right on the first try means she had already done real reasoning, even before anyone could check it."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Faster Than Science" and answered a lab manager who says a researcher "just guessed" the right formula out of millions and the experiment did all the work. Key ideas from the essay: in a large answer space, promoting a hypothesis to attention takes far more evidence than confirming it (the 4 billion equations example). A blind process, like the robot-controlled Ouija board, would essentially never find good hypotheses. So scientists at the frontier know things before Science confirms them. Science does not say where hypotheses come from.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent recall, well done). Below a full score, name the single most important gap, usually that the answer did not say why a blind guess among millions would fail. At full score, confirm briefly and add the essay's point that it is easier to train people to test ideas than to have good ideas to test. Grade only against what the essay supplied.

If the student says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold: ask how often a random pick out of a million would be right. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on where their answer stands.
