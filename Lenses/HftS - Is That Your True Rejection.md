title: "Is That Your True Rejection?"
tldr: "When someone rejects an idea and you ask why, the reason they give is often not the one that decided it. Fixing that reason may change nothing, so first ask whether it is the real one."
summary_for_tutor: "People reject Yudkowsky's ideas about superintelligence and give \"he doesn't have a PhD\" as the reason. The true rejection is whatever caused the rejection at its first moment, often a fast pattern match (\"science fiction\", \"strange weird idea\"). Asked why, people search for a justification and stop at a plausible-sounding fact. If that fact were fixed, the same rejection would happen and the search would stop somewhere else (\"come back when you're tenured\"). Eric Drexler got his PhD, and the critics did not change their minds. Businesses should think hard before spending huge efforts on a prospect's stated reason. In disagreements the true sources are often hard to communicate or expose (inferential distance, intuitions, professional zeitgeist, emotional commitments, fear of being shown wrong, self-deception). Both sides should ask themselves \"Is that my true rejection?\", and may humbly ask the other, without publicly psychoanalysing them. The question gives a friend who keeps finding new reasons not to come to a restaurant, and asks what is probably going on and what to ask before fixing the next reason."

#### Question: Open
id:: 76dc2376-bd59-4d1e-8a30-8d549d299111
content::
You invite a friend to a new restaurant. They say no because it is too far away. You offer to drive them. They say it is too expensive. You offer to pay. They say they are not sure they would like the food.

Using the essay, in your own words:

1. What is probably going on?

2. Before you try to fix the next reason, what should you ask?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

The essay's idea: the true rejection is whatever actually caused someone to reject something at the first moment, often a quick reaction they have not put into words. When asked why, they search for a justification and stop at a reasonable-sounding one. Fixing that reason does not change the rejection, the search just stops at another reason. So before putting effort into fixing a stated reason, ask whether it is the true rejection.

60: Part 1. The friend had already decided not to come, for some other reason, and each stated reason is a justification found afterwards, which is why removing one only brings up the next. Give 30 if the answer says the reasons are excuses without saying that the refusal came first, or that fixing the reasons will not change it.

40: Part 2. Ask whether that reason is the real one, for example "If I sorted that out, would you come?" or "Is something else putting you off?", or encourage the friend to ask themselves this.

Cap at 30 if the answer says the best move is to keep fixing each reason until none is left.
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's "Is That Your True Rejection?" and applied it to a friend who keeps finding new reasons not to go to a restaurant. Key ideas of the essay: the true rejection is what caused the rejection at its first moment, often a fast pattern match. Asked why, people search for a justification and stop at a plausible fact. Before spending effort on a stated reason, ask whether it is the true rejection. Ask yourself first, and ask the other person humbly, without psychoanalysing them.

The most likely gaps: not saying that the refusal came before the reasons, or not naming the question to ask.
