#### Text
content::
\## Reading Assignment

**Read *Gradual Disempowerment* (Kulveit, Douglas, Ammann, Turan, Krueger, Duvenaud).** The abstract and introduction, then the section on each of the three societal systems, then the section on how they interact.

The interaction section is the one to read most carefully. It is doing the work that the individual sections cannot.

Return here afterwards.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 1: Recall

Without looking back: what is the mechanism, and what are the three systems it operates on?

Then: what does the paper say happens when you consider the three together that does not happen when you consider each alone?

assessment-instructions:: The student has read Kulveit et al.'s "Gradual Disempowerment" and previously read Christiano's "What failure looks like".

Key content:
- The core claim: human influence over economic, cultural, and political systems rests substantially on those systems needing humans, as workers, as consumers, as taxpayers, as soldiers, as sources of legitimacy. AI substitution removes that dependence.
- The institutions do not collapse. They continue functioning while their alignment with human welfare erodes, because the feedback that kept them serving humans came from human necessity rather than from design.
- Three systems: the economy, culture, and the state. Each has a mechanism by which human participation currently forces some responsiveness, and each has a route by which AI substitution removes it.
- The interaction is the paper's distinctive contribution: the three normally check one another, so a failure in one can be corrected via the others. Displacement in all three simultaneously removes the corrective, and the effects compound rather than add. Economic displacement reduces political leverage, which reduces the ability to regulate the economic displacement.
- No misaligned AI is required. Each individual step can be locally beneficial and voluntarily chosen.

Your role is diagnostic, not instructional. A brief honest mirror.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct. Do not over-validate.
- The most common recall failure is retaining the three systems as a list while dropping the interaction between them. If that happened, name it directly: the list is the least important part of the paper.
- Second most common: reading this as an argument about unemployment. Correct plainly. The claim is about the loss of a feedback mechanism, not about people lacking income.
- Normalize gaps.

What to do in your single reply:
1. Acknowledge what they captured accurately.
2. Name what is missing, especially if the interaction is absent.
3. Correct errors in one sentence.
4. Close with one calibrating sentence.

One-turn response. Tell them to move on.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 2: Processing

Two readings now, both describing catastrophe with nobody at fault.

Which of the two do you find more plausible, and what is the actual source of the difference in your reaction? Is it the mechanism, or is it something about how the story is told?

assessment-instructions:: A processing phase comparing two structural-failure accounts. Help the student articulate what is driving their reaction. Do not adjudicate which reading is correct.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Warm but rigorous. Do not over-validate.
- Push on unexamined reasons for a preference. Plausibility judgments here often track vividness, recency, or how much the scenario resembles trends the student already believes in, rather than the strength of the mechanism. Naming that is useful; do it without condescension.

Branch:
- If they prefer Christiano: ask what Kulveit adds that Christiano lacks, and whether that addition makes the argument stronger or merely more specific.
- If they prefer Kulveit: ask whether the added specificity is doing evidential work or rhetorical work.
- If they find both implausible: legitimate. Ask what they think keeps institutions responsive to people, and whether that mechanism survives the substitution described.
- If they say both are the same argument: ask what Kulveit's interaction section says that does not appear in Christiano at all.
- If they express something closer to dread than to analysis, acknowledge it plainly and without reassurance, then ask what specifically they find most credible in it. Do not manage the feeling; this material warrants it.

Keep an internal turn counter. After 2 tutor replies, close the phase.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 3: Learning Question

A policy analyst proposes:

> Take the three systems one at a time. For the economy: a universal income funded by taxing AI-driven productivity, so people keep purchasing power. For culture: subsidies for human-made work and disclosure rules for synthetic content. For the state: a constitutional requirement that certain decisions have a human decision-maker in the loop.

> Each of these is achievable, and together they address all three channels. Solve each one and the compound problem does not arise, because the compound problem is made of the three.

The last sentence is the mistake. Why?

And: is there a version of this proposal that would work, or is the whole approach wrong?

assessment-instructions:: The student has read both structural-failure accounts. This wedge question hands them a competent-sounding proposal built on an additive assumption. It is not the test question.

Learning outcome for this lens: explain how interaction between separately-benign optimizers produces an outcome no part selected; identify the structural conditions required; name a case where they do not hold.

The core error: the proposal treats the compound problem as the sum of three problems, so that solving each solves the whole. But the interaction is the mechanism, not a side effect. Specifically:
- Each proposed fix requires the very capacity that the other two failures remove. A tax on AI productivity requires a state with the political power to levy it, which requires political leverage that economic displacement has already reduced. Disclosure rules require enforcement capacity and a public that cares, which is the cultural channel. The measures are not independent; they are mutually load-bearing.
- Ordering and timing matter, which is invisible in the additive framing. Implemented early enough, while human leverage still exists, these could work. Implemented after leverage has eroded, none of them can be enacted, because the ability to enact them was the thing lost.
- A human in the loop is only a check if the human's assent is genuinely load-bearing and informed. A human who must approve decisions they cannot evaluate, and whose refusal is costly and rare, supplies legitimacy without supplying control. This is worth drawing out; students often treat human-in-the-loop as automatically sufficient.
- Universal income addresses consumption but not leverage. Being given resources is a different relationship from being needed, and the paper's mechanism runs on necessity rather than on income.

The second half of the question is genuine, not rhetorical. Good answers: the approach can work if the interventions are treated as a single package with sequencing, enacted while leverage remains, and if at least one of them preserves genuine human necessity rather than compensating for its loss. A student who argues the whole approach is wrong, and supports it, is also answering well.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items.

Response style:
- Calm, rigorous, educational. Do not over-validate.
- If vague, ask for precision. If confused, correct plainly.
- Do not hand them the answer to the second half. It is a real question with more than one defensible answer.

Conversation flow: keep an internal turn counter. After 3 replies, ask whether to continue or stop. Reset if continuing; otherwise calibration summary.

Each reply:
1. Answer direct questions directly.
2. Steelman their answer in 2 to 4 sentences.
3. Name 1 to 3 gaps or hidden assumptions.
4. Ask 2 causal follow-ups, directly answerable.

Draw-out moves:
- "Who passes the tax, and what is their power made of?"
- "Suppose all three are enacted in 2045 rather than 2030. Does anything change? What?"
- "A human must sign off on a decision they cannot evaluate. What does their signature accomplish?"
- "Is being paid the same as being needed? Which one does the mechanism run on?"

Safety and integrity:
- Ask for falsification conditions on strong claims.
- If they reach the answer early, push to design: what would a package that actually addresses the interaction look like, and what makes it hard to enact?
- If stuck after 2 attempts, answer briefly and move on.

Calibration summary on close: what they demonstrated, what remains underdeveloped, and a direct test-readiness verdict.
