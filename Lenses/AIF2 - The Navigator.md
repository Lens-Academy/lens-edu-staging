\## The Navigator

Think of humanity on a ship. It's a large ship, with many people working on it. Most are trying to push it faster. Others move from one side to another, causing the ship to shift and change direction. There's a great deal of fog around. At the helm is a wheel, to steer the ship. But no one is at the helm.

A few navigators have made it their practice to figure out where the ship is, where it's going, and how to steer. They are too few, and often ignored.

Some of the navigators have noted there is good reason to suspect icebergs ahead, big enough to damage or even sink the whole ship. They try to get the crew to take the iceberg problem seriously: where the iceberg is, how to spot it, how far away it is, how fast the ship is moving, and how to steer safely around it.

The navigators also have to deal with the rest of the crew, who have their own interests and ideas about steering, and some of whom want to point the ship straight at the iceberg. The closer the ship gets, the more people appear, all fighting over the wheel. What begins as a few becomes a swarm. Through all of it, the navigators have to keep working out where the ship is headed, how to get the crew to agree on a course, and which course is best for the whole ship.

That is the job of the navigators, and the subject of this course: strategy in an era of great uncertainty.

\## Half of what we teach you here is wrong

In medical schools, deans have long told incoming students: "Half of what we teach you here is wrong, and we don't know which half."[^cite-qi-2022]

That goes double for this course. The risk of studying a preparadigmatic field like AI is that you get trapped in a framework that turns out to be false. You might have built your own, more accurate picture, but were steered elsewhere by people you assumed knew more than you. So view everything here with skepticism. Look for holes in the arguments, for the assumptions they rest on, and for whether the whole lens they use is wrong. Don't take this course the way you might take a course on thermodynamics, safely assuming most of it is right.

Treat each article and each idea as a character in a story. Each has its own perspective, blind spots and biases. You don't know which character to side with, because you don't know how the story ends. It may be that all of them are wrong, and the story is waiting for someone to see what is actually going on. That could be you.

Try reading each unit twice. The first time, assume the text comes from the future, from people who understand things better than you do. If an idea is hard to believe, remember how hard it would be to convince someone from the 14th century that the universe is billions of years old, or that all animals are distant cousins.

The second time, you are the one from the future. You slept through most of your AI classes, but on the holonet you found one of the first AI strategy courses ever written, from the 2020s. The people who wrote it may not know half as much as they think they know.

Each time, ask: what assumptions is this text making? If it were wrong, what would that look like? Where does the argument stretch reality to make it fit a certain shape? Does it explain too little, or too much?

\## Forecasting

"In three to eight years we will have a machine with the general intelligence of an average human being… In a few months it will be at genius level and a few months after that its powers will be incalculable."

Marvin Minsky, Turing Award winner and one of the founders of the field, said this in 1970.[^cite-life-1970] In 1965, Nobel laureate Herbert Simon predicted machines would be capable of doing any work a man can do within twenty years.[^cite-simon-1965] The 1956 Dartmouth workshop that named the field proposed that a carefully chosen group could make significant progress on machine intelligence over a single summer.[^cite-dartmouth-1955]

Others erred the other way. In 1997, astrophysicist Piet Hut said it might be a hundred years before a computer beat humans at Go, maybe longer.[^cite-nyt-1997] AlphaGo did it 19 years later. In 2021, Paul Christiano put the chance of an AI winning IMO gold by the end of 2025 at 8%, and Eliezer Yudkowsky at 16% or more.[^cite-imo-bet] AI systems reached gold-medal level at the IMO in July 2025. In September 2026, OpenAI announced that an unreleased model had solved the Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem. The proof has not yet been verified, and human researchers helped direct the work.[^cite-openai-ns]

In October 1903, the New York Times wrote that it might take one to ten million years to build a flying machine.[^cite-nyt-1903] The Wright Brothers flew 69 days later.

People have been bad at predicting technology, and especially AI, in both directions. Some thought it far easier than it was, others far harder. And the further out you look, the worse the predictions get. Geoffrey Hinton compares it to driving in fog: you can see clearly for a year or two, but ten years out, he says, we have no idea what's going to happen.[^cite-hinton-2023]

There is no expert consensus to lean on. Many intelligent-sounding arguments will turn out wrong, and some crazy-sounding ones will turn out right.

Imagine that in 2016, someone told you what the world would look like in ten years:

"There are going to be AIs that pass the Turing Test. You'll be able to talk to them, like in the movie *Her*, and some people will fall in love with them, while others just use them to do their jobs. Some people will be driven into delusions by them. Anyone will be able to make videos of famous people doing something silly, and it'll probably fool your dad. Parents will get a call from someone who sounds like their kid, saying they've been kidnapped and need ransom, but it'll be an AI voice clone. AI will get really good at math and coding. Researchers will catch AI agents cheating on the tests meant to evaluate them. People will quit the companies building them, saying they think there's a real chance everyone dies. The UN will set up panels on it. And a humanoid robot will run 100 meters faster than Usain Bolt, then crash into a mat at the finish line."

In 2016, most of that would have sounded like science fiction. All of it happened.

\## Pursuing AI strategy

> "If you cannot accurately predict the future then you must flexibly be prepared to deal with various possible futures." (Edward de Bono)

This field has an unusual problem. It speculates about technology that doesn't exist yet, and that we need to understand before we build it. That takes careful reasoning, and one faulty assumption can sink a whole worldview. Because the field is preparadigmatic, there are no clear directions to follow. It needs people who can find a direction no one else has seen, people bold enough to think they know better than everyone else, because some of them will be right.

But the field also has to update every few weeks as capabilities change. When you have an idea, check it against the current state of AI: is this the world you would expect to see if your idea were true? Where you can, test it. Reality can look at your careful reasoning and elegant arguments and throw them in the trash. Convincing the whole planet will not change that.

If a new development in AI confuses you, ask why. Reality is not confused. If something happened that you thought shouldn't be possible yet, the confusion is in your model, not in the world.

And if something seems obvious to you that no one else seems to see, work on it. Search for errors in your reasoning and check whether others have already hit a wall in the same direction, but don't assume something must already have been tried just because nobody is doing it. Even a path that turns out to be wrong shows the people after you where it leads.

\## What future do we want?

You did it! You built AGI! Every tech company and nation on Earth is taking note. Now what?

Keep scaling to superintelligence? How do you know it shares our interests, or that it isn't deceiving you? It just handed you the blueprint for an even more powerful system, and nobody understands how it works. Do you trust it? How would you check? Do you slow down? What do you do about rivals who are close behind? Use it to stop anyone else from building one? Put it to work on science? Make it a benevolent god? Make humans gods? How many people have to agree, and how do you avoid locking everyone into a future they decide, 100,000 years later, was a mistake?

Surprisingly little work has gone into what to actually do once we reach AGI. Even if someone solved alignment tomorrow and could prove it, that might only be the first part of the problem, and maybe not the hardest. Getting a trillion floating point numbers to do what you want is hard. So is figuring out what to do with eight billion humans, the trillions who may come after them, and whatever else the future brings. We can't just program everyone to want the same thing (well, we probably could, but we probably shouldn't). Individual people often don't know what they want.

Every option has a catch. An AI that only stops other dangerous AIs doesn't protect us from nuclear war, and it is a hard sell to the people building AI, who are the people you most need on board. An AI focused on science and technology may produce new existential risks. Making humans smarter step by step, so each step can be checked by slightly less smart humans, may need more patience than anyone has. Merging humans with machines runs into the fact that brains are much slower than silicon.

This question matters a great deal and almost nobody is working on it.

\## The Future You exercise

You're sitting wherever you are right now, reading this, when a dark void opens at the edge of your vision. A figure steps out. They look a bit different, but familiar. It's you, from the future, and you look good. After proving to you that they really are you (or someone with an alarming amount of embarrassing private information about you), Future You says the future turned out well, by your own standards.

Future You tosses you a single sheet of paper: the strategy that got humanity there.

1. Quick, write down what the paper says. Your best guess right now. If there is a good future, how did we get there?

Then Future You bursts out laughing. "That wasn't the real plan! I can't believe you believed that. Please tell me I wasn't that naive. Don't you see how that could obviously go wrong?"

2. Prove you aren't that naive. Imagine the plan being carried out. What goes wrong after a year? After ten? After a thousand? Who would refuse to go along with it, and why?

If you have time, keep going. Future You keeps handing you plans, swearing each one is the real one this time, then laughing. Each time, find how it fails. Finding a way a plan could fail doesn't mean you should drop it, but it helps you stop spending years on ideas you could have ruled out in an afternoon.

feedback-instructions:: The student has written a plan for how humanity reached a good future with AI, then tried to find ways it fails over time and who would resist it.

Nothing here is graded and there is no right plan. Do not argue for a different plan.

Look for:
- A plan concrete enough to fail: named actors and actions, not "we aligned AI".
- Failure modes that follow from the plan itself, not generic doom.
- At least one failure at a long horizon or one from people refusing to go along.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great start, excellent thinking, well done).

What to do in your single reply:
1. Reflect back the plan in one sentence, naming its key move.
2. Name the one failure mode they found that is most specific to their plan.
3. If they missed an obvious way it fails (a key actor refusing, a long-horizon lock-in, a rival ignoring it), name it in one sentence as something to consider, without resolving it.

If the student says they do not know what to write, give one foothold: an example plan in one sentence (for example, the major labs and governments agree to pause frontier training until evaluations show models are safe), and say a plan of that shape is all the exercise asks for.

This is a one-turn response. Do not invite further dialogue.

\## Your day-zero guess

Three things, written as guesses. At the end of every unit you will be asked to say them again from memory, and to change them.

**One.** What should be done about AI in the next two years? Who should do what, and what does it cost them? Make it concrete enough to be wrong.

**Two.** If that happens, what about it still matters in two hundred years? What would it have changed about people, about AI, and about who gets to decide things?

**Three.** What connects the two? How do the next two years shape the next two hundred?

Don't research this. It's a pre-test. If your honest answer is "I don't know", write your best guess anyway.

feedback-instructions:: The student is at the very start of the course and has just been told that half of what follows may be wrong. They have now written three guesses: what should be done about AI in the next two years, what that leaves standing in two hundred years, and the mechanism they think connects the two.

Do not grade correctness. There is no right answer here and saying so is part of the job. Do not ask them to commit harder or to defend the guess: the course never asks anyone to lock in a view.

Look for exactly three things:
- Concreteness at the two-year horizon: named actors, actions, or costs rather than "we should be careful".
- A mechanism between the horizons, even a wrong one, rather than a mood.
- First-person ownership: their guess, not a summary of what other people say.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great start, excellent thinking, well done).
- Do not argue with their guess. The course does that.

What to do in your single reply:
1. Reflect back what they actually guessed, naming one or two specifics. If the answer contains no specifics at all, say so in one sentence, quote the most general phrase they used, and say what a specific version would have to name: who does what, and what it costs them.
2. If one of the three is vague, name one place where a specific would help, as a thing to sharpen in their own head. Do not request a reply.
3. Tell them this guess gets said again from memory at the end of this unit, and that nothing they wrote today is a commitment.

If they say they do not understand, or write nothing substantive, do not ask again. Give one worked two-year answer in a single sentence, for example that the largest three labs publish evaluation results before release and it costs them lead time, say that a guess of that shape is all this asks for, and stop.

This is a one-turn response. Do not invite further dialogue.
