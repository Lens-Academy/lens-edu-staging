\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from Dennett and Strawson, without looking back. No need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read excerpts from Dennett's "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity" and the abstract and sections 1 to 4 of Strawson's "Against Narrativity", then wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- Dennett: a centre of gravity has no mass or colour, it is an abstraction, "a theorist's fiction", yet useful and well-behaved in physics. A self is also an abstract object we posit to interpret people, ourselves included. Looking for the self in the brain is a category mistake. Following Gazzaniga, the mind is a loosely joined set of partly autonomous systems that produces some unity, "enhanced by an illusion of greater unity". "We are all virtuoso novelists", and the chief character of the autobiography is the self.
- Strawson: two theses he rejects, the psychological Narrativity thesis (people do experience their lives as a story) and the ethical Narrativity thesis (they should, to live well). He separates experiencing oneself as a whole human being from experiencing oneself as a self. Diachronic people figure their self as there in the past and future. Episodic people do not, though they know they are the same human being. He is Episodic: no sense of his life as a narrative, memories from the inside that did not happen to "me*". The Episodic life is normal and can be a good way to flourish, and Diachronics and Episodics tend to misunderstand each other.

Be a brief, honest mirror: say what they got right without inflation, name what is missing, correct any error in one sentence, and treat gaps as normal. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. No follow-up questions and no generic praise. One reply, then tell them to move on.

\## Which are you?
Using Strawson's two descriptions, would you call yourself more Episodic or more Diachronic? Think of something you did ten years ago: does it feel like it happened to you, the self you are now, or to the human being you are?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Strawson's distinction: Diachronic people naturally figure their self as something that was there in the past and will be there in the future; Episodic people do not, although they know they are the same human being. Strawson says the two are not absolute, can vary with age, health and topic, and that neither is an inferior form of life. The learner now places themselves.

There is no right answer. Reflect their answer back in one or two sentences, using Strawson's terms accurately. If their answer is mixed, say Strawson allows that (embarrassing memories and thoughts of death can feel Diachronic even for Episodics). Ask one question that sharpens it, for example whether the answer differs for embarrassing memories versus neutral ones. Do not judge either style as better. 50 to 100 words. At most 2 replies. No generic praise.

\## Learning question
Someone says: "Dennett shows that the self is a story the brain tells. So everyone lives their life as a story, and people who don't have a coherent life story are missing part of themselves."

Using both readings, where does this go wrong?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Dennett (the self as a centre of narrative gravity) and Strawson (Against Narrativity), and got a plausible but flawed claim: "Dennett shows that the self is a story the brain tells. So everyone lives their life as a story, and people who don't have a coherent life story are missing part of themselves." This is practice, not a test.

Points a strong answer finds (any two are good):
- The step from Dennett's claim to "everyone lives their life as a story" mixes two things. Dennett's claim is about what the self is (an abstraction at the centre of the story told about a body's behaviour, by others and by the brain itself). Strawson's psychological Narrativity thesis is about how people experience their lives. One could accept Dennett's account and still allow that many people do not experience their lives as a story.
- Strawson denies the descriptive thesis: Episodic people like him have no sense of their life as a narrative, and he thinks this is a normal variation found in all cultures.
- "Missing part of themselves" is the ethical Narrativity thesis, that a narrative outlook is needed for a full life. Strawson rejects it: the Episodic life can be just as full, loving and responsible, and the past can shape the present without being remembered as one's own story.
- Dennett himself does not say coherence is required to be a self. He uses failures of coherence (two centres of gravity) to explain cases of multiple selves.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in 2 to 3 sentences, name one gap, and ask one or two direct follow-ups (for example: "Is Dennett's claim about what a self is, or about how people experience their lives?" or "What would Strawson say an Episodic person loses, if anything?"). If they are stuck after two attempts, give two of the points briefly. Do not say who is right. 100 to 180 words per reply. After 3 replies ask whether they want to continue. No generic praise.
