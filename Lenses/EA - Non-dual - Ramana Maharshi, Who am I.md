#### Text
content::
**Advaita Vedanta** is a school of Indian philosophy. In the words of the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, it holds that "the experiencing self (jīva) and the transcendental self of the Universe (ātman) are in reality identical (both are Brahman)", where **Brahman** is the ultimate reality ([Advaita Vedanta](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). The claim this module examines is Ramana's, in the text below: happiness is the nature of the self, and no object gives it.

Ramana Maharshi (1879 to 1950) was an Indian teacher in this tradition. He recommended **self-enquiry**, asking "Who am I?", as the main way to realise this, and his teaching has been popular in the West since the 1930s ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramana_Maharshi)). *Who Am I?* is a short text that sets out the method. It began as written answers to a visitor's questions around 1902. His ashram, Sri Ramanasramam, gives it away free ([gururamana.org](https://www.gururamana.org/Resources/Books/Who_Am_I_English.pdf)). Below are three parts of it: the opening, the method, and his answer to "What is happiness?". The translation is old-fashioned in places. "The mind becomes quiescent" means the mind becomes still.

#### Question: Open
id:: 87113f05-687b-4093-8fc4-96d8c74c54c1
content::
Ramana says that when you get something you wanted, the happiness does not come from the thing. It comes because the mind stops wanting and briefly rests in itself. Think of the last time you got something you really wanted. Does his account fit what you felt?
feedback-instructions:: The learner read three parts of Ramana Maharshi's Who Am I?: the opening (all beings want to be happy always; happiness is one's nature and is experienced in deep sleep), the method in question 11, and question 24: happiness is the nature of the Self, there is no happiness in any object, and when a desired object is obtained or a disliked one removed, the mind turns inward and enjoys the Self's happiness for a while before going out again (the shade and sun simile). Here they test that account against the last time they got something they really wanted.

The course explores and never says whether Ramana is right. Help the learner compare his account with what they remember. Useful contrasts, only where they fit: if the pleasure faded quickly or turned into a new want, that fits Ramana but also fits psychology's account of adaptation; if the pleasure seemed to be about the thing itself (its taste, a person's company) and not about the wanting stopping, that is a real finding against his account; relief at a want ending and enjoyment of the thing can be told apart by asking whether they would still enjoy it if they had never wanted it. If they are unsure, give one concrete foothold: "Right after you got it, was the good feeling more like relief or more like enjoying the thing?" 60 to 110 words. One reply, then send them on. No generic praise.

#### Text
content::
Now try the method in question 11 for five minutes. Sit, close your eyes, and let thoughts come. Each time you notice a thought, do not follow it. Ask "To whom has this thought arisen?". When the answer "to me" comes, ask "Who am I?" and stay with the question rather than answering it in words. The instructions are Ramana's, the five minutes are our suggestion.

If you start to feel unreal, cut off from yourself or emotionally flat, stop. If it does not pass soon after you stop, talk to a doctor or therapist. [Cheetah House](https://www.cheetahhouse.org/), founded by the meditation researcher Willoughby Britton, supports meditators in difficulty. The lens "When the sense of self changes" later in this module explains why.

#### Question: Open
id:: 137ee704-1b59-42d8-a1da-51f132638fe1
content::
What happened to your thoughts when you asked "To whom has this thought arisen?"
feedback-instructions:: The learner tried Ramana Maharshi's self-enquiry for about five minutes, following question 11 of Who Am I?: when a thought arises, do not pursue it, ask "To whom has this thought arisen?", and when the answer "to me" comes, ask "Who am I?". Ramana says the mind then goes back to its source and the thought subsides, and that with repeated practice the mind learns to stay in its source. The learner reports what happened to their thoughts.

Reflect their report back precisely in one or two sentences, keeping what they observed apart from what it might mean. If the thoughts stopped or thinned, ask one question: was there anything in the gap, and did it feel good, neutral or nothing at all? Ramana's happiness claim needs more than a pause in thinking. If the question turned into more thinking or nothing changed, treat that as a real result of a first try, not a failure, and mention that the experiments lens later in the module has a longer test. If they report feeling unreal, detached or flat in a way that did not pass, tell them to stop and point to Cheetah House (cheetahhouse.org). Do not tell them what they should have found. 50 to 100 words. One reply. No generic praise.
