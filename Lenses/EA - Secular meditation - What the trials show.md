#### Text
content::
Next, their summary of a large meta-analysis by Goyal and colleagues (2014). It only included trials with **active controls**: the comparison group did something else with the same time and attention, such as exercise or a health education class, so that expectation and attention alone could not explain a difference. An **effect size** here is the difference between the groups measured in standard deviations: 0.3 means the average person in the meditation group ended up about a third of a standard deviation better off than the average person in the comparison group. In Goyal's own numbers, the effect sizes were about 0.38 for anxiety and 0.30 for depression after eight weeks and 0.33 for pain, and they found no evidence that meditation programmes did better than drugs, exercise or other behavioural therapies ([Goyal et al. 2014](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1809754)).

#### Text
content::
Goyal's review only included people with a "clinical condition", which it defined broadly, from anxiety or stress to low back pain or advanced age ([Goyal et al. 2014](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1809754)). For adults in everyday settings, the largest review we found is a 2021 meta-analysis of 136 trials with 11,605 adults in nonclinical settings in 29 countries. Compared with no intervention, mindfulness courses reduced anxiety (effect size 0.56), depression (0.53) and psychological distress (0.45), and raised wellbeing (0.33), "in most but not all scenarios". The authors rated every trial at high risk of bias, partly because the outcomes were questionnaires filled in by participants who knew which group they were in, and partly because of missing outcome data ([Galante et al. 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1003481)). A friend says: "Science has settled it. Mindfulness makes people happier, and it works better than anything else you could do with that time."

Using what you just read, what is wrong with this claim?
assessment-instructions:: The learner is answering a question about the claim: "Science has settled it. Mindfulness makes people happier, and it works better than anything else you could do with that time." They have read evidence that mindfulness programmes reduce anxiety, depression and stress by small to moderate amounts compared with doing nothing (effect sizes roughly 0.3 to 0.6), raise wellbeing by a smaller amount (about 0.33 in nonclinical adults), show low, no or insufficient evidence for positive mood, and have not been shown to beat active alternatives such as exercise or other therapies. They also read that the research quality is weak (high risk of bias, self-reported outcomes) and that 'mindfulness' covers very different practices.

Score out of 100.
50: The answer says the evidence does not show mindfulness beats alternatives: compared with active alternatives such as exercise or other therapies, it has not been shown to do better.
30: The answer says the measured effects are modest or mostly about reducing negative states (anxiety, depression, stress), with smaller or uncertain effects on happiness, wellbeing or positive mood.
20: The answer names a reason the evidence is not "settled": weak study quality or risk of bias, self-reported outcomes, or that very different practices are lumped together as mindfulness.
Cap at 40 if the answer claims mindfulness has been shown not to work at all.
