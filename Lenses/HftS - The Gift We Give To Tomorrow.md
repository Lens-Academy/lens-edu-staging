#### Question: Open
id:: 3d0d45bd-e75b-4bc6-ba53-1b6ff313c846
content::
A friend says: "Evolution is blind and cruel. Yet it produced beings who care about beauty and kindness, and those are the things that really matter. That can't be a coincidence. Something beyond evolution must have steered it toward the right values."

Answer in your own words.

1. Why is this not the coincidence it seems, according to the essay?
2. Why does "something beyond evolution steered it" not explain anything?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The friend's claim: evolution is blind and cruel, yet produced beings who value beauty and kindness, which "really matter", and this is too big a coincidence, so something beyond evolution must have steered it toward the right values.

60: Explains why it is no coincidence: beauty and kindness seem like "the things that really matter" because we evolved to value them. We judge the outcome by the very values evolution gave us, so it is bound to look right to us, and there is nothing surprising to explain. Accept equivalent points, such as: the reasoning is circular, or a mind with no values would not be moved to care about beauty or kindness, or there is no standard outside us that our values happen to match. Give 30 if the answer only says that evolution can explain how these values arose (for example through kin love or reciprocal altruism) without saying why their seeming "right" is no coincidence.

40: Explains why the guide explains nothing: it only moves the question back, because we would then have to ask where that guide got its preference for love and beauty. Also accept: a complex thing like love has to come from something that is not already love, so an explanation that starts from something that already values love explains nothing.

Cap at 40 if the answer agrees that something beyond evolution is needed.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "We only think beauty and kindness are what really matters because evolution built us to care about them. So of course evolution's result looks like it picked the right values: we are judging it by the values it gave us. And saying a guide steered evolution just raises the question of where the guide got its love of beauty and kindness, so nothing has been explained."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "The Gift We Give To Tomorrow", a dialogue about how an uncaring evolution produced minds capable of love. The question gave them a friend who says the match between evolution's output and "the things that really matter" is too big a coincidence, so something beyond evolution must have steered it.

Key ideas from the essay:
- Evolution explains love, friendship and even altruism through particular adaptive contexts.
- It is no coincidence that evolution produced the values we find important: we call them important because we evolved to value them. The question is circular.
- There are no arguments that would move a mind with no values to care about beauty or altruism.
- A guide "beyond" evolution passes the recursive buck: where did the guide get its preferences? A complex pattern must come from something that is not already that pattern.
- Explaining love or a flower's beauty is not explaining it away. It can still be called a moral miracle.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Calm and direct. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent answer, well done).

If the score is below 100, name the single most important thing the answer missed or got wrong in what the question asks (why the match is no coincidence, and why an outside guide explains nothing), and ask one question that helps the student find it. The first and last key ideas are context you may mention, never gaps. At 100, confirm briefly what the answer got right, and you may add that the essay still calls the result a moral miracle. Comment only against the ideas above, which the essay supplied.

If the student says they do not understand, do not dismiss it or repeat the question. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, for example ask who decides that beauty and kindness are "the things that really matter", or ask where a guiding force would have got its taste for love. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different words.

Keep an internal count of your replies. After at most 2 follow-up replies, close with one sentence on what the student has understood and what to keep in mind, and tell them to move on to the next essay.
