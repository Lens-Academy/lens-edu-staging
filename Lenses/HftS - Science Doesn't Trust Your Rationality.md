tldr: Science works because it trusts no one's reasoning, not even its best scientists. It turns each scientist's stubborn wish to prove their own theory into a reason to run tests that could prove it wrong.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky draws an analogy between small-l libertarianism and Science. Both distrust reasonable-sounding arguments, both build systems more trustworthy than the people in them, and both accept that people are flawed and harness those flaws. The Scientific Revolution lasted because it said "trust no one, not even ourselves" instead of replacing Aristotle's armchair reasoning with different armchair reasoning. Science does not appoint impartial Eminent Judges. It harnesses each scientist's stubborn desire to prove their theory by rewarding new experimental predictions that are tested and replicated. It degrades gracefully when people play politics or skip replication. Because Science does not trust your probability calculations, letting Bayesian reasoning override Science's verdict is not a trivial step. The question describes a plan to replace testing with a panel of wise, impartial professors who decide by argument which theories are true, and asks which assumption about people the plan makes that Science refuses, and what Science relies on instead.

#### Question: Open
id:: d4002ff1-44d4-4bdc-989a-1d778a8153f6
content::
A research institute wants to speed up its work. It plans to stop waiting for experiments and instead appoint a panel of wise, impartial senior professors. They will read each new theory, argue it through carefully, and decide whether it is true.

Answer in your own words.

1. What does this plan assume about people that, according to the essay, Science refuses to assume?

2. What does Science rely on instead, and how does that make use of scientists' flaws?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The essay's idea: Science is built on distrust of reasonable-sounding arguments, including the reasoning of its own best people. It does not try to find impartial judges, because it assumes every scientist may be stubbornly attached to their own theory and cannot be trained out of it. Instead it lets experiment decide: a theorist wins by making a new prediction that is tested and replicated. This turns each scientist's stubborn wish to prove their own theory into a motive to run experiments that could prove it wrong, so the system is more trustworthy than its members.

Score out of 100.

50: Part 1. The plan assumes some people (the panel) can be trusted to reason impartially and reach the truth by argument. Science refuses this. Either of these ideas earns these points: Science assumes anyone, including eminent experts, may be biased, self-deceiving or attached to their views, or Science does not trust reasonable-sounding arguments on their own. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer only says the professors might be wrong, without saying that Science distrusts everyone's reasoning or impartiality.

50: Part 2. Science relies on experiment instead of anyone's judgement: theories must make predictions that are tested (and replicated), and the result decides. This uses scientists' flaws because a stubborn scientist who wants to prove their own theory has a reason to make predictions and run the tests, which can also refute it. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer names experiment or replication but does not say how it puts scientists' stubbornness or self-interest to use.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. It assumes the professors can be impartial and that careful argument will reach the truth. Science assumes nobody can be trusted that way, not even the experts, and that reasonable-sounding arguments are often wrong. 2. Science makes Nature the judge: you have to make a new prediction and test it, and others have to be able to replicate it. A scientist who stubbornly wants their theory to win has to put it to a test that could sink it, so their stubbornness ends up producing evidence."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Science Doesn't Trust Your Rationality" and judged a plan to replace experiments with a panel of wise, impartial professors. Key ideas from the essay: Science, like libertarianism, distrusts reasonable-sounding arguments and builds a system more trustworthy than its members. It does not choose impartial Eminent Judges, because it assumes every scientist may be stubbornly attached to their theory. It harnesses that stubbornness by rewarding new predictions that are tested and replicated. Replacing Aristotle's armchair reasoning with different armchair reasoning would not have lasted.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent recall, well done). Below a full score, name the single most important gap, usually that the answer did not explain how testing puts a scientist's stubbornness to use. At full score, confirm briefly and add that the panel would be swapping one set of armchair reasoners for another, as the essay says of Aristotle. Grade only against what the essay supplied.

If the student says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold: ask what a scientist who badly wants their theory to win has to do under Science to win. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on where their answer stands.
