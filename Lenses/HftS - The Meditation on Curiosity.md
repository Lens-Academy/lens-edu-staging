#### Question: Open
id:: a6b54214-28c8-4257-adc0-7428b5cc72bc
content::
Jon believes his new diet plan works. Feeling he ought to check, he reads two critiques of it, finds a reply to each, and stops. He is now "about 90% sure" the plan works, the same as before.

1. Using the essay, explain what has gone wrong in how Jon investigated.
2. Describe one of the methods the essay suggests and what it would look like for Jon's belief about his diet.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The essay's idea: criticising your beliefs out of a sense of duty makes you want to have investigated rather than want to investigate. You consider an objection, find a counterargument, and stop once you feel the duty is done, often keeping the inquiry on familiar ground and ending at the belief you started with. Real curiosity goes to the inquiries most likely to change your belief and is equally fine with a shift up or down. The essay's suggestions: watch for sparks of real interest or ignorance in your investigation and for possibilities you are flinching away from, remember that each new piece of evidence should be as likely to move you down as up, look at the belief's weak points rather than rehearsing support you already know, and use the Litany of Tarski (if X is true, I want to believe X, if X is false, I want to believe it is false, then think about the advantage of believing correctly if X is false).
50: Step 1. Explains that Jon investigated out of duty, to be able to say he had checked, and stopped once he had answered a couple of objections, so the inquiry was set up to leave his belief where it started.
50: Step 2. One of the essay's methods, applied concretely to the diet. Examples: seek out the strongest evidence against the plan, such as a careful study or his own measured results, rather than critiques he can easily answer, ask which new evidence could change his mind and be ready for it to move him down, or say the Litany of Tarski about whether the diet works and think about what he gains from believing it does not work if that is true. 25 if the method is named but not applied to the diet.
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Yudkowsky's "The Meditation on Curiosity" and answered a two-step question about Jon, who checked his belief in his diet plan out of duty and ended where he started. Key ideas from the essay: (1) duty makes you want to have investigated, not to investigate, (2) motivated stopping: answer an objection or two, then stop, (3) real curiosity looks where the belief could shift most and is fine with either direction, (4) the essay's methods: watch for sparks of interest and for possibilities you flinch from, expect each new piece of evidence to be as likely to move you down as up, attack weak points rather than rehearse known support, and use the Litany of Tarski.

Reply in 80 to 150 words, in short paragraphs, with no lists. Do not over-validate and avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Name the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important thing the answer missed or got wrong, then ask one question that helps them fix it. At 100, confirm briefly and tell them to move on. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on how well they have the essay's idea.

If the student says they do not understand, do not dismiss it and do not repeat the question. Give one concrete foothold from the essay: the contrast between "wanting to have investigated" and "wanting to investigate", or the Litany of Tarski about the diamond in the box, and ask what the same litany would say about Jon's diet. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms. Judge only against what the essay says, and treat as a gap only what the question asks.
