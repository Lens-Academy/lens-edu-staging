#### Text
content::
Understanding an AI safety organisation in some depth can inform both career decisions and judgements about the direction of the field. Its choice of research problems, underlying assumptions, and record of accomplishments provide context for interpreting its findings. Much of this information is scattered across publications and other sources, and the organisation's official description may lag behind its actual activities. Reconstructing its actual work therefore requires some investigation.

The following template can serve as a starting point for your investigation:

- **Research agenda and theory of change**
- **Activities and outputs**
- **Network and collaborations**
- **Team and resources**
- **Influence on the field**
- **Organisational culture and work environment**

We recommend using both Google and LLMs with web access to gather this information. This should save you some of the legwork. You will, however, need to examine the sources yourself, particularly where the LLM offers an interpretation of an organisation's strategy or impact.

\### Research agenda and theory of change

Start with the organisation's research agenda, strategy documents, and announcements of new research directions.

Search for publications by its founders and research leads on the Alignment Forum, LessWrong, and the EA Forum. Some of the most informative exchanges occur in the comment sections of posts written by researchers pursuing different agendas.

Search for publications by the organisation's founders and research leads. Try to reconstruct the argument that led them to pursue their current line of work. It is worth reading some of their older writing too: changes in an organisation's strategy can reveal which of its original assumptions have survived contact with research.

External reconstructions of research agendas can also help. Two examples are [(My understanding of) What Everyone in Technical Alignment is Doing and Why](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/QBAjndPuFbhEXKcCr/my-understanding-of-whateveryone-in-technical-alignment-is) and the annual [Shallow Review of Technical AI Safety](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Wti4Wr7Cf5ma3FGWa/).

\### Activities and outputs

Between the Alignment Forum, arXiv, GitHub, institutional blogs, and Twitter (X), there is no single publication channel through which you can follow the work of the entire field. Some substantial research contributions exist exclusively as LessWrong posts. Occasionally, the clearest explanation of a research project's motivation, or even an organisation's theory of change, can be found in a Twitter thread by one of its researchers and nowhere else.

Look through several years of an organisation's publications and projects to establish where its efforts have been concentrated. Retrospectives and annual reports may reveal unsuccessful experiments and discontinued projects that are missing from its publication archive.

For a picture of what the organisation is doing *now*, look at its recent job advertisements. The problems it needs to hire someone to solve can be quite different from those described in its founding documents.

\### Network and collaborations

Institutional boundaries in AI safety are fairly porous. Someone employed by one research organisation may supervise fellows at another while developing a third research agenda with independent collaborators.

Paper affiliations, acknowledgements, fellowship mentor lists, workshop programmes, and joint project announcements can help you reconstruct these connections. Following the work of individual researchers is often necessary to understand how two organisations are related. Repeated collaborations are especially informative.

\### Team and resources

Team pages and LinkedIn profiles are reasonable starting points, although the distinction between employees, affiliates, advisors, and visiting researchers is sometimes poorly documented. Keep this in mind when estimating an organisation's size and research capacity.

AI safety has an unusually useful source of public information about institutional finances: many of its grantmakers publish descriptions of individual grants. Explore [AISafety.fund](https://aisafety.fund/grants), Coefficient Giving's grant database, and EA Funds' payout reports.

A grant description may contain information absent from the recipient's website, including planned hires, experimental budgets, the duration of a project, and the funder's reasoning for supporting it. Comparing grants over several years can help you reconstruct the development of an organisation.

\### Influence on the field

Recall the distinction between outputs and outcomes from the previous modules. Once you know what an organisation produces, try to establish what happens to its outputs after publication.

Google Scholar can help you trace academic citations. On LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, you can find researchers attempting to replicate results, disputing conclusions, or incorporating someone else's findings into their own research agendas.

Look for evidence appropriate to the organisation's theory of change. For an evaluations organisation, this might mean finding its methodologies in a developer's safety reports. For an educational organisation, it might involve tracing the subsequent work of its alumni. For a policy organisation, examine whether any of its proposals have entered legislative or regulatory discussions.

\### Organisational culture and work environment

In 2023, the authors of [Critiques of prominent AI safety organizations](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/HzqCgzvieqDASFXa5/critiques-of-prominent-ai-safety-organizations-introduction) drew attention to an asymmetry in the AI safety community: established researchers could learn a great deal about organisations through private conversations, while newcomers had access to a much narrower range of information.

If you are considering working for an organisation, there is considerable value in talking to people who have worked there or collaborated with its team.

Ask them to describe actual episodes from their working lives. How did their last research project originate? How was the decision made to abandon an unsuccessful project? What did they spend most of their time doing?

For an illustration of how much these conversations can reveal, see [What is it like doing AI safety work?](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/TrfCQiutKZz5CJfeG/what-is-it-like-doing-ai-safety-work), based on interviews with ten researchers.

Remember that a senior researcher, a junior employee, and a short-term collaborator may have experienced quite different versions of the same organisation.

Important note: Leave blank spaces in your account where you have been unable to establish something. An LLM will often produce a plausible answer to every question in the template, even when the public evidence is scant.

If you are investigating a prospective employer, these blank spaces give you a set of organisation-specific questions to bring to an interview or a 1-1 with a team member.
