#### Question: Open
id:: 162f2e93-599e-4346-94f3-dd97ccf947aa
content::
The essay ends with a homework problem: what kind of cognitive algorithm, felt from the inside, would produce the debate about "free will"? Here are three replies a reader might send in.

**A.** "Free will is incoherent. Every event is either caused by earlier events or random, and neither of those is freedom. So the question has no answer."

**B.** "Our ancestors who believed they had free will felt more in control and looked after their lives better, so evolution left us with the belief."

**C.** "When I decide, my brain imagines each option as something 'I could do' before it has worked out which one it will pick. Not yet knowing its own output feels, from the inside, like the options being open out in the world. The debate is people arguing over whether that feeling describes something outside their heads."

Which reply is the kind of answer the homework asks for, and why? Why do the other two not count?

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay argues that proving a question meaningless or a concept self-contradictory does not explain why people still feel the question. It also separates "explaining why" a belief exists (for example an evolutionary story about why believing in free will helped people) from "explaining how" the mind produces the intuition, by describing the mental process step by step. The homework asks for the second kind: an account of the mental process that generates the intuitions behind the free will debate. Reply C attempts that. Reply A argues the concept is incoherent. Reply B gives an evolutionary reason why the belief exists. Whether C is true as cognitive science is not what is graded.

40: Picks C, with a reason that amounts to: it describes the process in the mind that produces the feeling of free will, which is what the homework asks for.

30: Says why A does not count: it argues that free will is incoherent or the question meaningless, which the homework rules out, or which leaves unexplained how the mind produces the feeling that there is a question. Either reason earns full marks for this element.

30: Says why B does not count: it explains why people might have the belief (a story about its usefulness to our ancestors), not how the mind produces it. The belief stays an unexplained given.

Cap at 30 if the answer picks A or B as the kind of answer the homework asks for.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "C, because it tries to show how the mind generates the feeling of free will, step by step. A just argues the concept doesn't make sense, which doesn't explain why people still feel they have it. B gives a reason why the belief might have been useful, but it doesn't say how the brain produces the feeling."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Dissolving the Question" and sorted three replies to its free will homework. The essay's key ideas: refuting a question, defining it precisely, or calling it meaningless leaves the feeling of a question unexplained. "Explaining why" a confusion exists (an evolutionary or social story) is not "explaining how" the mind produces it. You are done only when you can walk through the mental process step by step and no feeling of a question is left. C attempts "how". A refutes the concept. B is a "why" story of the kind the essay calls bogus.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap, often treating A as a solution because it sounds rigorous, or not saying what makes B a "why" story. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example what A leaves unexplained about the person who still feels free. Do not debate whether C is true. At 100, confirm briefly.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as its contrast between the social-status story about the tree argument and the neural network in "Feel the Meaning". If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
