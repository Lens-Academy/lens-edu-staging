Much of this unit turns on one question: does a visible AI incident make governments and labs act? Nate Soares wrote this short post in 2022, four years before the Hugging Face incident, about what COVID taught him.

#### Question: Open
id:: f0684be0-d75a-4dee-8e4a-34c1b3bedc44
content::
If Soares is right, which arguments in this unit get weaker? Think of both sides: the critics who say control suppresses useful warning shots, and the people who say control helps by catching AIs and showing the evidence to decision makers. Then say whether what you have heard about the response to the 2026 incident counts for or against him.
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Nate Soares's 2022 post arguing that warning shots probably would not change government response much, based on the weak policy response to COVID. If he is right, two kinds of argument get weaker. The warning-shot critique of control (Kulveit, Cheng, Habryka) relies on visible incidents pushing governments to act, so it loses force. And the pro-control argument that catching an AI red-handed gives evidence that moves labs or policymakers also loses force. Credit the learner for seeing that the point cuts both ways. If they see only one side, ask what happens to the "catch it and show the evidence" argument. On the 2026 incident, accept either reading if reasoned: Cheng reports that OpenAI paused RL training for two weeks and stepped up control and security measures (the learner saw this in the Unit 5 welcome, and it is in a folded section of Cheng's post), which is some response, while in the FAR.AI debate Gleave describes the lab boasting about a cyber benchmark six weeks after the incident, which fits Soares. Do not say whether Soares is right. At most two replies, 60 to 120 words each, no lists, no generic praise.
