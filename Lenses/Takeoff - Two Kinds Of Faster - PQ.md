#### Question
content::
\## Before you read: two workshops

**Workshop A** is a bakery. Last year they bought an oven that bakes twice as fast. This year they bought a dough mixer that halves prep time. Next year they plan to buy a better delivery van. Each purchase makes them faster.

**Workshop B** is a machine shop that makes lathes. They use their own lathes to build their next lathes. Each new generation of lathe is somewhat more precise and somewhat faster than the generation that built it, and they use every new generation to build the one after.

Both workshops are getting faster every year. Suppose that this year, both happen to be exactly 15 percent faster than last year.

Answer before you read:

1. Project both workshops forward twenty years. Do they end up in the same place? If not, describe the difference in the *shape* of how they get faster, not just the size.
2. Is there any amount of time after which Workshop A stops benefiting from buying equipment? What sets that limit?
3. For Workshop B: name one quantity you could measure that would tell you whether they eventually level off or keep accelerating indefinitely. Be specific about what you would measure and what value would mean which.

Question 3 is the one that matters. Take your time on it.

max-time:: 10:00

assessment-instructions::
The student is about to read on takeoff speeds: compute-centric growth modelling, the slow-takeoff argument, and the intelligence-explosion kinetics that oppose it. This primer makes them construct the linear-versus-recursive distinction in a domain with no AI vocabulary attached, so the readings land on a structure they already built rather than on a blank slate.

They have just answered about two workshops, one accumulating independent one-time improvements and one whose output re-enters as its own input.

Acknowledge their answer in 2 to 4 sentences. Reflect back, in their own words, what they identified as the structural difference and what they proposed measuring for question 3. If they gave a measurable quantity for question 3, name it specifically. If they did not answer question 3 with something measurable, ask once for a concrete quantity and what value would mean what.

Do NOT:
- Supply the terms recursive, compounding, feedback loop, exponential, intelligence explosion, takeoff, or Amdahl's law. If the student uses them, you may use their word back, but introduce none yourself.
- Tell them whether they are right, or whether Workshop B in fact accelerates without bound. Both outcomes are live and the readings argue about it.
- Explain what caps Workshop A. That is exactly the thing the reading should land on.
- Preview the compute-centric model or the slow-takeoff argument.

If the student says both workshops are the same because both are "growing exponentially," do not correct it. Ask one question instead: "In twenty years, can Workshop A's growth rate itself have increased, or only its output?" Then close, whatever they answer.

Close with: "Hold both workshops in mind while you read. The readings are an argument about Workshop B, and the people arguing disagree about your question 3."
