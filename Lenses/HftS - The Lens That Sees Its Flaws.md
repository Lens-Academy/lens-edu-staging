title: "The Lens That Sees Its Flaws"
tldr: Your brain is a flawed lens on the world, but unlike a mouse's it can understand its own flaws. Noticing when a belief comes from what you hope rather than from evidence lets you correct it.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky argues that human minds, unlike mouse minds, can reason about how they form beliefs. This is what makes science possible and what lets a person correct their own systematic errors. His example is a man who expected no nuclear war for 100 years out of "pure hope". A belief chosen because it feels good tells you about yourself, not about the world, and across many possible worlds it would not line up with the outcome. A footnote answers the reply that hope changes outcomes (it makes you work harder). The effect is far too small to justify the shift in belief. The question asks the student to apply both points to a founder whose 90% success estimate rests on hope.

#### Question: Open
id:: 34447713-2919-40b2-b93b-2da5a5a7e539
content::
Priya is about to launch a startup. Asked how likely it is to survive its first three years, she says 90%. She explains: "Honestly, part of it is that I can't bear to imagine it failing. But my confidence will make me work harder, so my hope is part of why it will succeed."

1. In your own words, why is "I can't bear to imagine it failing" not a good reason for a 90% estimate?
2. In your own words, what is wrong with her argument that her hope will make her work harder?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The essay's idea: asking which belief makes you happy tells you something about yourself, not about the world. Across many people in Priya's position, how much each one hopes would not line up with whose startup actually survives, so hope is not evidence and should not move the estimate. The essay also answers the "hope makes me work harder" reply: the effect may be real, but one person's hope changes the actual outcome only slightly, so it cannot justify the large shift it caused in her belief.
50: Step 1. Says that her reason is about her own feelings or wishes, not about the startup or the world, so it is not evidence about whether the startup survives (for example: wishing does not make survival more likely, or founders who hope hard are not the ones whose startups survive more often).
50: Step 2. Says that even if hope makes her work a bit harder, its effect on the real chance of success is small, much smaller than the jump in her estimate that her hope produced, so it does not justify a 90% estimate.
Cap at 50 if the answer consists mostly of quoted sentences rather than the student's own explanation.
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Not wanting it to fail is a fact about Priya's feelings, not about the startup. Plenty of founders feel the same and still fail, so the feeling tells her nothing about the odds. Her hope might make her work a little harder, but that can only raise the real chance a little, while it raised her estimate a lot. The size of the effect doesn't match the size of the shift."
