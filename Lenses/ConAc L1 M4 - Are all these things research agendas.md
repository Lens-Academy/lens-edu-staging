#### Text
content::
So far, we have looked at research agendas deliberately articulated by the people pursuing them.

However, when people attempt to describe the AI safety research landscape, they also talk about agendas that have no identifiable author, no shared research plan and sometimes very little coordination between the researchers involved.

Should we think of these as the same kind of thing?

Let's move away from technical alignment research for a moment and look at some examples from AI governance (we can do that because the same patterns exist in both areas). We define AI governance as the processes and structures through which decisions related to AI are made, implemented, and enforced

In May 2025, Peter Barnett and Aaron Scher from MIRI's Technical Governance Team published [AI Governance to Avoid Extinction: The Strategic Landscape and Actionable Research Questions](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04592v1). The authors organise their research agenda around four possible scenarios for AI development and the geopolitical response to it.

#### Text
content::
Notice the organising principle here: the authors begin with possible trajectories of AI development and governance, and derive research questions from the uncertainties and challenges associated with each trajectory.

Consider international AI agreements, for example. Researchers might investigate their political feasibility, the incentives for states to participate, the legal mechanisms available for establishing them, or the technical means of verifying compliance.

These questions draw on different academic disciplines and require different research methods. What connects them is their relevance to a particular governance strategy.

However, international agreements could also be studied in the context of several different governance strategies. Similarly, research on compute governance, information security or international institutions could contribute to multiple scenarios described by Barnett and Scher.

So how many research agendas are we looking at here? One overarching agenda, four scenario-specific agendas, or dozens of smaller agendas organised around particular governance problems?

The answer depends partly on what we are trying to understand about the research landscape.

\### What are we grouping together?

Let's look at three examples from AI governance. This time, we will pay particular attention to what connects individual pieces of research into something we might call an agenda.

**Example 1. Compute governance**

In the first module, we discussed the relationship between computational resources and AI capabilities. This relationship has implications beyond our ability to predict technological progress. It also creates opportunities to influence that progress.

Unlike many other inputs to AI development, computational resources have physical and economic properties that make certain forms of governance feasible. Advanced chips need to be manufactured, transported, purchased and operated. Their supply depends on a relatively concentrated industrial infrastructure.

In [Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08797), Girish Sastry, Lennart Heim, Haydn Belfield and their co-authors (2024) explain why these properties matter and what kinds of governance they might enable.

The opportunities described in this paper motivate a diverse collection of research activities. Researchers investigate how to improve our visibility into AI development, how to allocate computational resources to socially beneficial activities, and how to enforce restrictions on potentially dangerous uses of compute.

They also investigate the risks introduced by these interventions, including the concentration of economic and political power.

For example, a researcher might study the feasibility of using cloud providers to monitor AI development, as explored in [Governing Through the Cloud](https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/governing-through-the-cloud), or investigate how changes in semiconductor supply chains could affect the enforceability of AI regulation.

What connects these activities is a shared interest in computational resources as a potential lever for governing AI.

**Example 2. AI forecasting**

Consider a government deciding how much to invest in developing its capacity to regulate advanced AI. Its decision will depend, among other things, on how soon it expects particular AI capabilities to become available, how rapidly they might improve and how widely they might be adopted.

Similar uncertainties affect decisions made by AI developers, safety researchers and funding organisations.

Obtaining more reliable answers to these questions is itself a substantial research endeavour.

**Example 3. Research supporting a particular governance strategy: AI 2040**

Both compute governance and AI forecasting encompass research that could inform a wide range of governance strategies. There are also research programmes in which the choice of research questions is guided by a much more specific vision of what should happen.

One example is the work conducted by the AI Futures Project in developing [AI 2040: Plan A](https://ai-2040.com/). Published in 2026, Plan A proposes a strategy for navigating the transition to superintelligence through international coordination, research transparency and controlled increases in AI capabilities.
