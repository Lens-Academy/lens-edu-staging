#### Text
content::
Jason Hausenloy divides much of AI safety into two broad strategic camps:

- **“Alignment is hard but tractable”**: responsible actors win the race, use control and evaluations, and automate more alignment work.
- **“If anyone builds it, everyone dies”**: prevent superintelligence through political action, public persuasion, and international coordination.

This is an **opinionated map**, not a neutral or exhaustive taxonomy. The labels, membership, and implied assumptions are Hausenloy's reconstruction; real people and organizations often hold mixed or more nuanced views.

Read the two excerpts as candidate theories of victory. Your task is to understand and steelman each causal story before judging it.

#### Question: Open

content::
Reconstruct the strongest version of Camp 1's theory of victory as a causal chain, from present-day action to an acceptable outcome.

Which three assumptions are most load-bearing? For each, say whether Hausenloy presents it as the camp's explicit claim, an implicit premise, or his own criticism.

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Context for grading: Jason Hausenloy's article describes Camp 1 ("alignment is hard but tractable") under the theory "US wins the race + automated alignment". In his account the United States or a responsible lab keeps the lead, alignment is an engineering-shaped problem that improves by iterating on existing techniques, control, evaluations and warning shots give useful feedback, near-frontier AI speeds up alignment research, and responsible institutions then pause or deploy safely. He lists explicit claims of the camp (alignment is an engineering problem, the US or a responsible lab should win the race, control and evaluations can work, warning shots are useful, lab leaders are mostly well-intentioned, automating alignment is a top priority, a pause is unrealistic) and implicit premises he infers (current techniques are on the right track, the winner's values matter more than the act of building, the bottleneck is researcher-hours and not conceptual clarity, deception is detectable at a rate that matters, AI can help solve alignment without being aligned first, some existential risk is the price of the best outcome). His own criticism doubts three things: that alignment is an engineering problem and not a scientific one, that lab leaders can be trusted to act benevolently, and that the leader will pause right at the edge. 40: a causal chain for Camp 1 that starts from present-day action and ends at an acceptable outcome, with each step leading to the next, told as a proponent would tell it. A chain that only caricatures the camp (for example "labs race recklessly") earns at most 10 of these 40. 30: 10 for each of three assumptions that the chain depends on. Any assumption the camp's plan needs in order to work counts, not only those listed above. 30: 10 for each of those assumptions that the answer labels as the camp's explicit claim, an implicit premise or Hausenloy's own criticism, in a way that fits the article. Where the article supports two labels for one assumption (for example "alignment is an engineering problem" is an explicit claim that Hausenloy also doubts), accept either. For an assumption that the article does not state, the label earns its 10 when the answer says that Hausenloy does not state it, or gives the closest of the three labels. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording.
feedback-instructions:: The student has read Hausenloy's account of Camp 1: the United States or a responsible lab maintains the lead; alignment is engineering-shaped and improves iteratively; control, evaluations, and warning shots provide useful feedback; near-frontier AI accelerates alignment research; and responsible institutions pause or deploy safely.

Help the student steelman the causal chain before criticizing it. A good reconstruction distinguishes:
- What proponents explicitly claim.
- What Hausenloy infers as an implicit assumption.
- What Hausenloy himself argues or doubts.
- Intermediate outcomes from the actual win condition.

Correct source-attribution errors plainly. If the student reduces the view to “labs race recklessly,” ask them to reconstruct why a proponent thinks leadership, automation, and iterative evidence improve safety. Then press on the most load-bearing handoff.

Response length: 120–190 words. Ask at most two precise causal questions. Avoid generic praise and do not present this taxonomy as consensus.

#### Question: Open

content::
Now reconstruct Camp 2's strongest theory of victory. Then compare the two camps across four fault lines:

1. Is alignment primarily an engineering problem or an unsolved scientific problem?
2. Is the decisive bottleneck technical capability or political coordination?
3. Are warning shots likely to be useful?
4. Is the safer strategy to win and control the race, or prevent anyone from finishing it?

For each camp, identify its most vulnerable transition from “something helpful happens” to “the full plan succeeds.”

The excerpt finishes with several other theories of victory. Choose one. Does it fit inside either camp, combine parts of both, or expose a different strategic axis altogether? Name its own most load-bearing assumption.

force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Context for grading: Jason Hausenloy's article maps two camps in AI safety. Camp 1 ("alignment is hard but tractable"): alignment is an engineering problem that improves by iterating on existing techniques, the bottleneck is technical, warning shots are useful (catch scheming, retrain), and the right actors (the United States, a responsible lab) should win the race and use control, evaluations and automated alignment research. Camp 2 ("if anyone builds it, everyone dies"): alignment is an unsolved scientific problem with one critical try, the problem is political (buy time through collective action), there is no right winner of the race, and superintelligence should be banned through a treaty. Camp 2's chain is roughly: communicate the danger clearly, build public and political support (helped by a crisis that wakes people up), secure a superintelligence ban or international treaty, verify and enforce it, and keep a stable equilibrium in which nobody races. Hausenloy's critique of Camp 2 is that it may depend on a warning shot that is legible and not catastrophic, on persuasion reaching people with power, on governments acting competently and fast enough, and on a stable global stopping point. His critique of Camp 1 doubts that alignment is an engineering problem, that lab leaders can be trusted, and that the leader will pause right at the edge. The other theories he names: defensive acceleration and resilience, liability and insurance mandates, state regulation, crisis-driven coordination, AI deterrence, and market-led solutions. 20: Camp 2's theory of victory as a chain from action to outcome, told as a proponent would tell it. 32: 8 for each of the four fault lines on which the answer says where each camp stands, in a way that fits the article. On warning shots accept any reading the article supports (Camp 1 counts on them as feedback, Camp 2 hopes a crisis wakes people up, or doubts that catching and retraining helps). 24: 12 for each camp, for naming a specific step in that camp's chain as its most vulnerable one, the point where something helpful happens and the full plan may still fail. Any step of that camp's chain counts, not only the ones Hausenloy criticises. 24: for one of the other theories, 12 for saying whether it fits inside a camp, combines parts of both, or shows a different strategic axis, in a way that is plausible for that theory, and 12 for naming an assumption that theory depends on. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording.
feedback-instructions:: The student has read both of Hausenloy's camps and his brief list of other theories of victory. They should compare the two main camps without mistaking the author's map for a neutral census of the field, then use one alternative to test the limits of the two-camp framing.

Camp 2's proposed chain is roughly: communicate the danger clearly, build public and political support, secure a superintelligence ban or international treaty, verify and enforce it, and maintain a stable non-racing equilibrium. Hausenloy's critique is that this may depend on a legible non-catastrophic warning shot, persuasion reaching powerful actors, governments acting competently and quickly, and a stable global stopping point.

The alternatives named include defensive acceleration and resilience, liability and insurance mandates, state regulation, crisis-driven coordination, AI deterrence, and market-led solutions. These are sketches rather than complete or neutral accounts; help the student articulate the strongest plausible causal chain before evaluating one.

A strong comparison:
- Steelmans both camps in their own terms.
- Identifies the major technical-versus-political and race-versus-ban fault lines.
- Separates explicit claims, inferred premises, and Hausenloy's objections.
- Finds a concrete weak transition in each full causal chain.
- Uses one alternative theory to reveal overlap, a hybrid, or an omitted strategic axis.

If the student treats one camp or alternative as obviously foolish, ask for its strongest internal rationale. If they accept the map too literally, ask where real agendas might cross the boundaries.

Response length: 160–240 words. Be analytically neutral about which strategy is correct. Avoid generic praise.
