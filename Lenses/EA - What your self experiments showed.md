#### Text
content::
You have a baseline and ratings after two or more experiments, plus your predictions and what you found. This page puts them together.

#### Question: Open
id:: bbb9c490-8956-4997-81b6-18f5ca16422a
content::
Compare your ratings after each experiment with your baseline. Which ratings, if any, went up, and after which experiment?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner rated three NADA-S state items (boundaries of self dissolving, mind expanding into space, blissful warmth or energy; 1 to 10) after a 5-minute baseline of sitting with eyes open and after each experiment (Harding's pointing, Ramana's self-enquiry, optionally Mahasi noting and a Loch Kelly glimpse). They now report how the ratings compare.

Restate their pattern in one or two sentences. Then note one caution that fits it: a difference of a point or two on one try may be noise; the order of the experiments, time of day and expectation can all move ratings; and the NADA authors say such scales capture nondual awareness only partly. If all ratings stayed near the baseline, say that is a real result too. 50 to 100 words. One or two replies. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: 63d8608d-099b-41de-8392-a1cf1e1d18a6
content::
Which of the six changes in the sense of self from Lindahl and Britton, if any, did you notice during the experiments: narrative self, ownership, agency, embodiment, self-other or self-world boundaries, or basic self?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner did the module's self experiments and now names which of Lindahl and Britton's six changes in sense of self, if any, they noticed: change in narrative self (one's story of who one is), loss of sense of ownership (thoughts, body or feelings not felt as mine), loss of sense of agency (no one doing the actions), change in sense of embodiment (self located elsewhere or outside the body), change in self-other or self-world boundaries (expansion or no separation), loss of sense of basic self (feeling of not being there).

Check that the category they name fits what they describe, and suggest the better fit if not (for example "no head here, just space" is mostly embodiment and self-location, possibly boundaries). "None" is a valid answer. If they describe a change that was unpleasant or is still present, especially loss of basic self, agency or embodiment, point them to the stop rule and help options on the method page and suggest not repeating that experiment. 50 to 100 words. One or two replies. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: 30d51e71-e79f-4485-bc4c-455f5412d1d6
content::
Which claim from the comparison table do your results bear on, and do they count for it or against it?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner has done the module's self experiments and now says which claim from the module's comparison table (not-self as a method, no-self, Advaita's Self as awareness, IFS's Self, Hume's bundle, Parfit, Dennett, Strawson, Metzinger, Seth, Thompson, Wright) their results bear on, and in which direction.

Engage with the reasoning. Point out where a first-person result can bear on a claim (for example whether looking inward finds only passing perceptions, as Hume says, or a remaining awareness, as Ramana says; or whether letting go of identification eased stress, as Thanissaro's reading predicts) and where it cannot (claims about what exists beyond experience, about the brain, or about words like "illusion"). Ask one question about what would make their evidence stronger. Do not say which view is right. 60 to 110 words. One or two replies. No generic praise.

#### Text
content::
One try, by one person who knows what each experiment is meant to show, is weak evidence. The NADA study you took the rating scale from dealt with this in three ways ([Hanley, Nakamura and Garland 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6265073/)): it compared the practice with an active control (listening to an 11-minute reading), it randomised who got which, and it called both "attention training strategies" so participants did not know which was meant to work. You cannot blind yourself fully, but you can repeat each experiment on several days, alternate it with a control of the same length, and keep your ratings in one place. Module 3 covers how to design such a single-case experiment, and module 8 asks you to choose which experiments to continue.
