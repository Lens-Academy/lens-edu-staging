summary_for_tutor: "Unit 3 of AI Control Fundamentals (why people work on control). The learner reads the abstract, background and argument structure of 'A sketch of an AI control safety case' (Korbak, Clymer, Hilton, Shlegeris, Irving, January 2025, UK AISI and Redwood Research). Main points: safety cases are structured, evidence-based arguments that a system is safe, emerging as a risk-management tool for frontier AI. A control safety case argues that a model is incapable of subverting the control measures, using a control evaluation where a red team builds models that try to exfiltrate data in a proxy deployment. The argument rests on: (1) the estimated exfiltration probability is below the acceptable threshold, and (2) the evaluation upper-bounds the real probability, which needs (2a) the evaluated protocol to be at least as effective as the deployed one, (2b) the red team's attacks to upper-bound what the deployed model could do, and (2c) conservative extrapolation from 2,000 proxy tasks to the larger deployment. The authors hope it prompts discussion between frontier labs, government agencies and civil society about what assurance is sufficient. In this unit it stands for the route where control becomes a condition of deployment, set by a company's own policy or by regulation. It is also a voice from a government institute, not only from Redwood. Then the learner reads two excerpts from the appendix on internal usage monitoring of Anthropic's 'Risk Report: August 2026', which covers Claude Mythos 5 and an unreleased Model 2, used heavily inside Anthropic. The report rates misalignment risk 'low' (raised from 'very low' to reflect more uncertainty). Measures: an automated offline pipeline (a completion-only monitor on extended thinking, a prompt+completion monitor on a 25% sample of possibly impactful tool calls from common coding scaffolds, about half of agent tokens seen, a second-stage filter, about 50 trajectories a week reviewed by AI control researchers), people reviewing past code and research, cyber detection, external users' reports, pull-request review with security-invariant tests, sandboxing and blocking classifiers, and weight security. A Lens text between the excerpts gives one evaluation result (the current monitor flags 94% of 163 METR red-team attacks, 79% after the second-stage filter). Listed limitations: offline monitoring does not cover every internal surface or external deployments, no end-to-end evaluation and no adaptive red-teaming of the latest monitor, and asynchronous monitoring would not prevent a first successful irreversible step such as self-exfiltration. A Lens note after the excerpts summarises the weight-security appendix (limits on outgoing data, two-person approval, scoped against non-state attackers and unsophisticated insiders) and quotes the same report: mitigations give 'less protection than might be intuitively expected' against unknown pervasive misalignment, the low estimate there 'comes primarily from a belief that it is very unlikely', and the Long-Term Benefit Trust has not requested an external review, nor does the RSP require one. This is the condition-of-deployment route in practice, written by the company itself, with control covering residual risk. Four scored questions follow. So far the routes have mostly run through one company's safety team. This paper, by researchers at the UK AI Security Institute and Redwood Research, adds the last route of this unit. It sketches a different use of control: as the evidence in a "safety case", an argument a developer could be asked to make before deploying a model. Shlegeris is one of its authors. About three weeks after it came out, in the interview you just watched, recorded in February 2025,[^cite-80k-shlegeris-2025] he said that using control to get AI companies to "pretty substantially low levels of risk from scheming models" now "just seems pretty implausible", so he had "stopped thinking about the really high-assurance stuff". The sketch calls itself a best guess, not a high-assurance case. The middle part of the post, on how the evaluation is run, is collapsed. You met the method in Unit 1.

\## A company that relies on monitoring

Anthropic publishes regular risk reports under its Responsible Scaling Policy. Below are parts of its [Risk Report: August 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/aug-2026-risk-report), a report of nearly 190 pages: first its summary of the risk from misaligned models, then its appendix on how it monitors its own models while its staff use them.

Two terms the report uses:

- **Asynchronous monitoring:** checking an AI's actions after they have run.
- **Blocking:** stopping an action before it runs.

The report then tests these monitors. In one test, a staff member of METR, an outside organisation that evaluates AI models, had built 163 attacks while trying to get past an earlier version of the monitor. The current first-stage monitor flags 94% of them, and 79% are still flagged after the second-stage filter that decides what the human reviewers see. The appendix goes on with the other ways Anthropic can notice or stop a bad action.

#### Text
content::
The weight security appendix the excerpt points to lists controls such as limits on how fast data can leave its systems, which make copying multi-terabyte model weights out of Anthropic slower and easier to detect, and a second employee having to approve access to model weights. It scopes these protections against most non-state attackers and unsophisticated insiders, not against sophisticated insiders or nation-state attackers.

Elsewhere, the same report says its mitigations give "less protection than might be intuitively expected" against a model that is misaligned in most situations without Anthropic having noticed. For that case it writes that its low risk estimate "comes primarily from a belief that it is very unlikely, rather than that we are well positioned to mitigate any harm it might cause". It also notes that its Long-Term Benefit Trust, which may request an outside review of risk reports, "has not requested an external review (nor has the RSP required that we conduct one)", though Anthropic has "continued to conduct pilot external reviews" of parts of its reports, for example with METR.
