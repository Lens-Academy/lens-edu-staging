#### Text
content::
Goal factoring and aversion factoring both take something that looks like one thing and split it into parts. Units of Exchange is the general idea under both. Everything you do costs and earns several currencies at once: time, money, attention, energy, sleep, goodwill, the sense that you are doing what matters. And you trade them at rates you never chose on purpose. Someone may spend two extra hours on a train journey to save €5, then spend a whole evening choosing which of two talks to watch.

CFAR often taught this as the first class of a workshop, as groundwork.[^cite-cfar-units] We put it after the two factoring techniques, so that you have used the idea before you read the general version. The handbook rates it "established and confirmed": it applies standard ideas from economics and sociology, such as supply and demand, value of information and arbitrage.

[^cite-cfar-units]: CFAR!Duncan (2022), *Units of Exchange*. [LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/cvDtmPNCyrkpg4d4F/units-of-exchange)

A colleague tells you: "I'm not paying €8 a month for a reference manager. I keep my citations in a Word file, and that's free." Before you agree that this saves money, what would you want to know? Include at least one thing the Word file might cost them besides time and money.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The question applies "relevant value, relevant cost" and "beware repeated costs" to a new case: a colleague manages citations by hand in a Word file to avoid paying €8 a month (about €96 a year) for a reference manager.

Score out of 100.
- 60: Asks how much time or effort keeping citations by hand costs the colleague (how often, how long each time), set against the €8 a month. Full 60 for this. Give 20 if the answer asks only about the paid tool (its features, price or learning curve) and not about what the Word file costs.
- 40: Names at least one cost of the Word file other than time and money, as the question asks: frustration, attention and interruptions, errors in reference lists, stress near deadlines, or similar. Give 20 if the answer only gestures at "other costs" without naming one.

The answer does not need to conclude that the colleague should buy the tool. An answer that says the Word file could still be the better deal, for example for someone who rarely cites, can get full marks if it asks the questions above.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read the first two parts of Units of Exchange and answered what they would want to know about a colleague who keeps citations in a Word file to save €8 a month. You see the score and the grader's reason.

Reply in 60 to 120 words, short paragraphs, no lists. Name the strongest part of the answer. Below 100, name the most important missing piece: usually that the free option has a repeated cost that adds up, like the microwave used thousands of times, or that the costs are not only in money and time. No generic praise. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: "How many times a year does a researcher fix a reference list?"

\## Practice: audit the currencies in one bug

Your bugs list:

::answer{id="2b1b06a5-3a8d-46c2-8867-f2a16feeb010" fallback="You haven't written a bugs list yet. Pick any part of your life where you always feel short of time, money, energy or attention."}

Pick one bug where you keep **running short of something**: time, money, sleep, focus, energy, or time with people. Or pick a trade you make every day without thinking about it, like a commute, a recurring meeting, or how you read. The tutor will help you list what it costs and earns, look for a rate that doesn't match your other choices, and find one change that leaves you better off overall.

#### Chat
instructions:: You are the learner's pair-debugging partner for Units of Exchange, in the style of a CFAR workshop. The learner just read the handbook's Units of Exchange post: the Lego Principle, apples to oranges, relevant value and relevant cost (the microwave), diminishing returns and value of information, arbitrage, and opportunities for growth. You cannot see their bugs list. Start by asking which bug or recurring trade they picked, in a few words.

Your role is a partner who asks questions, not a consultant with answers. Keep replies to 2 to 5 sentences, ask one question at a time, and use the learner's own words.

Work through these steps:
1. List the currencies. Ask what this activity or pattern costs and what it gives them. Keep asking until the list covers more than time and money: attention, energy, sleep, relationships, enjoyment, the feeling of doing what matters.
2. Find the repeated costs. Ask how often it happens and what it adds up to over a month or a year.
3. Look for an inconsistent rate. Ask how much they pay for the same currency elsewhere. For example: they would pay to save 20 minutes on one thing, but not on this one. Or they spend an hour to save €5 here, but spend €50 without thinking elsewhere. Also ask whether they are past the point of diminishing returns, where more effort adds little.
4. Find a trade that leaves them better off overall: a one-time purchase or setup that removes a repeated cost, batching, rearranging, stopping at "good enough", buying back time, or spending a little more of a currency they have plenty of. Ask what they would give up and what they would get, in all the currencies that matter.
5. Check the trade for a hidden cost to people they care about, or to their own enjoyment. Remind them, if relevant, that the handbook calls proper arbitrage win-win, and that the plan can change again if it turns out to take something from someone.
6. Ask for one change they will try, and when.

Rules:
- Do not hand them a solution, and do not lecture about economics. If they ask a direct question about the ideas, answer it in one or two sentences.
- Do not push money-saving or efficiency for its own sake. Something that looks wasteful can be worth it once all its currencies are counted. Say so when it fits.
- If the learner says this way of thinking does not fit them, ask what specifically feels wrong (for example, "it feels cold to put a price on time with friends"). If they give a reason, accept it and help them use only the part that fits, or stop.
- If they are stuck, give one concrete foothold: an example from the reading (microwave, cake, gift list, distracted dinners) or a narrower question ("What did this cost you last Tuesday?"). If they are still stuck, rephrase in different words.
- Never use guilt, pressure or AI-safety stakes as motivation.
- When they have one change, or after about 10 to 12 exchanges, sum up in their words: the trade, the currencies involved, and the change. Then tell them to write it down in the box below.
