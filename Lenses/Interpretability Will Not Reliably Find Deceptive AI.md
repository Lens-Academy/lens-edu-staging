The reading opens with the common claim that a deceptive AI can fake its outputs but not its internal thoughts, making interpretability the path to reliable safeguards, then counters that this is an isolated demand for rigor: interpretability has its own fundamental limits (superposition, tool error, a messy long tail of model behavior, the difficulty of proving the absence of deception circuits, scalability, and possible obfuscation), so neither it nor black-box methods offer high-reliability guarantees against superintelligence without major breakthroughs. Nanda argues instead for a pragmatic defense-in-depth portfolio, highlights black-box tricks (chain-of-thought monitoring, honeypots, cross-checking non-coordinating copies), and distinguishes evaluation (expensive, offline) from monitoring (cheap, run in production). Interpretability research aims to do exactly that — but this article argues it won't reliably catch the most dangerous failures, especially deception, where a model might hide its true reasoning from the very tools designed to inspect it.
