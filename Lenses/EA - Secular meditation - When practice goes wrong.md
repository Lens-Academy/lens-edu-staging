Ingram warns about the Dark Night, and Shinzen Young about "too much too soon". The research below asks how often secular mindfulness courses cause problems and why the numbers were missing for so long.

Willoughby Britton's group then did what most trials had not: an independent assessor asked every participant of an eight-week course of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) about 44 kinds of possible side effect. The participants had mild to severe depression symptoms, so they were not a cross-section of healthy people.

#### Text
content::
The teachers give their own advice. Shinzen Young writes that practice can bring up "too much too soon", that people starting intensive programmes should be told beforehand what they may meet, and that the remedy is to change the technique, reduce practice time, or both. He says that what he calls the Dark Night, a serious difficulty in adjusting to a less fixed sense of self, is "relatively rare", even among people who practise intensively ([What is Mindfulness?](https://www.shinzen.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/WhatIsMindfulness_SY_Public_ver1.5.pdf), section I). Ingram uses the same name for a broader stretch of stages and says that anyone who crosses the Arising and Passing Away stage will go through it to some degree ([MCTB2](https://www.mctb.org/mctb2/table-of-contents/part-iv-insight/30-the-progress-of-insight/5-dissolution-entrance-to-the-dark-night/)). So the two teachers do not simply disagree about how common one thing is. They partly describe different things.

#### Question: Open
id:: 34ed7f09-ee03-443b-882b-072f9c6b3f65
content::
Suppose you start one of the experiments in this module. Which signs would tell you the practice is starting to harm you?
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Van Dam et al. 2018 on how meditation trials measure harms (fewer than 25% actively ask, spontaneous reports may undercount more than 20-fold) and the abstract of Britton et al. 2021 (MBCT, n = 96, independent interview: 83% at least one side effect, 37% with negative impact on functioning, lasting bad effects in 6 to 14%, linked to hyperarousal and dissociation, at rates similar to other psychological treatments). Earlier in the module Ingram claimed the Dark Night follows the Arising and Passing Away stage for everyone who crosses it, and Shinzen Young (if they read that lens) advised changing technique or cutting practice time when too much comes up too soon. The learner now names warning signs for their own experiment.

Reply in 60 to 110 words. Check that their warning signs include something concrete (for example trouble sleeping, agitation, feeling unreal or detached, panic, old memories flooding back, low mood that lasts beyond the session). If they are vague ("if it feels bad"), give one or two concrete examples from Britton's finding (hyperarousal, dissociation) and ask them to name one they would actually notice. If they mention suicidal thoughts or severe symptoms now, tell them plainly to stop the practice and contact a doctor or a crisis line. Do not frighten them: Britton's own conclusion compares the rates with other psychological treatments. No generic praise. One turn.

#### Question: Open

content::
What will you do if you notice one of those signs? Write it as a rule: which sign, for how long, and what you will change.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just named warning signs that a meditation experiment is harming them (after reading Britton et al. 2021: lasting bad effects in 6 to 14% of an MBCT course, linked to hyperarousal and dissociation, and Shinzen Young's remedy for 'too much too soon': change the technique, reduce practice time, or both). They now write a stopping rule. A good rule names a sign, a threshold (for example two nights of poor sleep, or feeling unreal after a session more than once), and an action (shorten sessions, switch to a gentler technique, pause, talk to a teacher or doctor). If any part is missing, name it and suggest one concrete version. If they mention suicidal thoughts or severe symptoms now, tell them plainly to stop the practice and contact a doctor or a crisis line. Reply in 60 to 110 words. No generic praise. One turn.
