content::
Ingram warns about the Dark Night, and Shinzen Young about "too much too soon". The research below asks how often secular mindfulness courses cause problems and why the numbers were missing for so long.

First, Van Dam and colleagues on how trials record harms.

#### Text
content::
Willoughby Britton's group then did what most trials had not: an independent assessor asked every participant of an eight-week course of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) about 44 kinds of possible side effect. The participants had mild to severe depression symptoms, so they were not a cross-section of healthy people.

#### Question: Open
id:: 34ed7f09-ee03-443b-882b-072f9c6b3f65
content::
Ingram says the Dark Night is close to inevitable once you cross a certain stage. Britton's study found lasting bad effects in 6 to 14% of an eight-week course. If you start one of the experiments in this module, what will you watch for, and at what point would you cut back or stop?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Van Dam et al. 2018 on how meditation trials measure harms (fewer than 25% actively ask, spontaneous reports may undercount more than 20-fold) and the abstract of Britton et al. 2021 (MBCT, n = 96, independent interview: 83% at least one side effect, 37% with negative impact on functioning, lasting bad effects in 6 to 14%, linked to hyperarousal and dissociation, at rates similar to other psychological treatments). Earlier in the module Ingram claimed the Dark Night follows the Arising and Passing Away stage for everyone who crosses it, and Shinzen Young (if they read that lens) advised changing technique or cutting practice time when too much comes up too soon. The learner now names warning signs and a stopping rule for their own experiment.

Reply in 80 to 130 words. Check that their warning signs include something concrete (for example trouble sleeping, agitation, feeling unreal or detached, panic, old memories flooding back, low mood that lasts beyond the session). If their stopping rule is vague ("if it gets bad"), ask them to make it specific: which sign, for how long, and what they will do (shorten sessions, switch technique, talk to a teacher or doctor). If they mention suicidal thoughts or severe symptoms now, tell them plainly to stop the practice and contact a doctor or a crisis line. Do not frighten them: Britton's own conclusion compares the rates with other psychological treatments. No generic praise. One or two replies.
