\## How to Use AI tools well for Impactful Research

Used well, AI can accelerate the kind of research that matters, it can help you read faster, think more clearly, and catch your own blind spots. Used carelessly, it can potentially hand you confident made up facts, or quietly replace your own thinking with something more generic. This guide is to help you use this tool wisely and to amplify your impact, rather than replace your own unique skills and ideas!

*Adapted with thanks to Melissa Kohler from Circles of Compassion.*

\## The key principle: your thinking first, AI second

Before anything else, we recommend that you try generating your own ideas before you bring AI into the picture. Sit with the question for a bit, brainstorm, sketch out your own rough first take, using your own reasoning and the specific context that only you have. Then bring AI in to build on that, stress-test it, and help you refine it further.

This isn't about avoiding AI. AI is good at extending and sharpening a real idea you've already started. It's much weaker at coming up with something genuinely novel or truly yours from scratch, and if you skip straight to asking it to generate something, you'll often end up with the same plausible, averaged-out answer everyone else gets who asked it the same thing. Your own first-pass thinking is what makes the work yours, AI's job is to help you sharpen it, not replace it!

In practice, this might look like: jotting down your own rough research question before asking AI to help sharpen it. Draft your own take on what a literature might say before asking AI to check you against it. Sit with a stuck point in your own thinking for a bit before asking AI what you might be missing. The order really does matter here!

This matters for your impactful journey specifically, not just for good research practice. Part of what you're building through this whole process is your own capacity to think clearly, form judgment, and design your own path, and those are genuinely human skills that can't be outsourced or replaced. AI can support that development; it can't do it for you.

\## Key Risks in AI Use

A language model functions by generating text that's likely, given what came before. It's not actually looking anything up, unless it's been given a tool or instruction to do so. So when it produces a citation from memory, it's really just producing text that looks like a plausible citation for that claim, which may or may not correspond to a real paper. This is why models can sometimes hallucinate with total confidence!

**A few key risks to be aware of:**

- **Fabricated citations.** A citation you haven't checked with your own eyes doesn't exist yet, as far as you're concerned. Treat it that way until you've verified it.
- **False confidence.** The model sounds equally sure of itself whether it's right or completely making something up, so confidence in the writing tells you nothing about accuracy.
- **Weak recall, strong reasoning.** It's much, much better at reasoning over text you actually give it than at recalling text it wasn't given. As such, it is best if you can upload the exact material you’re interested in rather than asking it to remember. Paste in the abstract, upload the PDF, use a search-enabled mode. Its accuracy on "here's a paper, what does it say about X" is enormously higher than on "what does the literature say about X."
- **Reputational and trust risk.** If a fabricated citation or an overstated claim slips into an application, a research proposal, or a piece of published work, it doesn't just cost you the correction, it can harm how much people trust your work going forward.
- **AI-sounding writing.** Text that's gone straight from an AI tool into your application, thesis, or outreach email often has a detectable rhythm and phrasing, and more people (admissions committees, reviewers, potential collaborators) are actively checking for it now than even a year ago! Beyond the ethics of it, you may be more likely to go further in applications or outreach if you took some time to write in your own words.

\## The three modes, and when to use each one

Most AI tools give you roughly three different ways of working, and they have very different reliability. Mixing them up is the main source of trouble, so it's worth knowing which is which.

**Mode 1: Asking from memory.** You ask a question, and the model answers from its training. Fast, but no citations, no verification.

- Great for: orienting yourself in a new area, explaining a concept, getting the vocabulary of a field, brainstorming search terms, sanity-checking your own reasoning (see 4.0!), or asking "what am I missing?"
- Not for: any specific fact, number, or citation that's going into your actual write-up.

Think of it like chatting with a well-read friend at a party. Great for pointing you in a direction, but you'd still check anything before quoting them!

**Mode 2: Search-enabled.** The model runs real web searches and answers based on what it finds, with links attached. Much more reliable, since the claims are anchored to actual retrieved documents.

- Great for: finding out whether something exists, locating specific papers, checking a claim, or getting the current state of a question.
- Still worth checking: that the linked source actually says what the summary claims it says. Misreading a source happens less often than fabrication, but it does happen!

**Mode 3: Deep research / research reports.** The tool runs lots of searches over several minutes, reads across sources, and produces a long structured report with citations. Much more thorough than a single search.

- Great for: the orientation phase, mapping out an unfamiliar area fast, getting a first sense of where a literature is dense or thin, or generating a candidate list of important papers and concepts.
- Its real weak spots:
   - It doesn't actually know what's important! It weights sources by how findable they are, not by quality, so a blog post and a meta-analysis can end up carrying similar weight.
   - It tends to smooth over disagreement. Reports often read as consensus even where a field is genuinely split, and that flattening is exactly the thing you need to catch, since the report itself will hide it from you.
   - The citations still need checking, usually they're real, but the claim attached to them can be a stretch.

Use these reports as a map, not as a source in themselves! They tell you where to look. What actually goes in your review is what you've read and judged yourself.

💡 These runs are usually rate-limited to a set number per month, so it's worth spending them on the broad, hard-to-scope questions at the start of a section, rather than on something you could answer with a single search anyway.

\## Elicit as an AI Research Tool

Elicit is an AI research assistant built specifically for academic literature, and one of the most useful tools for this kind of work, because it works over actual papers rather than the open web.

- *Quick note: Elicit's features and limits change over time, so what follows describes its general capabilities, do check your account for exactly what's available and how many runs you have before planning around it. If something here doesn't quite match what you're seeing, trust what you're seeing!*

**What you can use Elicit for**

1. **Paper search.** You describe what you're looking for in plain language rather than keywords, and it returns relevant papers. It's a genuinely different way of searching than Google Scholar, it'll surface things keyword search misses, and miss things keyword search finds. Worth using both, they complement each other rather than replacing one another.
2. **Data extraction tables.** This is honestly the standout feature! You gather a set of papers, and Elicit builds you a table with one row per paper. You can then add your own custom columns, in your own words ("what was the sample size and population?", "how was the outcome measured?", "what effect size is reported?"), and it pulls that field out of every paper at once. This turns "read 30 papers and note the same six things about each" into a table you can just scan, it's the single biggest time-saver available to you here.
3. **Uploading your own papers.** You can upload PDFs and ask questions directly, or include them in your extraction tables. This is the most accurate way to use any AI tool, since it's working from the actual document rather than from memory. If you've got institutional access to a paper, download it and upload it, you'll get much better answers than from the abstract alone, and it lets you dig into the methods section, which is honestly where the answers to most of your quality questions live.
4. **Reports and systematic-review functions.** Elicit can also produce structured summaries across a body of literature, at varying depth. These are usually run-limited too, so save them for the broad questions at the start of a section.

**Two things worth knowing:**

- It shows you exactly where in the paper it pulled each answer from. Use that! Whenever an extracted value really matters, a number going into your write-up, a claim you're building on, click through and check it against the actual source text.
- Treat it as roughly 80% accurate. Great for building an overview, mapping a literature, spotting patterns. Not something to lean on for a specific number you're about to base a recommendation on without checking it yourself.

\## How to prompt well

The difference between a mediocre AI output and an excellent one is almost entirely in the prompt. Four things make most of the difference.

1. **Give it context and purpose.** The model tailors its answer to what it thinks you actually need, so tell it!
   1. *Weak:* "What affects empathy in children?"
   2. *Strong:* "I'm doing a literature review to identify factors in a child's environment that cause compassion to develop, with the goal of finding factors an intervention could realistically change. I need to know which environmental factors have the strongest evidence behind them. I care more about causal evidence than correlational, and I need to know how confident I should be in each. What are the main factors, and what's the state of the evidence for each?"
2. **Ask for structure.** Tell it what shape you want the answer in, a table with these columns, a list ordered by evidence strength, three paragraphs. Unstructured requests tend to get unstructured answers that are just harder to use.
3. **Ask for uncertainty, explicitly.** Models default to sounding confident, so push back on that directly: *"For each claim, tell me how confident you are and why. Flag clearly anything you're unsure about or where you might be misremembering. If you don't know, say you don't know rather than guessing. Do not give me a citation unless you're confident the paper exists."* This genuinely works, and it's worth adding to almost every research prompt you write.
4. **Ask for what's against your position.** This is the most useful, and most underused, prompt of all: *"What's the strongest argument that this factor doesn't matter? What would a sceptical researcher say about this evidence? What am I likely to be missing?"* Models are agreeable by default and will happily run with whatever frame you hand them, so you have to explicitly ask for the other side.

**A few more things worth doing:**

- **Iterate rather than restarting.** Push back, ask for more depth on one point, ask it to redo a section. Honestly, the second and third turns are usually where the real value shows up.
- **Ask it to explain things at your level.** "Explain what a cross-lagged panel design is and what it can and can't establish, assuming I know basic statistics but haven't done research before."
- **Use it to critique your own writing.** "Here's my summary of this evidence. Am I overstating anything? Would a critical reader object to any of these claims?" This is really just 4.0 in action, hand it your own draft and ask it to sharpen it, not replace it.

\## What to use AI for, and what to keep for yourself

**Use it for:**

- Explaining concepts, designs, and statistics you don't know yet
- Orienting yourself in an unfamiliar area
- Generating search terms and finding papers
- Summarising papers you've given it
- Extracting the same fields across many papers at once
- Checking a specific study against a checklist
- Critiquing your own drafts
- Asking what you might be missing

**But keep for yourself:**

- Your novel ideas, the genuinely original ones are yours to generate, not to outsource
- Sitting with what you're actually interested in, before optimising or refining anything
- Judging how much weight the evidence can actually bear. This is the real work, and it's the part that needs you holding the whole picture at once
- Deciding what actually matters for your decision. The model doesn't know your constraints
- The final claims in your write-up. Every single claim should be one you've personally traced back to a source you've actually read

⭐ A good rule of thumb: AI can bring you the material and help you understand it. But you should be the one doing the judging and understanding of it! If you ever notice you've written something you couldn't defend from a source you've not read, it is worth revisiting.
