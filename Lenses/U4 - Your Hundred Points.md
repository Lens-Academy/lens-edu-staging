\## The unsettled question

This unit put two rival pictures of the lab on the table and never adjudicated between them. Buck Shlegeris plans for ten safety-minded people inside a company that will not slow down, because inside the company is where the marginal influence is: ten is much better than zero. Alex Kastner's scenario replies that the most dangerous person in the story may be the one those ten people report to, with every AI in the building doing exactly what it is told. If he is right, the insiders are watching the models while the threat is the org chart. One lab, two readings of it: the solution's home, or the threat's.

The course takes no side on that, and you do not need one settled to finish this unit. What you need is a budget. Money, careers, political attention, and your own hours all get spent somewhere, and refusing to choose is itself an allocation, made by whoever moves first. So the unit ends the way real strategy arguments end: someone puts numbers on the table.

*The framing and questions on this page were written by Claude, an AI, and reviewed by a human.*

#### Question
content::
\## Your hundred points

You have 100 points. Treat them as the next unit of influence the world gets to spend: funding, talent, political capital. Split them across these levers, whole numbers, zeros allowed.

- Technical alignment work: research on making systems aim at what their developers intend, the work every plan in this unit eventually leans on.
- Treaties and international coordination: agreements between states to cap, monitor, and verify frontier development, a family that also includes evals-tied if-then commitments and pause campaigns. If you want the detail, the optional treaty lens in this unit has it.
- Inside-lab practice: what a safety-minded minority inside a racing company can still do with limited influence.
- National project or deterrence: putting the frontier under state control, or credibly threatening someone else's.
- Anti-concentration guardrails: making sure obedient AI cannot quietly put one person in charge of everything.
- Buying time: anything that slows the frontier long enough for the rest of these to work.
- Other: a lever this unit missed. Name it before you fund it.

Then write two more lines.

First, the line that connects this to Unit 3. That unit ended with rival claims about where the risk lives: inside the systems, or between the institutions that build them. Your ranking of risks IS your allocation of points. Say whether yours actually matches, and if your biggest line item does not follow from where you put the risk, say what else is driving it.

Second, find the lever you shortchanged most. Write the strongest objection its advocate would make to your biggest line item. One sentence, their best shot, not a strawman.

Keep this allocation. Unit 5 will make you redo it with your name attached.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is the final lens of Unit 4, in a course about AI futures for newcomers. There is no reading attached. The unit walked through rival strategies for reducing AI risk, one lens each, and the student has just been asked to allocate 100 points of influence across those levers, to say whether the allocation follows from where they placed the risk at the end of Unit 3 (the aim problem inside the systems, versus the dynamics between the institutions that build them), and to write the strongest objection to their biggest line item from the advocate of the lever they most shortchanged.

UNIT 4'S READINGS, so you can recognise references. For your reference only. Do not deliver them.
1. Plans A, B, C, and D for misalignment risk, Ryan Greenblatt: which plan is best depends on how much political will the world supplies, from a strong international agreement all the way down to a handful of concerned people inside an unwilling company.
2. Ten people on the inside, Buck Shlegeris: what a small safety-minded minority inside a racing lab can still do with cheap, low-overhead measures.
3. Should the US do a Manhattan Project for AGI?, Delaney, Anderson-Samways, and Wildeford: a national AGI project is neither inevitable (forecasters put it near one in three) nor clearly a good idea, and treating it as inevitable can trigger the risks it is meant to beat.
4. Two short pieces checking the race premise: Karson Elmgren showing that the supposed 2017 Chinese AGI goal rests on one ambiguous phrase in one 2017 planning document, and Epoch data putting Chinese models about seven months behind the US frontier.
5. Alex Kastner's scenario of an AI company CEO taking over the world through secretly loyal AIs, with alignment assumed solved: the case for guardrails against power concentration.
An optional lens covered a proposed international agreement in detail (Scher and colleagues, MIRI Technical Governance Team). The student may not have read it. Do not assume they did.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Classify the allocation in the student's own terms and say it back in one line: you bet on institutions, you bet on the aim problem, you bet on time, whatever their numbers actually say. Use their words where you can.
2. Ask ONE question about the least-defended line item: the points that got spent without a reason appearing anywhere in what they wrote. Not the biggest item, the least defended one.
3. If their objection line was a strawman, ask what the advocate would say back to it. If it was strong, leave it alone.

Ninety points on one lever, defended, is a proper answer. An even spread defended only by fairness is the one to probe.

Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise. There is no correct allocation at this stage.

Write 60 to 110 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
