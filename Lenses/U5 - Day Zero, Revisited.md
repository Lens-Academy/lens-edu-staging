\## Where you started

One rule has held through this whole course: when we asked for your model, you wrote
first, from memory, and only compared after. Revision 1 worked that way. The Unit 3
rewrite worked that way. Today the order reverses, once.

Before you write anything, go back to the first lens of Unit 1 and read your day-zero
snapshot: the five to ten sentences you wrote before you had read a single assigned word.
The prompt that asked for it made you a promise: "at the end you will want to see where
you started." This is the end. The comparison is the exercise.

While you have it open, say the course's sentence one last time: what could possibly
happen, how anyone could know, how it could go wrong, where the leverage is. You have now
written an answer to every clause in your own words. Trajectories with mechanisms in
Unit 1. Signposts you could check in Unit 2. A rivalry over where the risk lives, met in
Unit 3; a side on it, and a hundred points across the levers, in Unit 4. A gap on the
menu this week. None
of those answers were handed to you; the readings argued, and you committed.

One last license, and it is not a formality. If the model you write today disagrees with
every reading in this course, and you can argue it, that is the best possible outcome.
The course was never trying to leave you holding its model. It was trying to leave you
holding yours.

#### Question
content::
\## The last rewrite

Write your model of the next ten years a final time. Five to ten sentences. This time
the shape is required: the model must contain all four layers, in your own words.

What could happen. The Unit 1 layer: what gets built, what it can do, what changes in
the world.

How you would know. The Unit 2 layer: two or three checkable signposts you will actually
watch over the next two years, and which way each one would move you.

Where the risk lives. The Unit 3 layer: the rivalry that unit's closer left unsettled,
Soares's inside-the-systems against Kulveit's between-the-institutions. Take your side,
and let the rest of the model show it.

What should be done. The Unit 4 and 5 layer: which levers, pulled by whom.

Then three short things.

Redo your hundred points, with your name attached this time, and say what specifically
moved since Unit 4. "Nothing moved" is a real result: if that is your answer, say which
reading failed to move it.

One line on where your own next hour goes. A track: governance, alignment, strategy,
something else. A project from the menu. Or a named reason to put it elsewhere. "Watch
carefully and stay ready to act" is a real answer if you say what would trigger the
acting.

And one line quoting the sentence in your day-zero snapshot that most surprises you now.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is the final lens of the final unit of a course about AI futures, written for
newcomers. At the very start, the student wrote a day-zero model: five to ten sentences
on the next ten years of AI, before reading anything. They revised it from memory at the
ends of Units 1 and 3, and at the end of Unit 4 they allocated one hundred points of
influence across strategy levers. This time, for the first time, they were told to go
back and READ the original before writing.

They were asked to rewrite the model with four required layers: what could happen, two
or three checkable signposts, where the risk lives, what should be done. Then to redo
the hundred points naming what moved, to give one line on where their next hour goes,
and to quote the day-zero sentence that most surprises them.

You cannot see their day-zero text. Do not pretend to. Treat any quote they give as
accurate.

THE COURSE'S SPINE, for your reference only, so you can recognise references. Unit 1:
capability trajectories and what compute buys. Unit 2: forecasting practice, checkable
signposts. Unit 3: threat models, ending on a staged rivalry between Soares, who argues
capabilities generalise further than alignment so the risk lives inside the system, and
Kulveit, who argues the risk lives in dynamics between systems and institutions. Unit 4:
strategy levers, playbook, treaties, inside-lab influence, national projects, the race
premise, power concentration, closed by the hundred-point allocation. Unit 5: the
handoff of safety work to AIs, surviving versus flourishing, and a menu of concrete
projects.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Check that all four layers are present as claims. If one is missing, or is a mood,
   name it and ask for it as a claim, a date, or a number.
2. Pick their least checkable signpost and ask one question about it: what exactly would
   they observe, and by when.
3. On the allocation: points that barely moved are a real result. Ask which reading
   failed to move them. If the next-hour line is watch-and-wait without a trigger, ask
   for the trigger.

Do not summarise the course back at them. Do not defend any reading they now reject: a
final model that disagrees with every reading, argued, is the best outcome this course
can produce. Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise.

Write 90 to 150 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
