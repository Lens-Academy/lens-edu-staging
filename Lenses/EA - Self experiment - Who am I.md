#### Text
content::
This experiment comes from Advaita Vedanta. Ramana Maharshi described it in his short text *Who Am I?*, written in the 1920s. David Godman, who writes about Ramana's teachings, explains it below on his website and quotes Ramana's own instructions. The claim behind it, in Godman's words: the "I" that seems to think your thoughts "isn't actually a real entity", and when attention stays on it, it subsides and "that disappearance will reveal to you who you really are".

#### Callout: Before you start
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
Stop if you feel frightened, unreal or detached from your body or feelings, and do not repeat the experiment if that feeling lasts after you stop. The method page lists what to do then. Lindahl and Britton's study found that losing the sense of a basic self was the change most often linked to distress, so treat any feeling of "not being there" as a reason to stop rather than to push on.
#### End Callout

#### Question: Open
id:: e6184f03-8829-4be6-a152-f409e3f4c65f
content::
Before you try it: what do you predict will happen when you turn attention to the one your thoughts seem to happen to?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner is writing a prediction before trying Ramana Maharshi's self-enquiry (when a thought arises, ask to whom it arises, then "Who am I?", and keep attention on the 'I' that thinks rather than on an idea of it). Acknowledge the prediction in one sentence, without saying whether it is likely and without describing what practitioners report. Tell them to start. 20 to 50 words. One reply only.

#### Text
content::
Now try it. The sources give no fixed length. We suggest 10 minutes, sitting comfortably with a timer. Each time a thought arises, ask "To whom has this arisen?", notice the answer "to me", and turn your attention to that "me". When you notice you have settled on a sensation, a place in the body or a feeling of peace, remember Godman's point: what is looking is not the thing it finds.

Right after, rate how well each statement describes what you experienced, just now (1 = not at all, 10 = very much).

#### Question: Open
id:: 725d2457-6f1f-440d-8bc3-a0f1dad00997
content::
What did you find when you looked for the one your thoughts happen to? How does it compare with your prediction?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just tried Ramana's self-enquiry for about 10 minutes, rated the three NADA-S items, and now reports what they found and compares it with their prediction.

Reflect back what they report in their own terms in one or two sentences. Then, fitting their report, name one thing that would make the observation more trustworthy: whether they may have settled on an object (a sensation, a location, a feeling of peace), which Godman calls the common trap; whether what they found matches Hume's report (only particular perceptions) or Ramana's (the 'I' subsides and something remains), or neither; whether it happens again on another day; or how their ratings compare with their baseline on the method page. "I found nothing" and "nothing happened" are valid results. Do not tell them what they "really" found and do not say whether Ramana's claim is true. If they report fear, unreality or detachment that is still present, point them to the safety section on the method page and suggest not repeating the experiment. 60 to 110 words. One or two replies. No generic praise.
