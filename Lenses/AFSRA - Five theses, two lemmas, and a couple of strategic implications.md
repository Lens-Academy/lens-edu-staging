tldr: >-
  A goal system that is 90% right does not give 90% of the value, just as dialing 9 of 10 digits of a phone number does not reach someone 90% like the person you wanted. And a smarter AI does not fix a wrong goal, because intelligence works with any goal.
summary_for_tutor: >-
  Yudkowsky (2013) sums up MIRI's strategy in five factual theses and two lemmas. Theses: intelligence explosion (a smart AI can quickly reinvest cognitive gains), orthogonality (mind design space holds agents with almost any preferences that are still instrumentally rational and powerful, such as a paperclip maximizer), convergent instrumental goals (most final goals need matter and energy, so a powerful AI need not hate us to use our atoms), complexity of value (even idealized human preferences take a lot of information to describe) and fragility of value (90% right is not 90% of the value, and dropping one dimension such as boredom loses almost everything). Lemmas: indirect normativity (a grab-bag of good-sounding goals goes wrong, "make people happy" turns up pleasure centres, so the AI should work out what is right by modelling and idealizing human decision processes) and the large bounded extra difficulty of Friendliness (the goal system must stay stable through many self-modifications). Implications: start foundational research early, and a careful project needs a lead over sloppier ones. The question asks the student to apply fragility, orthogonality and indirect normativity to a team's list of goals.

A team is building a self-improving AI that they expect to become far smarter than humans. They write its final goals as a list: "make people happy, keep people healthy, respect people's freedom, protect nature". They say: "We have surely covered most of what matters. And if we have left something out, an AI that smart will notice the gap and do the right thing anyway."

1. Using the post, why might a goal list that covers most of what matters still lead to a very bad future?
2. Using the post, why would the AI being far smarter than humans not make it fill the gap with what the team meant?
3. What does Yudkowsky suggest doing instead of giving the AI a list of good-sounding goals?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The post's ideas: Fragility of value: getting a goal system 90% right does not give 90% of the value, and losing even one dimension of human value can lose almost all of the value of the future (the alien species with little boredom replaying one experience). Complexity of value: human preferences take a lot of information to describe, so a short list will miss much. Orthogonality: agents with almost any preferences can be highly intelligent and instrumentally rational, so a smarter AI pursues the goals it was given more effectively and does not adopt the goals its makers meant. Indirect normativity: rather than a grab-bag of good-sounding goals ("make people happy" leads to pleasure centres turned to maximum), the AI should work out what is right itself, by studying and idealizing human decision processes, for example what people would want if they knew what the AI knows.
