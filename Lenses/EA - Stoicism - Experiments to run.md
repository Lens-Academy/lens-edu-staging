#### Text
content::
Each experiment below comes with the instructions the Modern Stoicism team wrote for Stoic Week, from their free 2014 handbook, and where possible the ancient text it is based on. The studies in the previous lens tested whole packages of these exercises, so there is little evidence for any single exercise on its own.

**How to measure.** Stoic Week asks participants to fill in the same questionnaires at the start and end of the week, among them the **Satisfaction with Life Scale** (5 statements) and the **Scale of Positive and Negative Experience** (12 feelings, each rated by how often you had it), both free from their author, Ed Diener ([SWLS](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~ediener/SWLS.html), [SPANE](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~ediener/Documents/Scale%20of%20Positive%20and%20Negative%20Experience.pdf)) ([Stoic Week 2025 report, appendices G and I](https://stoicismtoday.substack.com/p/stoic-week-2025-report-well-being)). Fill them in before you start and again when you finish, and keep the exercise's own record each day. The SPANE asks about the past four weeks, so a one-week change will be partly diluted.

The easiest way to run the whole package is to join Stoic Week itself, which is free. The 2026 edition starts on 26 October 2026 and includes the questionnaires ([Modern Stoicism courses](https://learn.modernstoicism.com/)).

#### Callout: 1. Live like a Stoic for a week
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**Claim tested:** a week of Stoic practice raises well-being. **Dose:** about 15 minutes in the morning, at midday and in the evening, for seven days, though the handbook says five or ten minutes is fine. **Measure:** the two questionnaires above, before and after.

#### Article
from:: Q: How do I know that living like a Stoic will benefit me?
to:: If you can only spare five or ten minutes, that’s fine.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 2. What is in your power?
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**Claim tested:** you are upset by your judgements about things you cannot control, and sorting what is up to you changes how you feel (Epictetus, *Handbook* 1 and 5, which you read in module 2). **Dose:** a few minutes on one upsetting situation, then the question "What aspects of this situation are up to me?" through the day. **Measure:** note how strong the feeling is (0 to 100) before and after you answer the questions.

#### Article
from:: Today’s Lunchtime Exercise: What is in our Power?
to:: implications for your attitude toward events.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 3. Morning preparation and evening review
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**Claim tested:** a daily cycle of planning and review makes you act more in line with your values and react less to events. **Dose:** 5 to 10 minutes each morning and evening for at least a week. **Measure:** in the evening, rate how far your actions matched your values (0 to 100), as the handbook's self-monitoring record suggests, and track the questionnaires.

#### Article
from:: When you wake up each morning
to:: “Beware of doing that again - and this time I pardon you.”

#### Text
content::
The evening review comes from Seneca, who describes doing it himself in *On Anger*, book 3, chapter 36.

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/seneca-of-angerbook-iii-wikisource-the-free-online-library]]
from:: The spirit ought to be brought up for examination daily.
to:: whether the person to whom you speak can bear to be told the truth."
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 4. Premeditation of adversity
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**Premeditation of adversity** (Latin *premeditatio malorum*) means imagining a misfortune in advance. **Claim tested:** picturing a feared event calmly and for long enough reduces the distress it causes. **Dose:** 20 to 30 minutes a day, starting with small things. **Measure:** the handbook's record: how strong the feeling is at the start and at the end (0 to 100%), and for how many minutes you stayed with it.

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/modernstoicism-the-stoic-week-2014-handbook]]
from:: Today’s Lunchtime Exercise: Preparation for Adversity
to:: down what you can learn from this experience.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 5. The view from above
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**Claim tested:** picturing your life from far above, within the whole of space and time, makes everyday troubles matter less to you. **Dose:** once a day, for example at midday, using the handbook's text or its audio recording. **Measure:** in the evening, note whether the day's irritations felt smaller, and rate the strongest one (0 to 100).

#### Article
from:: Today’s Lunchtime Exercise: The View from Above
to:: and the mere turn of a screw in terms of cosmic time.

#### Text
content::
Marcus Aurelius returns to this image several times in the *Meditations*. Here is book 9, section 30.

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/aurelius-the-meditations-book-nine]]
from:: Look down from above on the countless herds of men
to:: and that neither a posthumous name is of any value, nor reputation, nor anything else.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: 6. Voluntary discomfort
collapse:: closed
#### Text
content::
**Claim tested:** losing comforts is less bad than you fear, and practising it makes you less dependent on them. **Dose:** the handbook's small version is to give up something like coffee or snacks for the rest of the week. Seneca's version is three or four days at a time of the cheapest food and rough clothes. **Measure:** before you start, rate how bad you expect it to be (0 to 100). Afterwards, rate how bad it was.

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/modernstoicism-the-stoic-week-2014-handbook]]
from:: Today’s Lunchtime Exercise: Stoic Simplicity

#### Question: Open
id:: 911434fd-7899-4d76-a33b-887f6f691c16
content::
Which experiment will you run, and which Stoic claim will it test?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner has read six Stoic experiments, each with a claim it tests: (1) a whole Stoic Week raises well-being; (2) sorting what is and is not in your power changes how you feel; (3) morning preparation and evening review make you act more in line with your values and react less; (4) premeditation of adversity reduces distress about feared events; (5) the view from above makes everyday troubles matter less; (6) voluntary discomfort shows losing comforts is less bad than feared. They now pick one and name its claim.

Reply in 40 to 80 words. Check that the claim they name fits the experiment. If it is vague ("Stoicism works"), ask them to make it specific in one sentence. Do not push a choice. No generic praise. One turn, then send them to the next question. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: ask which exercise they would most likely still be doing on day five.

#### Question: Open
id:: d8987699-3daa-4e9a-95b8-c4f60c4ae084
content::
Write your plan: what you will do each day, for how many days, and what you will measure before, during and after.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner chose one of six Stoic experiments in the previous question and now writes a plan. Each experiment in the lens has a dose and a measure from the Stoic Week 2014 Handbook: whole Stoic Week, about 15 minutes three times a day for seven days (5 to 10 minutes is acceptable), with the Satisfaction with Life Scale and SPANE before and after; what is in your power, a few minutes per situation with 0 to 100 ratings; morning and evening meditation, 5 to 10 minutes each; premeditation of adversity, 20 to 30 minutes a day, rating the feeling 0 to 100% at the start and end, starting with small things; view from above, once a day; voluntary discomfort, giving up coffee or snacks for the week, or Seneca's three or four days of the cheapest food and rough clothes. In module 3 they learned to design a single-case test: baseline, dose, measurement, confounders.

A good plan has a daily dose, a duration, a baseline measured before starting, a measure during or after, and one step against obvious confounders (for example, not starting during an unusually good or bad week, or noting other changes). Reply in 80 to 130 words. Name the strongest part of the plan and the most important missing piece. If they picked premeditation of adversity with a major personal loss as the first topic, point to the handbook's advice to start small. No generic praise. One turn, then send them to the next question. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: ask what number they will write down on the first day.

#### Question: Open
id:: f32ee49e-dd84-458c-a139-e2c466590cc9
content::
What result would make you doubt the claim?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner chose a Stoic experiment, named the claim it tests and wrote a plan in the previous two questions. Now they say what result would count against the claim. A good answer names a concrete result on their own measure (for example, "my evening ratings of distress do not fall over seven days", or "giving up coffee was as bad as I expected"), and ideally notes what else could explain a positive result (expectation, a good week, the effect of any daily routine), so that a positive result is not overread.

Reply in 50 to 100 words. Say whether their result is concrete enough that they will know when it happens. If they only say what would confirm the claim, ask for the opposite in one sentence. No generic praise. One turn. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: ask what number on their measure would make them say the exercise did nothing.
