title: "Making Beliefs Pay Rent (in Anticipated Experiences)"
tldr: "Judge a belief by what it tells you to expect, and by what it says should not happen. If two people disagree but expect exactly the same experiences, they are arguing about words, not about the world."
summary_for_tutor: "Yudkowsky argues that beliefs should be judged by the experiences they lead you to anticipate, and better still by the experiences they forbid. Examples: the tree falling in the forest (both arguers expect the same recordings and brain scans, so they only disagree about the word sound), the bowling ball dropped from a building (abstract beliefs about gravity and height still yield a precise prediction of the clock tick), phlogiston (explanations fitted after the fact), and Wulky Wilkinsen the retropositional author (beliefs linked only to each other, which he calls floating beliefs). The question gives a new case, two coworkers arguing whether Pluto is really a planet while agreeing on every observation, and asks the student what to ask them and what the answers would show."

#### Question: Open
id:: 3914653d-4453-4d75-baa9-5e1eba8afb06
content::
Two coworkers have argued for an hour about whether Pluto is "really" a planet. They agree on every measured fact: its size, its orbit, and what a telescope shows when it is pointed at it.

Apply the essay's test to their argument.

1. What would you ask each of them?

2. What would their answers most likely show about what they disagree on?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Background for the grader: the essay says a belief should be judged by what experiences it leads you to expect, or rules out. If two people disagree but expect exactly the same experiences, they are not disagreeing about any fact of the world, only about a label, and they can argue forever without settling it. Score out of 100. 50: the answer asks each of them what they would expect to see or experience differently if their view were true (or what their view rules out), in any wording. 50: the answer says that, since they agree on every observation, they would expect no different experiences, so they are disagreeing about a word or a definition (what to call Pluto), not about a fact of the world. Cap at 40 if the answer treats it as a factual question that one of them simply has wrong. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "I'd ask each one: if you're right, what would you see that the other wouldn't? They'd both expect the same telescope images and the same orbit, so there's no difference in what they expect. They're arguing about what the word planet should cover, not about Pluto."
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Eliezer Yudkowsky's essay "Making Beliefs Pay Rent (in Anticipated Experiences)" and applied its test to two coworkers arguing whether Pluto is really a planet while agreeing on every observation. Key ideas from the essay: (1) ask what a belief leads you to expect, and better, what it forbids, (2) when two people expect the same experiences, their dispute is about labels, like the tree falling in the forest, (3) beliefs connected only to each other float free of experience, like "retropositional" and "alienated resublimation". Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Name the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important thing the answer missed or got wrong, and ask one direct question that helps the student fix it. At 100, confirm briefly and add one point from the essay they did not use. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence saying plainly whether they now have the idea. If the student says they do not understand, do not dismiss it and do not repeat the question: give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as the tree in the forest, where both arguers expect the same sound recording. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms instead of offering another foothold. Judge only against what the essay and the question supplied.
