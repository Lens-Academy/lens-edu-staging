#### Text
content::
The views so far deny that there is a lasting self, or say it is a construction. Two schools say the opposite: under the everyday "I" there is a true Self. They come from very different places.

The first is **Advaita Vedanta**, an Indian philosophy. In the words of the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, it holds that "the experiencing self (jīva) and the transcendental self of the Universe (ātman) are in reality identical (both are Brahman)", where **Brahman** is the ultimate reality ([Advaita Vedanta](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). Ramana Maharshi was a modern teacher in this tradition. His short text *Who Am I?* began as written answers to a visitor's questions in 1902. Read the opening and the first ten questions.

\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from Ramana and Schwartz, without looking back. No need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read the opening and questions 1 to 10 of Ramana Maharshi's Who Am I? and Richard Schwartz's account of IFS (parts and the Self), then wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- Ramana: everyone wants lasting happiness, which is one's nature and is experienced in deep sleep, where there is no mind. One is not the body, the senses, the vital airs, the mind or nescience. What remains after negating all these is Awareness, "that I am", whose nature is Existence-Consciousness-Bliss. The world is like a snake seen in a rope: the Self is not realised while the world is taken as real. The mind is only thoughts and projects the world. The "I"-thought is the first thought. The enquiry "Who am I?" destroys the other thoughts and in the end itself, like the stick used to stir a funeral pyre, and Self-realisation follows.
- Schwartz: the mind is "an ecology of relatively discrete minds". Managers protect and control, exiles carry old pain, firefighters douse it impulsively (drink, food, work). Everyone has at their core a Self with qualities like perspective, confidence, compassion and acceptance. He found it clinically: when clients asked parts to step back, a calm, caring state appeared that they called "who I really am". Later he saw it was known in spiritual traditions. Eight Cs: calmness, curiosity, clarity, compassion, confidence, creativity, courage, connectedness.

Be a brief, honest mirror: say what they got right without inflation, name what is missing, correct any error in one sentence, and treat gaps as normal. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. No follow-up questions and no generic praise. One reply, then tell them to move on.

\## Learning question
A friend says: "Ramana, Schwartz and the Buddha all teach the same thing. Drop the small ego and you find your true Self underneath."

Using this page and the not-self readings earlier in the module, where does this go wrong?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Ramana's Who Am I? (questions 1 to 10) and Schwartz on IFS, and earlier in the module SN 22.59, Thanissaro and Sujato on not-self. They got a plausible but flawed claim: "Ramana, Schwartz and the Buddha all teach the same thing. Drop the small ego and you find your true Self underneath." This is practice, not a test.

Points a strong answer finds (any two are good):
- The Buddhist texts read here never point to a true Self underneath. SN 22.59 says each aggregate is not self. Thanissaro sets the question aside, and Sujato and Bodhi say "all phenomena are nonself", which Bodhi says rules out even a transcendent self. Ramana says the opposite in words: what remains is Awareness, "that I am".
- Ramana's Self and Schwartz's Self are different kinds of thing. Ramana's Self is pure awareness without qualities of a person, found when the "I"-thought itself is destroyed, and the world is like an illusion. Schwartz's Self is a person's core with qualities (the eight Cs), it leads the parts in everyday life, and it was found by clients in therapy, not by ending thought.
- Their grounds differ: Ramana's realisation and a contemplative method; Schwartz's clinical observations; the Buddhist argument from change and lack of control.
- What they share is a practice of stepping back from one's thoughts and feelings, which may be why they sound alike. The similarity of practice does not make the claims about what remains the same.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in 2 to 3 sentences, name one gap, and ask one or two direct follow-ups (for example: "What does Ramana say remains, and does it have qualities like compassion?" or "Which Buddhist reading you met comes closest to a true Self, if any?"). If they are stuck after two attempts, give two points briefly. Do not say which view is right. 100 to 180 words per reply. After 3 replies ask whether they want to continue. No generic praise.
