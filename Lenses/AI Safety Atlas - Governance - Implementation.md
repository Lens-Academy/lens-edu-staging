Regulatory visibility, built on external scrutiny meeting the ASPIRE criteria of access, searching attitude, proportionality, independence, resources and expertise, plus model registries as early-warning systems, know-your-customer schemes applied to compute providers as chokepoints, and incident reporting, where the section notes aviation's confidential non-punitive reporting system as a precedent but says no equivalent exists for AI and that what counts as an AI incident is far less clear than a near-miss in aviation. Ensuring compliance, through licensing regimes modelled on pharmaceuticals or nuclear energy, requiring a structured safety case before deployment, with the EU enforcing horizontally through the AI Office, China vertically through the Cyberspace Administration, and the US fragmented across agencies with no national licensing authority. It closes on five limits: some risks resist technical solutions; measurement gaps mean compliance becomes interpretation rather than verification; expertise shortages may be the most serious constraint, with most talent concentrated in a few firms; coordination costs and speed mismatches, since capabilities advance in months and agreements take years; and regulatory arbitrage, since a model can be trained in one country, deployed from another and used everywhere.
