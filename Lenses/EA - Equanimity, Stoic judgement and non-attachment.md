#### Text
content::
The last page was psychology's own vocabulary. This page gives three older traditions in their own words. Each tells you what to do when a feeling arises. Read them for the instruction and for the claim about what it will do for you.

\## Equanimity

In module 2 you read Desbordes and colleagues' definition of **equanimity**: an even-minded state or disposition toward all experiences, pleasant, unpleasant or neutral. Shinzen Young, "an American mindfulness teacher and neuroscience research consultant" ([shinzen.org](https://www.shinzen.org/)), turns it into a working instruction. Read the first three pages of his synopsis [Natural Pain Relief](https://www.shinzen.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/art_synopsis-pain.pdf) (about 10 minutes), up to the section "Pain Without Suffering". It is about physical pain, but he writes that "the same basic concepts and skills work equally well when applied to emotional pain such as anger, grief, fear and guilt."

Here is what to look for. His central claim: "Suffering is a function of pain and the degree to which the pain is being resisted. (S = P x R)". **Resistance** is fighting the sensation: in the mind as judging thoughts ("I can't stand this pain"), in the body as tension and holding. His method is to observe the pain precisely while "dropping resistance", relaxing into each small change in it. He promises a lot: eventually "a decrease in negative emotions" and "a sense of happiness independent of your circumstances". And he adds a limit: "Dropping resistance to the subjective flow of pain in no way implies that you stop resisting the objective source of the pain."

#### Text
content::
Shinzen's claims come from his teaching and from what students report. We found no trial that tests them as stated. His site offers paid trainings, retreats and an app ([shinzen.org](https://www.shinzen.org/)). The last paragraph matters for later in this module: dropping resistance to a feeling is not the same as giving up on changing the situation that causes it.

\## Stoic judgement

The Stoics held that a feeling follows a **judgement**, a thought about what is happening and what it is worth, and that the judgement can be true or false, as the handbook below puts it. You read Epictetus, *Handbook* 5, in module 2. Modern Stoicism, which runs a free yearly online course called Stoic Week, explains the idea and turns it into a practice in its [Stoic Week 2016 handbook](https://modernstoicism.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Stoic-Week-2016-Handbook-Stoicism-Today.pdf). Read two passages there (about 8 minutes): section "2. Emotions" in the introduction (page 10 of the PDF), and the Wednesday section on Stoic mindfulness, from "He says that we should train ourselves to avoid errors in our judgements" to the three questions that end it (PDF pages 30 and 31).

The handbook's central claim, in its words: "our emotions are ultimately the product of the judgements that we make. It is because we think external events are what 'really matters' that we feel anger or fear." It denies that Stoics repress their feelings, and names three healthy emotions that follow from wise judgements: joy, caution and wishing. The practice: notice the early signs of an upsetting feeling, step back from the "impression" behind it, postpone acting on it, and then ask three questions. Is this within my power? What would a wise person do? What strengths do I have to meet it?

#### Text
content::
The evidence for this practice is thin. Stoic Week's own survey compares participants before and after, with no control group, and only 450 of 1,569 starters answered at the end ([Stoic Week 2023 report](https://modernstoicism.com/report-on-stoic-week-2023-by-tim-lebon/)). Also, the one randomised trial we found compared a two-week online Stoic course (56 people) with a waiting list (63 people). It found less avoidance of unpleasant inner experience and fewer depression symptoms. It is an unpublished doctoral thesis ([King 2025](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/modern-stoicism-and-mental-health-a-randomised-controlled-trial/)). The founders of cognitive therapy, Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, both named the Stoics, Epictetus among them, as its philosophical origin ([Robertson](https://donaldrobertson.name/2019/09/16/stoic-philosophy-as-a-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-2/)). In our view that makes CBT's evidence only indirect support for Stoic practice, because the two are close but not the same.

#### Text
content::
There is a short, seven-item version, the [Nonattachment Scale NAS-7](https://josephciarrochi.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/brief-measure-of-nonattachment.pdf) (one page, free). Open it now and rate each item from 1 to 6. You will use it as a measure in the experiments at the end of this module, so keep your scores.

#### Text
content::
In a survey of 7,884 Americans, people who scored higher on non-attachment were more satisfied with life ([Sahdra, Ciarrochi and Parker 2016](https://non-attachment.com/downloads/sahdra%20et%20al.%20nonattach%20and%20mindful.pdf)). The survey measured everything at one time, so it cannot show that non-attachment causes the satisfaction. The items are also easy to answer the way you wish you were.

#### Question: Open
id:: 2bc59c8f-0e63-46b0-9a1f-c55697522c7f
content::
Without looking back, write down what you remember: what each of the three traditions tells you to do with a feeling, and what each claims will happen if you do.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read Shinzen Young's synopsis "Natural Pain Relief", the Stoic Week 2016 handbook on emotions and Stoic mindfulness, and the description and items of the Nonattachment Scale, and wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- Shinzen Young (equanimity): observe the pain or feeling precisely and drop resistance, in mind (judging thoughts) and body (tension). Suffering = pain x resistance. He says the method works the same for emotional pain such as anger, grief, fear and guilt, and promises less suffering, fewer negative emotions and happiness independent of circumstances. Dropping resistance to the feeling does not mean giving up on changing its cause.
- Stoic judgement (Stoic Week 2016 handbook): Stoics do not repress emotions. Emotions are the product of judgements; treating external things like health and money as truly good or bad causes anger and fear. Wise judgements bring three good emotions: joy, caution, wishing. Practice: step back from the impression, spot early-warning signs, postpone, then ask: is it within my power, what would a wise person do, what strengths do I have.
- Non-attachment: not feeling an internal pressure to get, hold, avoid or change experiences. Seven items, for example enjoying pleasant experiences without needing them to last.
- Evidence: Shinzen is teaching and testimony. Stoic Week surveys have no control group, one small trial against a waiting list. Non-attachment is correlational.

Your role is a brief, honest mirror. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. Acknowledge what is right without inflation, name the most important missing points, correct errors in one sentence, normalise gaps. No re-teaching, no follow-up questions. One turn, then tell them to move on.
