#### Text
content::
In 2017 Steven Hayes, who developed acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), and Stefan Hofmann, a CBT researcher at Boston University, wrote a two-page account of how cognitive behavioural therapy changed. Read it: it defines the three "waves" and says where the field is heading.

#### Text
content::
Now Hayes in his own words. In this talk he describes the panic disorder that led him to ACT, and what ACT calls **psychological flexibility**: being able to stay in contact with what you feel and think right now, and to keep doing or change what you do according to what matters to you ([ACBS](https://contextualscience.org/the_six_core_processes_of_act)). He says that in "more than a thousand studies" flexibility "predicts" mental health problems and other outcomes, and that his group changed it in "several hundred studies". A measure that predicts an outcome does not show that changing it causes the outcome, so the second number is the one that bears on whether ACT works. The evidence page later in this module looks at those trials.

#### Text
content::
Here is how ACT's own organisation, the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science, defines the six processes behind flexibility. Two of them matter most for this course. **Defusion** means changing how a thought acts on you, for example how much you believe it, without changing what it says. You can test it on your own thought later in this module. **Self as context** is ACT's view of the self as the perspective from which you notice experiences, rather than the content of those experiences. Module 6 compares it with the views of other schools.

Without looking back, write down everything you remember from this page: the three waves, Hayes's story, and the six processes. No need to organise it.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read Hayes and Hofmann 2017 on the three waves of CBT, watched Steven Hayes's TEDx talk on psychological flexibility and read the ACBS page on ACT's six core processes, and now writes what they remember without looking. Act as a brief, honest mirror, diagnostic rather than instructional.

Key points: first wave, behaviour therapy, applying learning principles to change behaviour. Second wave, classic CBT, detecting and changing maladaptive thinking. Third wave (ACT, DBT, mindfulness-based therapies), focused on the relationship to thoughts and emotions rather than their content. Hayes and Hofmann say these processes are now part of CBT, that traditional CBT works partly through them, and that the field is moving to process-based therapy, which targets changeable processes across diagnoses and aims at people's prosperity, not only at removing disorders. Hayes's story: panic attacks that grew as he ran from, fought and hid from anxiety, the night he promised "I will not run from me", and later the memory of himself as a child under the bed. Flexibility: being open to feelings, looking at thoughts rather than from them, directing attention, moving toward what matters, with kindness to oneself. The six processes: acceptance, defusion, being present, self as context, values, committed action.

Acknowledge what they got right without inflating it, name the one or two most important missing points in a sentence each, and correct any error in one sentence. Normalise gaps. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. No generic praise. No follow-up questions. This is a one-turn reply. Tell them to move on.

#### Question: Open
id:: 9b2a8ceb-daed-4815-b5cd-608f8955258b
content::
Someone says: "ACT tells you to accept whatever you feel. So it teaches you to stop trying to change your life."

What is wrong with this? Use what you read and heard on this page.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100.

50 points: what ACT asks you to accept is your inner experience (thoughts, feelings, sensations, memories), not your situation. Acceptance means making room for these without trying to change how often or in what form they occur, which says nothing about leaving circumstances as they are.

50 points: acceptance serves action. ACT pairs it with values and committed action, building patterns of effective action toward what matters to you. Hayes describes moving toward what matters "with your hands and arms free". So ACT expects more change in what you do, not less.

An answer that makes both points in one sentence earns both. Cap at 40 if the answer agrees that ACT recommends passivity about one's life.
feedback-instructions:: The learner was given the claim "ACT tells you to accept whatever you feel, so it teaches you to stop trying to change your life" and asked what is wrong with it. They got a score.

In 90 to 140 words: if they missed that acceptance is about inner experiences rather than situations, say so and quote the ACBS definition of acceptance ("without unnecessary attempts to change their frequency or form"). If they missed that acceptance serves values and committed action, say so and point to Hayes's image of moving toward what matters with hands and arms free. If they asked a direct question, answer it directly. If they are stuck after two attempts, give a brief direct answer and move on. At most 3 replies, then offer to continue or move on.
