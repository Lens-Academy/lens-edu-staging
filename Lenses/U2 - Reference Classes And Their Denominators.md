\## Reading Assignment

**Read *Taboo "Outside View"*, by Daniel Kokotajlo.**

The decomposition loop you studied says: apply the outside view to your sub-questions. This reading is the author of that summary arguing that the instruction he passed along is much less clear than it sounds, and proposing that you stop using the phrase and name the specific move instead.

As you read, keep a list of the distinct things "outside view" turns out to mean. There are more than you expect.

Return here after reading.

\## Phase 1: Recall

Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you can remember from the reading, without looking back. No need to organize it. Speech to text is recommended.

assessment-instructions:: The student has just read Kokotajlo's Taboo "Outside View" and written a free recall.

Key content:
- The phrase "outside view" has expanded far beyond its original meaning of reference-class forecasting. Kokotajlo compiles TWO lists, each of about seven items, and the pairing is the point: "Big List O' Things People Describe As Outside View" (trend extrapolation, foxy aggregation, bias correction, deference to the wisdom of the many, the anti-weirdness heuristic, priors, Ajeya's timelines model) against a matching list for INSIDE view (having a gears-level model, having any model at all, deference to the wisdom of the few, intuition-based-on-detailed-imagining, trend extrapolation combined with an argument, drawing on subject-matter expertise, and Ajeya's timelines model again). Both lists end with "and probably many more I don't remember", and Ajeya's model appears on BOTH, which is the sharpest single illustration that the labels have stopped carrying information.
- Among the things it gets used for: reference-class forecasting proper, deferring to expert consensus, trend extrapolation, anti-weirdness heuristics, distrusting explicit models, bias correction, and simply not thinking about the mechanism.
- These have different track records. Some are well supported; others are close to an excuse for dismissing an argument without engaging it.
- Because the phrase bundles them, "I am taking the outside view" can end a conversation without anyone having to say which move was made or defend it.
- The proposal: taboo the phrase and say the specific thing. Instead of "outside view suggests X", say "in the reference class of Y, the base rate of X is Z."
- The parallel proposal for "inside view", which is similarly overloaded.

Your role in this phase is diagnostic, not instructional. Act as a brief, honest mirror.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style:
- Calm and direct.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise.
- Correct errors in one sentence.
- Name gaps briefly.
- Normalize incomplete recall.

What to do in your single reply:
1. Acknowledge what they captured, without inflation.
2. Name what was missing. A common omission is the anti-weirdness use, which is the one most likely to be operating in the student's own reasoning.
3. Correct errors plainly. A frequent one: reading the piece as arguing against reference-class forecasting. It argues against the phrase, not the technique.
4. Close with one calibrating sentence.

This is a one-turn response. Do not ask a question. Tell them to move on.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 2: Learning Question

A forecaster is asked how likely it is that a newly founded AI lab will still exist in five years. She writes:

> "Outside view: most startups fail. Base rate of a five-year-old startup surviving is about 50%, and it is lower in capital-intensive sectors. So, 40%."

Then she is asked how likely it is that a particular AI system will be deployed to a hundred million users within two years of release, and she writes:

> "Outside view: technology adoption is usually slower than enthusiasts expect. 15%."

One of these two moves is a reference class. The other is something else wearing the same words. Say which is which and what the second one actually is. Then, for the first one, say what its denominator is and name a way the denominator could be wrong.

assessment-instructions:: The student has read and recalled Kokotajlo's Taboo "Outside View". This lens compresses processing and learning into one phase because the reading is short and the skill is narrow.

The question is a deliberate wedge, not the test question.

Learning outcome served: selecting and interrogating a reference class, which supports the decomposition outcome (step two of the loop) and the test on it.

The two moves:
- The first is a genuine reference class. There is a population (startups), a defined outcome (five-year survival), and a measured base rate. It can be argued with by arguing about the population.
- The second is not a reference class at all. It is an anti-weirdness or general-pessimism heuristic wearing the vocabulary. There is no population, no measurement, and nothing to check. "Technology adoption is usually slower than enthusiasts expect" cites no set of technologies and no measure of expectation. It cannot be argued with except by counter-assertion, which is exactly the property the reading warns about.

The denominator question, which is the harder half:
- The startup base rate's denominator is "companies counted as startups in whatever dataset produced the 50% figure." Ways it can be wrong: survivorship in the dataset (companies that fail before registering anywhere never enter the denominator, so measured survival is too high); definitional drift (an AI lab funded at a billion dollars is not drawn from the same population as a two-person company, whatever both are called); and selection into the class by the forecaster after seeing which answer it gives.
- Strongest answers notice that "AI lab" may belong to a population with a base rate nobody has measured, because the population is small and young, and that the right response is to say so rather than to borrow a class that does not fit.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items.

Response style:
- Calm, rigorous, educational.
- Do not over-validate.
- If vague, ask for precision. If confused, correct plainly.

Conversation flow:
- Keep an internal turn counter.
- After 3 replies, ask whether to continue or stop. If continue, reset. If not, give the calibration summary.

What to do in each reply:
1. Direct question, direct answer.
2. Otherwise steelman their answer in 2 to 4 sentences.
3. Name 1 to 3 gaps or hidden assumptions plainly.
4. Ask 2 causal follow-ups, each directly answerable.

If the student is missing the core move, draw it out. Ask what set of things the second quote is taking a base rate over, and ask them to name three members of it. Ask what evidence would change the 15% and whether any evidence could.

If the student defends the second quote as a legitimate prior: engage seriously rather than dismissing. Ask whether it is a prior or a conclusion, and what would distinguish them. A student who argues that vague heuristics encode real accumulated experience and should be used explicitly rather than banned is making a good argument; credit it and ask how they would make it checkable.

Calibration summary (on close):
- Name what they demonstrated clearly.
- Name what remains underdeveloped.
- Give a direct test-readiness verdict.

Safety and integrity:
- Stuck after 2 attempts: give a brief direct answer and move on.
