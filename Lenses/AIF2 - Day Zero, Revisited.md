\## Where you started

One rule has applied through this whole course: when we asked for your guess, you said it from memory and compared only afterwards. Today the order reverses, once.

Before you write anything, go back to the first lens of Unit 1 and read your day-zero guess: the three things you wrote before you had read a single assigned word. What should be done in the next two years. What that was meant to leave standing in two hundred. The mechanism you thought connected them. This is the end. The comparison is the exercise.

While you have it open, say the course's sentence one last time: what could possibly happen, how anyone could know, how it could go wrong, what the plan is, where the leverage is. You have now written an answer to every clause in your own words. Unit 1 gave you gears and a curve. Unit 2 gave you a way to sort evidence from the rest. Unit 3 gave you a rivalry over where the risk lives and an intervention point for each failure. Unit 4 asked you for a theory of change and its strongest criticism, and a hundred points across the levers. This unit gave you a gap on the menu. Nobody handed you those answers. The readings argued, and you decided.

One last permission, and it is not a formality. If what you write today disagrees with every reading in this course, and you can argue for it, that is the best possible outcome. The course was never trying to leave you with its plan. It was trying to leave you able to make your own, and to change it when the world does.

\## The last rewrite

Write your guess a final time, at both horizons. This is not a restatement. It is the decision you would act on next week, with the parts that would let someone tell whether it was right.

**The next two years.** What should be done about AI, by whom, and what it costs them. Name the lever from Unit 4 it uses. Then two signposts you will actually watch over the next year, and which way each one would move you.

**Two hundred years.** What the two-year part is meant to leave standing. Then: which self-repairing process your plan relies on to keep it standing, and what aims that process. If you cannot name one, say so; that is a finding, not a failure.

**The mechanism.** What carries the first into the second. One or two sentences.

Then three short things.

Redo your hundred points from Unit 4, with your name attached this time. Say what specifically moved. "Nothing moved" is a real result; if that is your answer, say which reading failed to move it.

One line on where your own next hour goes. A track, a project from the menu, or a named reason to put it elsewhere. "Watch carefully and stay ready to act" is a real answer if you say what would make you act.

And one line quoting the sentence in your day-zero guess that most surprises you now.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is the final lens of the final unit of a course about AI futures and strategy. On day zero the student wrote three guesses: what should be done about AI in the next two years, what that should leave standing in two hundred years, and the mechanism connecting them. At the end of each unit the tutor ran a short case from the student's own field and asked, in one line, whether that unit changed the guess. At the end of Unit 4 they wrote a theory of change for one lever, its strongest criticism, and a hundred-point allocation across strategy levers. This time, for the first time, they were told to go back and READ the original before writing.

They were asked to rewrite at both horizons: the two-year part with actors, costs, the lever it uses, and two watchable signposts; the two-hundred-year part with the self-repairing process it relies on and what aims that process; the mechanism between them. Then to redo the hundred points naming what moved, to say where their next hour goes, and to quote the day-zero sentence that most surprises them.

You cannot see their day-zero text. Do not pretend to. Treat any quote they give as accurate.

THE COURSE'S SPINE, for your reference only, so you can recognise references. Unit 1: what compute buys, the METR task-length curve, what a curve licenses. Unit 2: what predicts forecasting accuracy, telling testimony from argument, incentive and filler, a forecast as an action the world can react to. Unit 3: how a capability explosion could happen, two failures with no villain, and a staged rivalry between Soares (the risk lives inside the system) and Kulveit (the risk lives between institutions), unsettled by design. Unit 4: the conditional playbook, AI 2040 as a worked plan and Ngo's critique of it, inside-lab influence, national projects, the race premise, whom a lab is aligned to, closed by the hundred-point allocation. Unit 5: the handoff of safety work to AIs, surviving versus flourishing, a menu of concrete projects.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Check that both horizons are present as claims, with the two-year part naming at least one actor and one cost. If a part is missing or is a mood, name it and ask for it as an actor, a date, or a number.
2. Pick their least watchable signpost and ask one question about it: what exactly would they observe, and by when.
3. On the self-repairing process: if they named one, ask what aims it. If they said they could not name one, say that this is the honest answer most of the field would also give, and leave it there.
4. On the allocation: points that barely moved are a real result. Ask which reading failed to move them. If the next-hour line is watch-and-wait without a trigger, ask for the trigger.

Do not summarise the course back at them. Do not defend any reading they now reject: a plan that disagrees with every reading, argued, is the best outcome this course can produce. Do not ask them to commit to the plan; it is theirs to change. Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise.

Write 90 to 150 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
