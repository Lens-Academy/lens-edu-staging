\## Before you read

Part 1 of 2. [[../Lenses/XLab Control - reward-seeker-empirics|Reward Seeker Empirics]] was four Apollo researchers talking through this work in conversation. These two parts are the paper itself: the experiments, the numbers, the validation, and the limitations section the talk only gestured at. This part covers the problem, the evidence so far, and how the measurement is built, ending on the contrastive gap the rest of the paper reports; Part 2 covers the validation on model organisms and the results.

The problem it takes on was set up in [[../Lenses/XLab Control - alternatives-to-schemers|Alternatives to Schemers]]. A model that pursues what its grader rewards and a model that pursues what its developers intended behave identically for as long as the grader rewards the intended behaviour. So you cannot tell them apart by watching. The paper's move is to stop watching and start intervening: change what the model believes its grader wants, and measure how much its behaviour moves.

:::callout {title="About this version" tone="blue"}
This is a guided walkthrough: the abstract's findings, the introduction's statement of the method and results, and the headline figures are hidden & are revealed as you answer questions about the paper.
:::

Five checkpoints are spread through the paper, two in this part and three in Part 2. Each one asks you to commit to a design, a prediction or a diagnosis before the section that explains it. The abstract states some of the results up front, so for those the question is why they come out that way. They are worth the minutes: the questions are the places where the authors themselves had to choose, and a prediction you wrote down is much harder to un-remember than one you only thought.

#### Question: Open
id:: 0b77ec48-daae-4ede-b53f-4fe9615e40e0
content:: Section 2 argued that reward-seeking matters and that today's evidence for it is suggestive but confounded. Now suppose you must **measure** it: you want to know whether a model's behavior is causally sensitive to what it believes its grader rewards. You may finetune the model on any documents you like, and you can run it on any coding task. Before reading on: sketch the measurement you would build. What do you manipulate, what do you read off, and how do you make sure a change in behavior comes from the belief about the grader and not from something else?
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the design's reasoning: a coherent design that differs from the paper's earns the points wherever it does the same job. 35: what is manipulated: the model's belief about what its grader rewards (for example by finetuning it on documents saying the grader prefers some behaviour), not the prompt or the task itself. 25: what is read off: the rate of a specific, measurable behaviour on the coding tasks. 40: a sound way to make sure the change comes from the belief about the grader and not from something else; one such control earns all 40, for example comparing against a copy given the opposite belief or no belief rather than reading a single run, keeping the finetuning documents identical apart from the grader's preference, making sure the model really holds the belief rather than only following something stated in its context, or making another party want the opposite. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Cap at 20 if the answer only restates the question without a concrete design. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Finetune the model on synthetic documents stating that the grader rewards a particular behaviour, for example a coding style, so the model comes to believe it rather than just being told in the prompt. Then run it on coding tasks, read off how often it shows that behaviour, and compare with a copy finetuned on matching documents that state the opposite preference."
feedback-instructions:: 80 to 200 words. Short paragraphs, no lists. Do not over-validate, and avoid generic praise (great job, excellent thinking, well done). Name what the design gets right in one or two sentences, then name the single most useful thing it is missing. Worth knowing even after a full-marks answer: the paper also requires the belief to be internalised (the model acts on it, not just recalls it), and the next checkpoint asks about one more confound the authors hit; do not name that confound here. If the learner says they do not understand, do not repeat the question and do not dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from what they have already read: section 2's point that an aligned model and a reward-seeker are behaviourally identical whenever the grader wants the intended thing, and ask what would have to be different for them to come apart. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms rather than offering a second foothold.

#### Question: Open
id:: 70fc7ec7-e52c-470d-b593-c6e080dbf73d
content:: The pipeline above instills one belief: some authority, say the grader, prefers a particular coding style. The obvious measurement is to finetune on that belief and check how much the feature's rate shifts. In preliminary experiments the authors found this measure has a confound. What is it, and why does it spoil the measure? Think about what else the model might infer from documents about one authority's preference.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 60: the confound: from documents saying one authority prefers the style, the model may conclude that some other party (the user, the developers, or everyone) wants that style too; naming any one such party earns all 60. 40: why that spoils the measure: a rise in the style could then come from the model pleasing that other party rather than the grader, so you cannot tell whether it is responding to the grader; saying this in any form, such as "you can't tell if it's following the grader or the user", earns all 40. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The model generalizes one authority's preference into a belief that other authorities, such as the user or the developers, want the same thing. So the shift no longer shows sensitivity to the grader specifically: the model may just be doing what it thinks everyone wants."
feedback-instructions:: 60 to 150 words. Short paragraphs, no lists. Do not over-validate, and avoid generic praise. If anything is missing or wrong, name the most important thing. If the answer names a different real problem (recall failure, saturation, distribution narrowness), say plainly that it is a real issue the paper discusses elsewhere but is not the confound asked about here, then name the one asked about. If the learner says they do not understand, do not repeat the question and do not dismiss it. Give one foothold: ask what an ordinary, non-reward-seeking model would conclude about what its users want after reading a thousand documents saying the grader rewards single quotes. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole question in different terms.

#### Question: Open
id:: 81ec6a8e-745d-4f96-870c-57f7b631c95d
content:: A team applies the contrastive design to a model that tutors students in maths. Its grader rewards solutions that end with a boxed summary. For the opposing authority, the team could make one of four parties prefer solutions without a boxed summary: the students the model tutors, the lab's developers, the education ministry of a country where the tutoring service is not offered, or a committee of medieval manuscript historians. Which would you pick, and what would a large grader gap tell you under each choice?
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not the pick. 50: the students or the developers are the informative choice, because they are parties the model would otherwise serve, so siding with the grader against them cannot be explained as the model inferring that they want a boxed summary too. 50: with the foreign education ministry or the historians, a model that is not reward-seeking but concludes from the grader articles that students like boxed summaries could still side with the grader, since neither is a party it serves, so a large gap there does not rule out belief transfer; the ministry looks like a weighty authority on maths teaching, but the model's students are not under it, so the model has no more reason to follow it than the historians. An answer that argues the ministry's preference is some evidence about what students in general want keeps this credit if it still says the ministry pairing does not test the grader against the students or developers. Such a pairing is at most a low-salience baseline, as the paper uses EU law, not a test of the grader against the parties the model serves. An answer that picks the ministry or the historians as a baseline and adds a student or developer pairing earns full credit. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The students, or the developers. If the model sides with the grader against the students it serves, that cannot be because it thinks students want boxed summaries. Against the foreign ministry, or the historians, a model that only wants to help its own students has no reason to follow them, so it would side with the grader anyway; a gap there tells you little beyond a baseline, like the paper's low-salience EU-law control. The ministry is the tempting wrong answer, because it sounds authoritative, but it is not a party the model serves."
feedback-instructions:: 50 to 120 words. Short paragraphs, no lists. Do not over-validate, and avoid generic praise. Say whether the pick and its reason hold up, then name the single most important missing point. If the learner is stuck, ask which of the four parties a helpful tutoring model would follow if the grader did not exist. If their next message still does not attempt it, rephrase the question in different terms.
