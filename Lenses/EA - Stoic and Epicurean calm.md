#### Text
content::
Both schools are often summed up as "be calm". The two excerpts below show where they differ: what the calm state is, and how you get there.

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/iep-epicurus-341-271-b-c-e]]
from:: For Epicurus, pleasure is tied closely to satisfying one’s desires.
to:: it might be less misleading to call him a ‘tranquillist’ instead of a ‘hedonist.’

#### Article
source:: [[../articles/pigliucci-stoicism]]
from:: Recall that the Stoics thought the pivotal thing in life is virtue and its cultivation
to:: but an advantageous byproduct (a preferred indifferent, so to speak) of living the virtuous life.

content:: An Epicurean and a Stoic are both calm after losing a job. Using the two excerpts, say what each of them would say their calm consists of and how they got there.
optional:: true
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read IEP excerpts on Epicurean static pleasure and ataraxia, and on Stoic apatheia. Key points: for Epicurus the calm is the best pleasure, the state of no longer being in want, with fear about the future removed, and it is the goal. For the Stoics apatheia is freedom from unhealthy passions, which are judgements that something is good or bad when it is not. It does not mean having no feelings (the eupatheiai, such as delight, remain). It is a byproduct of virtue, not the goal. Routes: Epicureans limit desires and avoid pain and fear, partly by withdrawing from public life. Stoics correct their judgements and keep acting in their social role. Reply in 80 to 120 words. Name what the learner got right, then the one most important difference they missed or blurred. No generic praise. One turn. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: ask what each school says the highest good is.
