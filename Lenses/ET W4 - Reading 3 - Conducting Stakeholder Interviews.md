\## 4. Conducting Stakeholder Interviews

When your outreach is successful, the next step will be actually having a conversation with these key stakeholders! Stakeholder interviews are where your research moves from theory into the real world. By speaking directly with experts, practitioners, and potential beneficiaries, you gain insights and opportunities for collaboration you can’t get from papers or reports alone.

\### Key Reminders for Effective Interviews

Stakeholder interviews are about curiosity, learning, and building relationships. Experts are human - not infallible authorities - so approach every conversation with openness, respect, and confidence that your perspective matters. These conversations are opportunities to test assumptions, refine ideas, and expand your network of stakeholders who are working on a problem you care about.

- **Use stakeholder interviews to address your key uncertainties:** Use interviews to test the assumptions that matter most for your thesis. For example, if you’re unsure whether your research will meet the needs of a key stakeholder group, interviews help you check what they actually want and need.
- **Ask the right questions to the right stakeholders!** Different stakeholders have different expertise. Make sure the person you’re speaking to can meaningfully answer the questions you’re asking. For instance, beneficiaries might not know the details of the methodology, and academics might not fully understand real-world implementation challenges.
- **Ask your ideal direct users of your research what they want/need (the people next in your ToC).** Seek input from the people who will actually use or benefit from your research. For example, Faunalytics interviews animal advocates to figure out what evidence would be most useful and then designs research to fill those gaps.
- **Follow your curiosity:** Let your natural curiosity guide the conversation. You don’t need to know everything or say the “right” thing - honest questions often spark the most useful insights.
- **Look for potential mentors or collaborators:** Some experts may be interested in supporting your research or career. Building a good impression and professional relationship could open opportunities for co-authorship, data access, or guidance on your thesis.
- **Prepare thoughtfully:** Doing your homework shows respect for their time and helps you ask better questions. Read relevant papers, reports, or summaries they’ve produced. Have your questions ready and know which are most important to prioritise.
- **Ask comparative or context-focused questions:** Experts may not be able to provide exact probabilities or precise numbers, but they can offer valuable insight when asked to compare approaches, interventions, or strategies. For example, “Does intervention X seem more effective than Y in this context?” is more actionable than asking for exact figures.
- **Pause and give them space:** Allow time for reflection after each response. Silence often encourages experts to expand on points they might otherwise omit.

#### Callout: Reminder: Limitations of Expert Input

Experts can provide valuable guidance, but they **might not have the exact expertise to evaluate your specific work**. Their advice can be influenced by biases, personal experience, or the limits of their perspective. Just because an expert says “don’t do this” doesn’t mean it’s gospel - treat their input as guidance, not a final verdict.

💡 **Takeaway:** Use expert insights alongside other evidence, compare perspectives from multiple sources, and critically evaluate how their advice applies to your research.

\### How to Structure the Interview

Stakeholder interviews are an opportunity to gather insights that can help you refine your thesis question, validate assumptions in your Theory of Change, and identify opportunities for collaboration or impact. The goal is to have structured conversations that balance understanding the problem landscape, exploring potential solutions, and seeking guidance on your specific research ideas or career path.

**When preparing for these conversations, you can focus on:**

- **Problem Understanding:** Learn about the key challenges, bottlenecks, and knowledge gaps in the field.
- **Solution & Action Landscape:** Understand who is working on the problem, which approaches are effective, and where opportunities for intervention exist.
- **Thesis-Relevant Insights:** Gather feedback on your research questions, identify missing evidence, and explore opportunities for co-development or collaboration.
- **Career & Mentorship:** If relevant, use the conversation to understand the field, career pathways, and skills you should prioritize.

\### Sample Interview Questions

You can use the sample interview questions below as a guide. They are organized to cover background, problem landscape, solutions, thesis-specific insights, and career advice, ensuring that each interview is both focused and actionable.

\#### Background

**Question List:**

- Could you tell me about your journey in this field?
- What led you to focus on this topic or problem area?

\#### Problem Landscape Questions

**Question List:**

- From your perspective, what are the most pressing unanswered questions or knowledge gaps in this area - where could more research make a meaningful difference?
- What do you see as the main bottlenecks preventing progress in this field? Consider factors such as funding, technical capacity, political will, infrastructure, and human behavior.

\#### Solution Landscape

**Question List:**

- Which approaches or pathways seem most promising for addressing this problem?
- Who else is actively working in this space? What types of actors are involved? (i.e., refer to categories of stakeholders in readings)
- How much resource or capacity is currently dedicated to this problem?

\#### Thesis-Relevant Insights & Collaboration Opportunity

**Question List:**

- Based on your experience, are there gaps or assumptions in the current research that more research could address?

**⭐ Collaboration Angle:** If you’re speaking with someone you hope to collaborate with, you can frame your questions around how your research could directly support their work:

- I’m hoping to orient my research so that it could directly support work like yours. Are there any research questions or projects you wish you had the capacity to explore but currently don’t? Would it make sense for my work to tackle any of those gaps or questions so that it could complement or support your efforts?
- Are there areas where my thesis or research could directly contribute to your work or ongoing initiatives?

\#### Feedback on Specific Thesis Ideas *(if applicable)*

**Question List:**

Share your thesis idea or research question.

- What are your thoughts on its impact, relevance or feasibility?
- Do you see any limitations, risks, or ways to improve it? Are there additional perspectives, evidence, or contexts I should consider to strengthen this idea?

\#### Career & Mentorship Questions (optional, recommended)

**Question List:**

*If you’re speaking with someone whose career path you admire, use this as an opportunity to explore career pathways, build connections, and learn from their experience.*

- How did you get started in this field, and what were the key decisions or experiences that shaped your career?
- At this stage, what specific steps or activities would you recommend for a student to build the skills, knowledge, and network needed to pursue a career in this field?
- What skills, experiences, or knowledge do you think are most valuable for someone entering this area?
- Are there common mistakes or pitfalls that early-career professionals tend to make in this field?
- Are there mentors, professional networks, or resources you’d recommend for someone pursuing this path?

\#### Closing Questions

**Question List:**

- Are there any insights or considerations we haven’t covered that you think are important?
- Who else would you recommend I speak with about this topic, and could you introduce me?
- Do you have any questions for me?

\### What to do after the interview

After a stakeholder interview, the real value comes from turning the conversation into insights and action. Here’s a practical approach to what to do after your interview:

- **Send a follow-up email:**
   - Thank them for their time and insights, and mention any key points from your conversation that were particularly helpful.
   - If relevant, you may politely ask if they’re open to future collaboration or if they could connect you to others who might offer additional perspectives.
- **Synthesize the interview for your own notes:**
   - Summarize key insights, takeaways, and new questions that arose.
   - Note any assumptions that were confirmed, challenged, or need further investigation.
   - Highlight potential collaborators, resources, or data sources mentioned.
- **Plan your next steps:**
   - Decide whether you need to interview more stakeholders in this area or shift focus to a different type of stakeholder.
   - Reflect on whether your thesis question or approach needs adjusting based on what you learned.
   - Identify opportunities for collaboration, data access, or mentorship that could strengthen your research and make it more impactful.
