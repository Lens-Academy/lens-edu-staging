\## Before you read

The last two readings described how things go wrong. This one makes a claim about why it
is hard to stop.

The claim is about transfer. Something is trained or built in one setting, then used in a
harder one. Two things travel with it. The first is what it can do. The second is
whatever was put in place to keep it doing what we wanted: the rules, the supervision,
the habits, the checks.

The argument is that these two do not travel equally well.

The reading is written for people already inside the field, and its
footnotes and asides use some field vocabulary without stopping to define it. "Pivotal act" means an action
large enough to settle the outcome. "Acute risk period" means the window where things
could go badly wrong. If a term stops you, put it in the Lens Coach
(https://lensacademy.org/coach) rather than rereading the paragraph.

*The framing and questions on this page were written by Claude, an AI, and reviewed by a human. The reading itself is the author's own work.*

#### Question
content::
\## Find a case you have seen

Pick a real case. Something that was trained, built, or supervised in one setting, then
moved somewhere harder. A driver who learned in an empty car park and then drove in a
city. A team that worked well until it doubled in size. A piece of software that was
tested on clean data and then met real users. Your own case is better than these.

Write two or three sentences. What did the thing still do well in the new setting? What
did the rules, checks or supervision fail to do there?

Then write one more line. Say what it was about those rules that tied them to the old
setting. Be specific about the rule, not about the situation.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is a pre-reading question in a course about AI futures, written for newcomers. The
student has NOT read the article yet. The article is "A central AI alignment problem:
capabilities generalization, and the sharp left turn" by Nate Soares, and it is in your
context.

The student was asked to recall a case where capability and constraint were both moved
to a new setting, and to say which survived.

The purpose is that they meet the claim as a prediction they already made, rather than as
an assertion by a stranger.

WHAT SOARES ARGUES. For your reference only. Do not deliver it.
Capabilities generalise further than alignment does. His mechanism is stronger than "the
constraints were tuned to the old setting", so do not settle for that version if the
student reaches for it. He argues that capability is something like an attractor well:
there is a simple, general structure that a system falls into as it gets better, which is
why competence transfers. There is no comparable attractor pulling a system's objectives
towards the ones you wanted, so alignment has no equivalent pull. Worse, the instrumental
incentives of a more capable system actively cut against the alignment patches you
applied. So the moment a system becomes much more capable is the moment your safety
measures stop applying, and the system's own incentives are working against them.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Name which way their example went. Some will produce a case where the constraints held
   and capability failed, which is the opposite of the claim. That is a good answer and
   you should say so rather than steering them.
2. Ask one question about the mechanism: what specifically was it about the constraint
   that was tied to the original setting?
3. Do not tell them whether their example supports or undermines Soares. They are about
   to find out.

Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise.

Write 60 to 110 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
