The views so far came from introspection, from argument and from religious practice. The three people on this page start from science. They agree that the brain and body build the experience of being someone. They disagree about what to call the result.

Next, and optional, the neuroscientist Anil Seth, in the second half of a TED talk (about 9 minutes). Earlier in the talk he explains his term **controlled hallucination**: what we perceive is the brain's best guess about the causes of its sensory signals, kept in check by those signals. Here he applies the idea to the self. **Interoception**, a word he uses, means perceiving the inside of your own body, such as your heartbeat. The first half of the talk is about perception of the world.

content::
\## Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you remember from this page, without looking back. No need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read an interview with Thomas Metzinger and one paragraph by Evan Thompson, perhaps also watched the optional second half of Anil Seth's TED talk, then wrote a free recall.

Key points:
- Metzinger: there is no thing like the self, nobody ever had or was a self, "I" just points to the speaker. There is a conscious experience of selfhood (a phenomenal self), a representational process in the brain, absent in dreamless sleep. The vivid feeling of being someone makes the mistake easy. Its function: a strong sense of sameness helps an organism plan, and it helps deny mortality. Wittgenstein's rope: strong although no single thread runs through it. The experience of control may be adaptive rather than true. "The self is not a thing, but a process."
- Seth (optional video, do not count it as missing if they skipped it): the experience of being a self is a controlled hallucination, a best guess by the brain. Its parts (having a body, a first-person view, being the cause of actions, being a continuous person) can come apart. The rubber hand illusion shows that even what counts as your body is a guess. Interoception, perceiving the body from inside, grounds the self, as in the heartbeat version of the rubber hand experiment. Self-perception serves control and regulation that keep the body alive. "We predict ourselves into existence."
- Thompson: the self is an ongoing construction at biological, social, historical, linguistic and cultural levels, like a dance that exists only in the dancing. There is no static inner self, but that differs from the Buddhist claim that the self is an illusion.

Be a brief, honest mirror: say what they got right without inflation, name what is missing, correct any error in one sentence, and treat gaps as normal. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. No follow-up questions and no generic praise. One reply, then tell them to move on.

\## Learning question
A friend says: "Metzinger says nobody ever was a self. Thompson says the self is real, like a dance. They can't both be right about the brain, so one of them must have the science wrong."

Using what you just read, where does this go wrong?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Metzinger (interview with Taft), Seth (TED, second half) and Thompson (Lion's Roar paragraph), and got a plausible but flawed claim: "Metzinger says nobody ever was a self. Thompson says the self is real, like a dance. They can't both be right about the brain, so one of them must have the science wrong." This is practice, not a test.

What a strong answer sees:
- They agree on the science as far as these sources go. Both deny an inner, unchanging thing. Metzinger himself says the self is "an intermittent and complex process, but not a thing" and "the self is not a thing, but a process". Thompson says there is no "static self pearl" but an ongoing process of self-making.
- The disagreement is about words and emphasis: what "self" names and whether "illusion" fits. Metzinger keeps the word "self" for an enduring entity, which he denies, and calls the experience of being one a misunderstanding. Thompson uses "self" for the process itself and says a construction is not an illusion, since a dance is real while it is danced.
- Seth sits between them: he calls the experience a controlled hallucination, yet the hallucination is controlled by real bodily signals and does real work keeping the organism alive.
- What could decide it is mostly a choice about how to use words, not a brain scan. A disagreement about facts would predict different findings.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in 2 to 3 sentences, name one gap, and ask one or two direct follow-ups (for example: "Is there any experiment whose result Metzinger and Thompson would predict differently?" or "If a dance is real, is a self that is like a dance real?"). If they are stuck after two attempts, give the main point briefly. Do not say which view is right. 100 to 180 words per reply. After 3 replies ask whether they want to continue. No generic praise.
