title: "How An Algorithm Feels From Inside"
tldr: Even when you know every fact about an object, asking whether it is "really" a blegg, or Pluto "really" a planet, can still feel open. The feeling comes from how your mind builds categories, not from anything left to learn.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky asks why people argue over whether a tree falling in an empty forest makes a sound. Using bleggs and rubes, he shows that "Is it a blegg?" stands in for a disguised query (which bin does it go in, will it glow in the dark), and once every feature of an object is observed there is nothing left for the question to stand for. He contrasts two neural networks. In Network 1 every unit is an observable feature. Network 2, more like a human brain, has a central category unit that can still vary after every surrounding feature is known. From the inside, that unsettled central unit feels like a leftover question about the world ("but is it really a blegg?", "is Pluto a planet?"). We do not see our intuitions as intuitions, we see them as the world. The question asks the learner to apply this to two friends who agree on every feature of a hot dog but argue over whether it is really a sandwich.

#### Question: Open
id:: 34c3f7d3-4d47-4372-a22b-8ca9fee6b115
content::
Two friends agree on every fact about a hot dog: it is a sausage in a long bun that is split but still joined along one side, it is eaten by hand, and most menus list hot dogs separately from sandwiches. Still, they argue for an hour over whether a hot dog is *really* a sandwich.

Using the essay's model of how categories work in the mind, explain why the question still feels open to them, and why there is nothing left for them to find out.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

Background the grader needs: the essay contrasts two ways a mind could handle a category. In one, every part is an observable feature. In the other, which the essay says is more like the human brain, the features all connect to one central unit for the category, and that unit can stay undecided even after every feature is known. From the inside, the undecided central unit feels like an unanswered question about the object itself ("but is it really a blegg?"), because we experience our intuitions as the world rather than as our own mental process. Once every feature is known, any practical question the category word stood for (which bin, will it glow) already has an answer.

60: Why it still feels open. The answer says that the friends' minds sum up the features under one category ("sandwich"), and this category can stay unsettled even though every feature is known. They experience that unsettled category as a question about the hot dog itself.

40: Why there is nothing left to find out. The answer says that they already know every feature, so any real question "is it a sandwich?" might stand for (which part of the menu, how to eat it) is already answered, and the leftover question is about the category in their heads, not about the hot dog.

Cap at 30 if the answer tries to settle the argument by deciding that a hot dog is, or is not, really a sandwich.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Our minds don't just store the features. They link them all to one 'sandwich' category, and that category can stay undecided even when every feature is known. From the inside, that feels like a real question about the hot dog. But they already know everything about it, so any practical question behind 'is it a sandwich' already has its answer. What's left is only how their category is wired, not a fact about hot dogs."
feedback-instructions::
The student has just read Yudkowsky's "How An Algorithm Feels From Inside" and applied it to an argument over whether a hot dog is really a sandwich. The essay's key ideas: a category word stands in for disguised queries, and once every observable is known there is nothing left for it to stand for. Network 2, the brain-like design, has a central category unit that can stay unsettled after all features are clamped. That unsettled unit feels from the inside like a leftover question about the world, because we see our intuitions as the world. Even "it depends on how you define it" is still about how the central unit is wired.

Reply in 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists. Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Start with the strongest specific part of the answer. If the score is below 100, name the most important gap, often explaining why there is nothing left to find out but not why it still feels like a question. Ask one question that lets them fix it, for example what in their minds is still undecided once every feature is known. At 100, confirm briefly.

If the student says they do not understand, do not repeat the question or dismiss it. Give one concrete foothold from the essay, such as the blue egg that contains palladium and still glows in the dark. If their next message still does not attempt the question, rephrase the whole task in different terms. Judge only against what the essay and the question supply. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close with one sentence on what they can now do and tell them to move on.
