tldr: "Control is only worth what we do before models outgrow it. Greenblatt and Shlegeris argue that a schemer without wildly superhuman skills in dangerous domains would probably find careful control hard to evade, that the controllable window may last only 6 to 12 months at full speed but could stretch to years, and that labs, the public and researchers should all treat control as a priority."
summary_for_tutor: "Part 4 of 4 of Greenblatt and Shlegeris (2024), 'The case for ensuring that powerful AIs are controlled', continuing 'The case for control (3)', which argued that the first transformatively useful AIs will be very dangerous but probably not wildly superhuman in the problematic domains (hacking, persuasion, manipulating scientific results or conceptual arguments), and introduced the control window and control frontier with the control-timeline widget. This lens carries the post from the subsection 'Without qualitatively wildly superhuman skills in problematic domains, evading careful control is probably difficult' through 'Implications and proposed actions', with all the appendices at the end, folded, for reference. The argument, in order: (1) without such skills, evading careful control is probably hard, as the AI's-eye view of a careful lab shows (unreliable inputs, no reliable sense of time, monitoring by trusted models, adversarial training, little thinking time, restricted coordination); this rests on GPT-4 confidently not scheming, so it can serve as a trusted monitor and paraphraser, and the extent of uninterpretable 'neuralese' reasoning and communication is another key issue (named here, at article line 240, and explained in the folded appendix 'Concerns with \"neuralese\" reasoning and memories'); (2) iterating against control evaluations helps develop robust protocols, as in computer security, but control needs serious, intentional effort as a core organizational priority; (3) the delay between controllable transformatively useful AI and the first uncontrollable AI is reasonably likely to be too short (6 months or a year) if capabilities are pushed as fast as possible, but holding off for several years is plausible: by default the authors expect a delay of a few years (1-3) between the few leading actors reaching transformatively useful AI and other actors imposing large exogenous risk, and AI labour can help build delay; and, by an Amdahl's-law argument, much more powerful AIs would add little speedup over transformatively useful ones, because human setup time stays a bottleneck, while being much more dangerous; (4) implications: labs should strongly consider committing to ensure the control property for all models plausibly capable of scheming, including internal use, until they have an extremely solid argument for alignment; control is cheap for current models and might cost around 10% of a lab's resources later; black-box control cannot handle wildly superintelligent AI, so avoiding building it matters; the public should consider asking labs for control, and control requirements could become a target of regulation or international agreement; technical researchers should treat control as a core strategy, explicitly red-team their techniques, and stop calling a safe-and-useful protocol 'aligned'. Vocabulary assumed from parts 1 to 3: scheming, the control property, untrusted versus trusted models, protocol, control evaluation, red team, catching red-handed, transformatively useful, uncontrollable, qualitatively wildly superhuman, problematic domains, control window, control frontier. New here: neuralese, and the two dimensions of a window, capability room and calendar time. **Same start, different futures.** Both worlds cross the *transformatively useful* line at the same moment. In **World A** the control frontier sits far above, so protocols scale across several model generations, but capabilities race there in little time. In **World B** the frontier is near, so only one generation stays controllable, yet the lab lingers inside the window for years. Read the lower timelines alone and A looks like the worse deal, but that flat "window in time" view hides that A packs four controllable generations into its sliver where B fits one. Time is only one of the window's dimensions.

\#### Part A: what must differ?

Both worlds begin at the same moment: the leading lab has just built its first transformatively useful model and it runs the best protocols it can afford. From here the worlds diverge. Notice that both windows are large, but not in the same way: World A's spans more model development with many controllable generations, while World B's spans more calendar time but with only one controllable generation.

These worlds started from the same model at the same moment, so something about each of them produced the picture you see.

What are the most important differences between World A and World B that could produce these two windows? For each difference, say where the control frontier sits and how fast the capability curve climbs toward it.
