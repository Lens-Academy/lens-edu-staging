#### Question
content::
\## Before you read: make the estimate, then interrogate it

Here is a setup. Suppose that tomorrow, without any new ideas, algorithms, or insights, the world's compute available for a single training run increased by twelve orders of magnitude. A trillion times more compute, running the methods we already have today.

**Part one.** Would that be enough to produce AI systems that can do essentially any cognitive job a human can do? Give a yes, no, or probability, and give the single strongest reason for your answer.

**Part two, which is the part that matters.** Whatever you answered, you used some method to get there. Now interrogate the method itself:

1. Suppose your answer turns out to be wrong. Is it more likely wrong in one particular direction? Which one, and why?
2. Is there anything your method structurally could not have taken into account? Not "things I don't know," but things of a kind your reasoning had no way to include.
3. Say what your answer is an answer *to*. Is it "this is when it happens," or "it cannot happen before this," or "it cannot happen after this," or something else? Pick one and defend the pick.

Question 3 is the one to spend time on.

max-time:: 12:00

assessment-instructions::
The student is about to read Daniel Kokotajlo's "Fun with +12 OOMs of Compute" and then Holden Karnofsky's two pieces on biological anchors, the second of which argues the method bounds rather than locates.

They have just produced their own estimate and been asked to classify what kind of estimate it is.

Acknowledge in 2 to 4 sentences. Reflect back, in their own words, what they answered to part two question 3, since that is the load-bearing one. If they classified their estimate as a location ("this is when it happens"), simply note that they did, without comment on whether that is right.

Do NOT:
- Introduce the terms lower bound, upper bound, one-sided, bounding, or pinpointing. The student must meet these in the reading, having already committed.
- Signal that treating an estimate as a location is a mistake, or that there is a better answer to question 3.
- Explain the biological anchors method or preview Karnofsky's correction.
- Evaluate whether twelve orders of magnitude would suffice.

If the student did not answer question 3, ask once, briefly, for a pick among the options offered, then close regardless of what they say.

Close with: "Keep your answer to question 3 where you can find it. The second reading is going to ask you the same question about a much more careful version of what you just did."
