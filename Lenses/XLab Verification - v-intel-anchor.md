content::
The previous section cataloged what undeclared development might show. This one
asks the empirical question: when undeclared facilities have actually been
found, what found them?

The answer is the unit's anchor, and it comes from Baker's Appendix E. Of the
four states where the IAEA investigated what turned out to be undeclared
facilities (Iraq, North Korea, Iran, Syria), **three investigations began
mainly or entirely because of national intelligence tips, and in none did the
IAEA find the facilities through its own safeguards processes.** Baker's
assessment of those tips: they "appear to be irreplaceable."

That is the identify/resolve split stated as evidence rather than as theory.
Intelligence generated the lead. The regime did the resolving: requesting
clarification, inspecting sites, collecting samples, comparing evidence against
declarations, and assessing compliance.

\## Where undeclared activity can be happening

Before the cases, the space of possibilities. Undeclared activity may occur:

- At a completely undeclared facility;
- Inside a declared facility performing an undeclared activity;
- Across several facilities whose combined activity crosses a treaty threshold;
- At a military or otherwise access-restricted site;
- Through a shell company, contractor, or proxy organization;
- In a third country;
- Within existing commercial cloud or industrial infrastructure;
- During a short operational window that is difficult to observe afterward.

Note how many of these are invisible to a signature catalog that assumes one
large, dedicated, civilian building.

\## The caveats, taught with the cases

Two honest qualifications travel with this finding, and they matter as much as
the finding does.

The cases mostly predate in-force Additional Protocols, so the safeguards regime
they indict is not quite the regime that exists now. And even the 2019–20 Iran
complementary-access successes began with an intelligence tip (the archive),
with treaty tools used for resolution. The split holds, but it is not evidence
that safeguards are useless; it is evidence about which step each tool performs.

Intelligence evidence is also characteristically **suggestive but not decisive
or internationally credible**. Satellite images are ambiguous. Shared documents
might be fake. That is precisely why regimes pair intelligence with escalation
mechanisms rather than treating a tip as a verdict.

Cover stories, though, are falsifiable: Iran's denial of activity at a site was
contradicted by commercially available satellite imagery.

force-feedback:: first
content:: Baker's four cases are nuclear programs. Would intelligence find a covert AI program the way it found those facilities? Answer each part in a few sentences:

1. Pick one feature of the nuclear cases that helped intelligence find the facilities. Does a covert AI program share it?
2. Pick one hiding place from the list above where intelligence would likely do worse against an AI program than against a nuclear one, and explain why.
3. What follows for how an AI agreement should split the work between national intelligence and the treaty's own tools?
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 30: one feature of the nuclear cases that helped intelligence find the facilities, with a reasoned judgment of whether a covert AI program shares it. Features that count include large, dedicated buildings with distinctive features visible from satellites, specialized inputs bought through procurement networks that leave a trail, a small community of specialists whose movements can be tracked, and intelligence agencies already watching those states. Either judgment earns the points when it is reasoned, for example that AI training also needs a large data center, power and cooling that can be seen, but such a building looks like a commercial one from outside, or that advanced chips come from a few suppliers, much as nuclear inputs did. 40: one place where undeclared activity can hide where intelligence would likely do worse against AI, with a reason tied to how AI development works. The places are (1) a completely undeclared facility, (2) an undeclared activity inside a declared facility, (3) several facilities whose combined activity crosses a treaty threshold, (4) a military or access-restricted site, (5) a shell company, contractor or proxy, (6) a third country, (7) existing commercial cloud or industrial infrastructure, and (8) a short operational window. Reasons that count include that training can run on rented commercial infrastructure that looks the same as legitimate use, that compute can be split across sites or accounts that each stay under a threshold, that a training run is short and leaves little physical trace afterwards, that the result (a set of model weights) is small and easy to move, and that the signals that would reveal it, such as power, cooling and chip purchases, are shared with ordinary commercial use. 30: what follows for dividing the work. For example, an AI agreement should still expect national intelligence to supply leads and keep its own tools (declarations, inspections, challenge inspections, audits, records) for resolving them, or it should lean more on built-in records such as chip accounting or provider reporting where intelligence is weak, or a tip should trigger escalation rather than settle a case, because intelligence evidence is suggestive rather than decisive. Credit any conclusion that follows from the answer's own first two parts. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Nuclear facilities were large, dedicated buildings fed by specialized inputs that few suppliers make, so intelligence had something specific to look for. A covert AI program shares part of that: frontier training needs a large data center, power and cooling, and chips from a few suppliers. But from outside, a data center looks like any commercial one. Intelligence would do worse against training on existing commercial cloud infrastructure: the run can be rented, split across accounts that each stay under a threshold, and finished in weeks, leaving no new building to photograph and records held by a provider. So an AI agreement should still treat national intelligence as a source of leads, but it cannot count on intelligence alone to find covert runs. It needs built-in records, such as chip accounting and provider reporting, and treaty tools that can turn a tip into an inspection."
