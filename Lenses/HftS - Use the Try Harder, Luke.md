summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky writes an imagined outtake from The Empire Strikes Back in which Mark Hamill argues with George Lucas about the scene where Luke fails to raise his X-wing from the swamp and gives up. Hamill's points are that the ship had already started to rise (a feasibility demonstration), that Yoda, with 800 years of experience, said it was doable, that Luke has not shown it is impossible, only failed once, that the stakes are huge (without the ship he is stuck on Dagobah), and that he should keep trying, rest and try again, at least for five minutes. Lucas closes with the point that people would not try for five minutes before giving up even if the fate of humanity were at stake. #### Question: Open
id:: e624d453-3d0e-44c9-ae16-3af50e32ae87
content::
Think of a time you called something impossible after one or two tries, like Luke with the X-wing. What did you do next, and what would trying harder have looked like?
optional:: true
feedback-instructions:: The student has just read Yudkowsky's "Use the Try Harder, Luke" and written an optional, ungraded reflection about a time they called something impossible after one or two tries, what they did next, and what trying harder would have looked like. There is no right answer and no score. Hamill's points in the essay: the X-wing had already started to rise, so it was shown to be possible, Yoda, an expert, said it was doable, Luke gave up after one brief try and could have rested and tried again, and the stakes were high. Lucas adds that people often give up within five minutes even on things that matter enormously.

Reply in 60 to 120 words, in short paragraphs, with no lists. Do not over-validate and avoid generic praise (great job, excellent, well done). Take their example seriously and do not judge their choice. Point to the one part of their story that matches one of Hamill's points most closely (a partial success, an expert who thought it doable, a very short try, high stakes), in plain words. If they described what trying harder would have looked like, help them make it one concrete step. If not, ask one question that helps them picture it. Allow at most 2 follow-up turns, then close briefly. If the student says they cannot think of an example, offer one small everyday kind (a hard conversation put off, a skill dropped after the first try) and let them skip if they want.
