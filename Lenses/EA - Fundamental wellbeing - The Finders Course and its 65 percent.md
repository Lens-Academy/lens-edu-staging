#### Text
content::
In 2014 Martin turned his interview findings into an online programme of meditation methods and positive psychology exercises, with questionnaires before and after ([Martin, "The Finders Course Experiments", slides](http://nonsymbolic.org/Yale-Presentation3-StatsVer.pdf)). The **Finders Course** is the original four-month version. **45 Days to Awakening** is a six-week version. Its website says that "approximately 65%" of participants "transitioned to Fundamental Wellbeing" ([Finders Course FAQ](https://finderscourse.com/faq.php)).

This lens reads the study behind that number, then Martin answering a sceptical interviewer, then a review by one participant. One more term first. A course lasts less than a year, so nobody can reach Martin's one-year mark for PNSE during it. For the course studies he uses **ONE** (ongoing non-symbolic experience): the same state, reported before it has lasted a year.

#### Callout: Who runs it and who is paid
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
- 45 Days to Awakening costs 497 US dollars, and the four-month Finders Course 3,000 US dollars ([Finders Course FAQ](https://finderscourse.com/faq.php)).
- Martin directs the Center for the Study of Non-Symbolic Consciousness, which ran the study below. For the six-week programme, the ethics review also came from that Center ([Martin et al., preprint](http://www.nonsymbolic.org/Effects_of_Two_Online_Positive_Psychology_and_Meditation_Programs_on_Persistent_Self-Transcendence-Pre-Print_Draft_Full.pdf), "Participants").
- The authors say the data are "not made publicly available at this time" (same preprint, "Author Note").

None of this shows the results are wrong. It means the people who measured the course also sell it, and nobody outside the team can check the numbers.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: What the earlier slides show and the paper does not
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
In 2015 Martin presented results from three earlier runs of the four-month course. Besides wellbeing measures, they included a dissociation scale (DES-II). On average across the three runs, scores on that scale rose by about 31% from before to after the course, and scores on its depersonalisation and derealisation factor rose by about 73%. Among participants who reported the transition, these rose by about 37% and 78%. Among the others, the rises were smaller and not statistically significant ([Martin, slides](http://nonsymbolic.org/Yale-Presentation3-StatsVer.pdf), "Disassociation/Absorption"). The list of measures in the 2023 paper does not include these scales.

We read this two ways. Martin's own interviewees were told by clinicians that their descriptions resembled depersonalisation and derealisation "except for the positive nature of the experience" ([Martin's paper](https://nonsymbolic.org/PNSE-Article.pdf)). So a rise may be the intended change in the sense of self showing up on a clinical scale. It may also be a cost. These numbers alone cannot tell the two apart.
#### End Callout

feedback-instructions:: The learner just read the evidence on Jeffery Martin's paid course: the 2023 study preprint (65% of six-week completers reported ongoing non-symbolic experience at the end, no control group, recruited from people interested in self-transcendence, self-rating, only completers analysed, 16% dropout), the Location 4 discrepancy (the questionnaires did not support claims that Location 4 brings the highest wellbeing), Martin's earlier slides showing dissociation and depersonalisation scores rose, Martin telling Spencer Greenberg that "a control group doesn't matter" and that about half of enrollees were already in fundamental wellbeing, the producer's note that the 65% was asked at the end of the course, and Kat Spartz's review (no fundamental wellbeing for her, but loving-kindness cured her impostor syndrome, and she thinks the 65% is exaggerated). They now write how it landed.

This is a processing phase, not a teaching phase. Help them say their reaction more precisely, do not resolve it. Confusion: ask what exactly is unclear. Skepticism: treat it as legitimate and ask what evidence would change their mind. Being impressed: ask which part of the evidence impressed them, and whether it was the numbers or the people's stories. If the learner says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold from the reading rather than repeating the question. 60 to 120 words. No generic praise. Keep an internal turn counter: after 2 replies, close the phase and send them on.

#### Question: Open
id:: c6d5efda-bc8b-4c31-95e6-9b1346a513fd
content::
A friend says: "A peer-reviewed study shows that 65% of people who take this six-week course reach lasting enlightenment." Name three reasons, from what you just read, why the study does not show this.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The learner was told a friend's claim: "A peer-reviewed study shows that 65% of people who take this six-week course reach lasting enlightenment." They are asked for three reasons why the study does not show this. Background the learner read: in the study, 65% of participants who completed the six-week programme, and who had said at the start they were not in the state, reported at the end of the programme that they were in ongoing non-symbolic experience. The study had no control group, recruited people already interested in self-transcendence, analysed only those who completed (16% dropped out), relied on self-ratings made with the researchers' own descriptions and unsupervised surveys, measured right at the end of the course with no follow-up, and was run, ethically reviewed (for the six-week version) and sold by Martin's own organisation, with data not public.

Score out of 100. Each valid reason is worth 34 points, capped at 100. A valid reason states one of these ideas in any words:
- No comparison group, so the change cannot be attributed to the course rather than expectation, time, or other practice.
- The 65% is people's own report, judged with descriptions the researchers supplied, so expectation, wanting to please, or having paid can inflate it.
- Asked at the end of the course, not followed up, so it shows nothing about lasting or "persistent" change (the one-year mark).
- Only those who finished were counted, so dropouts are missing from the denominator.
- The sample was self-selected people already interested in this kind of experience, so it does not generalise to "people who take the course" in general or to everyone.
- The people who ran and evaluated the study also sell the course, and the data are not public, so no one independent has checked it.
- "Enlightenment" overstates what was measured: the study measured ongoing non-symbolic experience by self-rating, which is not the same as the traditional meaning of enlightenment or awakening.
Two reasons that are the same idea in different words count once. A reason that is factually wrong about the study (for example, "it had only 10 participants") earns nothing.
feedback-instructions:: Tell the learner which of their reasons counted and which did not, and why, in plain words. If they scored below 100, name the single most important reason they missed: usually the lack of a control group or the end-of-course timing. Then add one sentence that is fair to Martin: the study still shows that most completers reported large changes in wellbeing measures, and that Spencer Greenberg and the participant reviewer both found something real in the course, even while doubting the number. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. No generic praise. One turn.
