\## Set points can move

A **set point** is the level of happiness a person returns to after good and bad events. Diener, Lucas and Scollon, whom you read in module 2, write that set points differ between people, "partly dependent on their temperaments", and "can change under some conditions" ([Diener, Lucas and Scollon 2006](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~ediener/Documents/Diener-Lucas-Scollon_2006.pdf)). Headey, Muffels and Wagner used 25 years of the same German panel to test how far set points move, and what people's own choices do to them. They compared each person's average life satisfaction in 1984 to 1988 with their average in 2004 to 2008, as a position in the distribution: a move of 25 percentiles is, for example, from the middle to the top quarter.

\## Aiming at peace can backfire

If circumstances only partly decide how you feel, the obvious move is to aim at the feeling directly. Iris Mauss's lab has studied what happens when people do. In one experiment, women who were led to value happiness more reacted less positively to a happy film, and the authors traced this to disappointment at their own feelings ([Mauss et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3160511/)). Brett Ford and Iris Mauss review this work in a book chapter, [The paradoxical effects of pursuing positive emotion](https://eerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/papers/Ford_Mauss_Pursuit_of_Positive_Emotion.pdf). Read its first two paragraphs and the section "How can we pursue happiness without paradoxical outcomes?" (pages 372 and 373, about 8 minutes).

Their summary: "the more people pursue positive emotion, the less likely they are to experience positive outcomes". They name three reasons: people set high standards for their happiness, they pick activities that do not make them happy, and they keep checking how happy they are, which gets in the way. They conclude that "happiness may be within reach if (a) people remove impossible standards and instead accept their emotions and avoid striving for any particular emotional state, (b) people engage in prosocial activities or effective emotion-regulation strategies, and (c) people automatize the pursuit of happiness".

The 2011 experiments studied only women, 59 in the first study and 70 in the second ([Mauss et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3160511/)). The chapter reviews the authors' own research programme. In our reading, the finding also applies to inner peace: wanting very much to feel equanimous is still wanting a particular emotional state.

\## Where the evidence lands

Putting module 2 and this page together, in our reading:

- Circumstances do move life satisfaction for years, and some losses do not fully heal ([Diener, Lucas and Scollon 2006](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~ediener/Documents/Diener-Lucas-Scollon_2006.pdf)). Over 20 years, 38% of the people followed moved by a quarter of the distribution or more, and life goals, a partner's emotional stability, working the hours you want, social life and exercise all go with lasting differences ([Headey, Muffels and Wagner 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2964245/)).
- People who report deep, lasting peace still react to events. What they report is a fast return, from seconds to a minute and a half for everyday upsets. Deep personal triggers can last for years, and a partner can see stress they do not report ([Martin](https://nonsymbolic.org/PNSE-Article.pdf)).
- Accepting your feelings goes with better mental health months later. Accepting your situation does not ([Ford et al. 2018](https://eerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/papers/2018_Ford_etal_Acceptance2.pdf)). Shinzen Young draws the same line: drop resistance to the pain, keep fighting its cause ([Young](https://www.shinzen.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/art_synopsis-pain.pdf)).
- Striving for a particular feeling, including happiness, tends to lower it ([Ford and Mauss](https://eerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/papers/Ford_Mauss_Pursuit_of_Positive_Emotion.pdf)).

So we found no source that supports "circumstances do not matter". What has evidence is a smaller claim: how you relate to your feelings changes how much circumstances hurt, and for how long.
