TLDR of what the user just read:
Why AI safety is intrinsically hard, in three parts. First, properties of the problem: it is new and poorly understood, so safeguards must be designed for technologies that do not yet exist; the field is pre-paradigmatic, with researchers disagreeing on threat models, sudden takeover versus gradual loss of control, and on which solutions are promising; models are trained rather than built, so they are black boxes without the modularity that makes ordinary software analysable; complexity creates blind spots, with glitch tokens like SolidGoldMagikarp as the example; no risk framework is exhaustive and no scenario holds most of the probability; some appealing arguments turn out misleading, including one whose own author now rejects his instrumental-convergence theorem as a way to think about the problem; time may be short; key terms like agency are hard to define and may be intrinsically fuzzy; a single solution probably does not exist; and safety is hard to measure, since capability progress benchmarks easily while safety progress does not, which can create an illusion of control. Second, expert uncertainty and divergent worldviews, with Hanson approaching risk through economic and evolutionary lenses and Sutton framing AI succession as a natural step rather than a catastrophe. Third, safety washing: portraying work as safer than it is, which misdirects funding and talent, with the debated impact of RLHF given as a case where well-intentioned work might be net negative.
