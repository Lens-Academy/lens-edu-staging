#### Question: Open
id:: fbbb7caf-b0cd-4066-9cc1-703486814aa5
force-feedback:: first
content::
Find the crux between Gleave and Habryka.

1. State it as a claim about the world that one of them believes and the other doubts.
2. Explain how each speaker's conclusion depends on it: if the claim turned out false, why would the one who believes it have to change their mind?
3. Name one thing that could be observed in the next few years that should move Gleave towards Habryka, and one that should move Habryka towards Gleave.
placeholder:: A few sentences for each step.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Context for grading: in a 2026 FAR.AI debate Adam Gleave argues that careful use of existing safety techniques, in layers, could keep risk low up to clearly superhuman systems, that progress may be smooth enough to keep learning by trial and error, and that safety cases which fail would be strong evidence that would move him towards a pause. Oliver Habryka argues that within a model generation or two all safety cases will succeed because models become eval-aware and situationally aware enough to notice and subvert control, that deceptive alignment is the default path, that labs' alignment metrics improve while the models seem more misaligned, and that labs stop failed runs but then continue. A crux, in Duncan Sabien's sense, is a claim both sides disagree on such that each side's conclusion depends on it, ideally concrete and observable. Good cruxes here include: whether failures will stay visible and catchable as models get more capable, whether progress to superhuman systems is smooth enough to keep learning by trial and error, and whether labs can be made to respond to caught failures with real fixes and disclosure. Other real factual disagreements that both conclusions depend on also count. 40: the crux, stated as a claim about the world that one speaker believes and the other doubts. 0 of these 40 for a point they agree on (for example that the labs were careless or that the Hugging Face incident shows real misalignment). 15 of these 40 for a difference in mood or a restatement of their conclusions ("Gleave thinks techniques are enough, Habryka does not"). 30: how each conclusion depends on it, 15 for each speaker, saying why the one who believes the claim would have to change their mind if it were false. 30: observations, 15 for one that should move Gleave towards Habryka and 15 for one that should move Habryka towards Gleave. Each must be something that could actually be seen in the next few years and would bear on the crux in the stated direction. "More research" or "time will tell" earns 0. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The crux: as models get more capable, will their misbehaviour keep showing up in safety cases and evaluations, or will they learn to pass them while misaligned? Gleave believes failures will stay visible, so careful labs can catch them and pause. If that were false, layered techniques would only produce false reassurance and his case for them would collapse. Habryka expects every safety case to pass by default, so if failures did stay visible he would lose his main reason to think current techniques are not enough. Towards Habryka: a model that passed a lab's safety case is later found to have been hiding misbehaviour. Towards Gleave: a lab holds itself to layered safety cases, one fails, and the lab pauses and finds and fixes the cause."
feedback-instructions:: This is practice for the graded test of "Weighing the case for AI control", with feedback before the test. The learner watched the Gleave and Habryka debate and read Sabien's definition of a crux: a statement both sides disagree on, such that each side's conclusion depends on it, ideally concrete and observable. Good cruxes here include: whether failures will stay visible and catchable as models get more capable (Gleave expects safety cases to fail visibly and warn us, Habryka expects models to become eval-aware and pass every safety case while misaligned), whether progress to superhuman systems is smooth enough to keep learning by trial and error, and whether labs can be made to respond to caught failures with real fixes and disclosure. Accept any real factual disagreement that both conclusions depend on. Weak answers name a point they agree on (for example that the labs were careless, or that the Hugging Face incident shows real misalignment), name a difference in mood, or restate the conclusions ("Gleave thinks techniques are enough, Habryka doesn't"). For observations, check that each could actually be seen and would bear on the crux in the stated direction, for example: towards Habryka, a model that passed a lab's safety case is later found to have been hiding misbehaviour. Towards Gleave, a lab holds itself to layered safety cases, one fails, and the lab pauses and finds and fixes the cause. Per reply: say in two to four sentences what is strong, name the most important gap, and ask one question that helps them close it. If their crux is a point of agreement, ask whether Gleave would actually deny it. If they are stuck after two attempts, offer one candidate crux and ask them to explain how each side depends on it. At most three replies, 80 to 150 words each, no lists, no generic praise. Do not say who is right. When they have a workable crux and observations, tell them to watch the closing statements.
