#### Text
content::
Paul Gilbert is a clinical psychologist in Derby, England ([Gilbert 2014](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/GilbertCFT.pdf)). He developed **compassion-focused therapy** (CFT) for people whose problems are tied to high shame and self-criticism, drawing on evolutionary psychology, cognitive behavioural therapy and Buddhist psychology ([Neff 2023](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Neff-2023.pdf), [Leaviss and Uttley 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4413786/)). Neff's talk said that self-criticism triggers a threat response and that kindness calms it. Gilbert's model spells that idea out. It also says something about wanting and striving, which is why we think it matters for this course.

In a 2014 paper for other clinicians, Gilbert sets out the three emotion systems at the centre of his model, which he calls the three circles. The text is dense in places: read for the three systems and how they differ, and skip the citations.

#### Text
content::
Gilbert applies the model to how people talk to themselves in a handout he wrote for the public ([Gilbert 2010](https://www.getselfhelp.co.uk/docs/gilbert-compassion-handout.pdf)). On page 28 he argues that self-criticism stimulates the threat system in the same way that being bullied by someone else does, and that kind and supportive self-talk can stimulate the soothing system instead. He adds that for very self-critical people, being kind to themselves can at first feel strange or threatening. On page 55 he answers the fear you may have met in the friend exercise, that without self-criticism you would stop trying. He separates **shame-based self-attacking**, which wants to punish, looks back at past errors and focuses on fear of failure, from **compassionate self-correction**, which wants to improve, looks forward and encourages. His example is two teachers with a struggling child: one picks on every mistake, the other points out what the child did well and how to improve.

**How well tested is the model?** Gilbert calls it "quite simplistic" in the handout ([Gilbert 2010](https://www.getselfhelp.co.uk/docs/gilbert-compassion-handout.pdf), page 16), and in the 2014 paper he writes that it is "a simplification" of complex processes, chosen because clients find it easy to understand ([Gilbert 2014](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/GilbertCFT.pdf)). The claims about specific brain chemicals (dopamine for drive, endorphins and oxytocin for soothing) come from the research he cites. We have not checked them, and this page does not test them. A 2026 study tried to test the model more directly: 88 adults rated how strongly they felt each of the three systems on a digital scale while their heart rate variability was recorded. The ratings moved as the model predicts across rest, rumination and recovery, and the balance between soothing and the other two systems tracked heart rate variability ([University of Queensland summary](https://learning-lab.uq.edu.au/article/2026/06/new-research-puts-physiology-behind-three-circles-compassion)). The evidence that the therapy helps is on the evidence page.

Gilbert founded the Compassionate Mind Foundation, a registered charity that sells training in CFT to professionals ([Compassionate Mind Foundation](https://www.compassionatemind.co.uk/)).

**Recall.** Spend two minutes writing down everything you remember from this page about Gilbert's model, without going back to it. No need to organise.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read an excerpt of Paul Gilbert's 2014 paper on the three emotion systems of compassion-focused therapy, plus our summary of his handout on self-criticism, and is writing what they remember without looking back. Key points: (1) three systems: threat-protection (anger, anxiety, disgust; our dominant system, behind the negativity bias; also triggered by blocked goals and by our own emotions), drive or seeking and acquiring (joy, excitement, pleasure in getting and achieving, competitive drive, dopamine, the lottery example), and soothing or contentment (safe, peaceful, content, neither threatened nor seeking, linked to affiliation, endorphins and the rest-and-digest system, different from mere relaxation); (2) Gilbert calls the model a simplification chosen because clients understand it; (3) concern that Western societies overstimulate wanting and competition, and overreliance on achievement can lead to depression when goals are blocked; (4) mindfulness and retreats may engage the soothing system; (5) from the handout: self-criticism stimulates threat like an outside bully, kind self-talk stimulates soothing, self-kindness can first feel threatening; (6) compassionate self-correction (wish to improve, forward-looking) versus shame-based self-attacking (wish to punish, backward-looking). Act as a brief, honest mirror: acknowledge what they got right without inflation, name the most important missing point in one sentence, correct any error in one sentence, and say that gaps are normal. Do not re-teach and do not ask follow-up questions. If they say they did not understand something, give one concrete foothold (for example the lottery winner who cannot sleep, as drive, versus an animal resting after it has eaten, as contentment). 60 to 120 words, short paragraphs, no lists, no generic praise. One turn, then tell them to move on.

content::
A friend says: "I feel great after I close a big deal or finish a hard project. So my soothing system must be working well."

Using Gilbert's model, explain what is wrong with this conclusion.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 70 points: the good feeling after closing a deal or finishing a project is the pleasure of the drive system (achieving, winning, excitement, activating positive emotion), not the soothing system. Soothing is a different kind of positive feeling: contentment, safeness and calm when not striving, wanting or achieving, often linked to kindness and connection. Accept any wording that makes clear the friend's feeling belongs to achieving or wanting and that soothing is a calm, non-striving contentment. 30 points: one further point from the model, either (a) a strong drive feeling says nothing about whether the soothing system is well developed, so the friend could still lack contentment when not achieving, or (b) drive that depends on success can turn into threat or depression when goals are blocked or fail. Cap at 30 if the answer says the feeling comes from the threat system.
feedback-instructions:: The learner was asked why feeling great after closing a deal does not show a well-working soothing system. They got a score. In 60 to 110 words: confirm what they got right. Below 100, name the most important missing point: the feeling is drive-system pleasure from achieving, while soothing is calm contentment when not striving or wanting; or that strong drive pleasure says nothing about whether one can feel content without achieving, and that blocked goals turn drive into threat. If they say they do not understand, give one foothold: Gilbert's example of a big lottery win that keeps you awake for days, as drive, versus an animal resting once it has eaten and is safe, as contentment. At most 3 replies, then offer to move on. No generic praise.
