\## Why start with "existential"?

It is easy to hear the phrase *existential risk* and treat it as a dramatic synonym for "a really bad thing". That loses most of what made the concept useful in the first place. Nick Bostrom's influential definition focuses on outcomes that either end Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically destroy its potential for desirable future development.[^bostrom] The second half of that definition does a lot of work. A catastrophe can be historically enormous, kill millions of people, and still leave later generations with the ability to rebuild. Another catastrophe can harm fewer people immediately and still permanently close off recovery, political agency, moral learning, or control over the future.

Derek Parfit's comparison between a catastrophe that kills 99 percent of humanity and one that kills 100 percent is one way to make the difference vivid. If we only count immediate deaths, the final one percent looks small next to the first ninety-nine. If the surviving one percent eventually rebuilds, however, all of the future lives and projects that follow recovery remain possible in one case and disappear in the other. Bostrom uses this kind of reasoning to argue that extinction may destroy far more than one generation. The argument becomes extremely strong on moral views that assign substantial value to future people, but it does not need to be accepted in its strongest form before irreversibility becomes relevant. Someone can reject astronomical population ethics and still care about preserving humanity's ability to recover, revise its values, correct mistakes, and keep options open for people who already exist.[^bostrom]

This also explains why the word *existential* can be useful even when it creates confusion. It directs attention to a property that ordinary measures of severity can miss: some losses are hard to reverse, and some are impossible to reverse. A death toll, a GDP loss, or a temporary collapse in living standards does not capture that distinction on its own. At the same time, the label is not a ranking algorithm. Calling something existential tells us something about the kind of downside being claimed. It tells us very little about the probability of that downside, whether a proposed intervention changes it, what the intervention costs, or what other harms the intervention might create.

\## A future can be permanently lost without everyone dying

Extinction is the cleanest example of irreversible loss, but it is not the only one. Bostrom discusses permanent stagnation and irreversibly flawed futures in which civilisation survives while losing much of its potential.[^bostrom] Recent AI-risk work develops this possibility in a more concrete way.

Jan Kulveit and coauthors describe a scenario they call *gradual disempowerment*. Their starting point is that modern economies, states, and cultures are not magically aligned with human interests. They are partly responsive to humans because humans are needed for them to function. Firms need workers and customers. States need taxpayers, administrators, soldiers, voters, or at least some degree of public cooperation. Culture needs human creators, audiences, and social networks. These dependencies create leverage. They do not make existing institutions just or perfectly aligned, but they give humans many channels through which preferences and needs can influence what the systems do.[^kulveit]

AI could weaken those channels gradually. If machine labour becomes more competitive than human labour across a large share of cognitive work, firms gain reasons to rely less on people. If states can raise revenue and exercise power with less dependence on citizens, some political incentives to remain responsive to citizens may weaken. If most cultural production and mediation passes through AI systems, human creators and audiences may become less central to what culture selects for. Kulveit and coauthors are especially interested in the feedback loops between these domains. Economic power affects politics and media. Political decisions affect the distribution of economic power. Culture shapes the preferences that drive both. A modest loss of human influence in one system can therefore make losses elsewhere easier.[^kulveit]

The point is not that this process must happen, or that AI adoption automatically makes institutions less humane. The paper's claim is conditional: the mechanisms that currently keep social systems responsive to people may depend more heavily on human economic and cognitive participation than we notice. If those mechanisms disappear, high output and high technological capability could coexist with low human influence. A society could look prosperous in familiar statistics while humans gradually lose the ability to determine what the prosperity is for. In the extreme case, humans could retain comfortable lives for a time while becoming politically and economically irrelevant, with no realistic path back to meaningful control. Kulveit and coauthors count sufficiently deep and permanent disempowerment as an existential catastrophe because the loss concerns humanity's future agency, not only its survival.[^kulveit]

Atoosa Kasirzadeh develops a related distinction between *decisive* and *accumulative* AI existential risk. Decisive scenarios look like the cases that dominate popular discussion: a sudden capability transition, a rogue system, a major military event, or some concentrated failure that quickly produces irreversible catastrophe. Accumulative scenarios build differently. Manipulation, economic instability, surveillance, military competition, information failures, and institutional erosion can interact over time. Each local disruption may look recoverable. Together they can reduce the resilience of the larger system until a later shock pushes it across a threshold from which recovery is no longer realistic.[^kasirzadeh]

This changes what we should look for. If the only model of AI existential risk is a single decisive takeover, then current misinformation, labour displacement, surveillance, or institutional weakening can look unrelated to existential safety. Under an accumulative model, some of those present-day harms can become part of a longer causal pathway if they damage society's ability to respond to later shocks. The distinction is not that "all current AI ethics issues are secretly existential". Most are not. The useful question is whether a present disruption degrades a system that future safety depends on.

\## Survival is not the only thing that can go very badly

There is a second reason not to make extinction the whole target. Some futures could preserve civilisation while containing enormous amounts of suffering. Research on *s-risks* uses the term for risks of suffering on a scale that is significant relative to the long-run future.[^srisk] The proposed mechanisms are diverse. Future digital minds could be created in huge numbers and treated as disposable labour. Powerful agents could use suffering as punishment, leverage, or part of conflict. A civilisation could deliberately or accidentally lock in values that permit severe suffering. Some scenarios involve no malicious actor at all; the suffering could be an incidental by-product of goals that ignore the welfare of the beings being created.

The moral case for giving these scenarios special attention is not universally accepted. Suffering-focused ethical views give particularly high priority to preventing severe suffering, sometimes much more priority than creating additional positive welfare.[^sfe] Other moral theories give suffering substantial but non-dominant weight. What matters for this unit is not settling that dispute. It is noticing that "humanity survives" and "the future goes well" are different propositions. A safety programme that only tracks extinction can miss futures that are terrible under a wide range of moral views.

Brian Tomasik's discussion of near-miss alignment makes the point in a deliberately sharp form.[^tomasik] Imagine that a system learns something close to human values, but a crucial part is represented incorrectly. A completely alien optimizer may be indifferent to suffering. A nearly aligned optimizer could create many morally relevant minds and still get their welfare badly wrong. Tomasik uses toy examples such as sign errors and "misconfigured minds" to show that moving a system closer to the target does not guarantee that every intermediate failure is less bad than every distant failure. His article does not establish that alignment research makes s-risks more likely overall, and he is explicit about the uncertainty. The example is useful because it forces us to separate *distance from our intended values* from *how bad the resulting world is*.

The Center on Long-Term Risk's overview makes a related point about intervention design. Solving alignment to human intent may reduce many acute risks, but it is not automatically sufficient to prevent astronomical suffering if human values themselves permit harmful treatment of future minds, if alignment succeeds for a malevolent actor, or if conflict between powerful systems creates suffering as a strategic side effect.[^srisk] This is one reason later units in this course will spend time on moral status, value aggregation, political legitimacy, and what a good future actually means. Technical control is part of the problem, not a complete moral specification of success.

\## One phrase, several different losses

By this point, at least four kinds of catastrophic loss are on the table. There is extinction, where there is no human future. There is unrecoverable collapse, where civilisation survives only in a permanently diminished state. There is permanent disempowerment, where humans survive but no longer control enough of the systems that determine the future to recover meaningful agency. And there are suffering catastrophes, where the future contains enormous disvalue even if civilisation remains technologically advanced.

These categories can overlap, and there is no reason to force every scenario into exactly one box. Their value is diagnostic. They tell us what the intervention has to preserve. An intervention aimed at extinction risk may focus on survival. A disempowerment intervention may focus on maintaining meaningful human control over institutions and resources. An s-risk intervention may care more about the welfare and bargaining relationships of future sentient beings. An accumulative-risk intervention may focus on resilience and feedback loops long before any single event looks existential.

This also changes how we should interpret the original question "why intervene at all?" If the only feared outcome were a speculative abrupt extinction event, then skepticism about that pathway could weaken the whole case for present action. Once the risk landscape includes slower forms of disempowerment, institutional fragility, malicious use, and catastrophic suffering, the case is less dependent on any one story. That does not make the overall case automatically stronger. Some of the broader pathways may be less severe, more tractable, or already well covered by ordinary policy. The gain is conceptual: we can ask which pathway we are discussing and what evidence bears on it.

\## The label still does not set the priority

There is a temptation, once a scenario is described as existential, to treat the moral work as finished. That move is too fast. A one-in-a-billion irreversible catastrophe and a near-certain but recoverable disaster can still present a genuine allocation problem. A cheap intervention that reduces the former by a large fraction may deserve attention. An expensive intervention with weak evidence may not. Some actions can also reduce one catastrophic pathway while increasing another.

So the useful role of existential-risk language is limited. It helps us notice irreversibility, future option loss, and forms of disempowerment that ordinary harm measures can miss. It does not tell us how much probability to assign, how much to spend, or how quickly to act. Those are questions about uncertainty and decision-making, which is where the next submodule goes.

As you continue, keep one question available: **what, exactly, becomes unrecoverable in the scenario being discussed?** Sometimes the answer is human life. Sometimes it is political or economic agency. Sometimes it is the ability to revise values later. Sometimes it is the welfare of future sentient beings. If you cannot answer that question, calling the risk "existential" is not doing much analytical work.

\## Phase 3: Learning Question

Consider two equally likely AI-caused outcomes.

**Outcome A:** 500 million people die, but civilisation retains enough institutional capacity to recover over the next century.

**Outcome B:** 20 million people die, and humanity remains prosperous, but humans permanently lose meaningful control over the economic and political systems that determine the long-run future.

A policymaker says Outcome A is obviously worse because it kills far more people.

What is missing from that argument, and what additional moral or empirical questions would you need to answer before deciding which outcome deserves greater priority?

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

45 points: The answer identifies a dimension not captured by the immediate death count, such as irreversibility, permanent loss of human agency, future option value, or unrecoverable effects on later generations.

30 points: The answer explains why that dimension can matter to the comparison, without assuming that it automatically outweighs present deaths.

25 points: The answer identifies at least one further moral or empirical question needed for the decision, such as how valuable continued human control is, whether the loss is genuinely permanent, how future welfare changes, or which intervention is tractable.

feedback-instructions::
Give 100 to 170 words of feedback. Name the strongest distinction the learner made and the most important missing consideration if any. A learner can reject special moral weight for future agency and still score highly if they identify the issue and argue coherently.
