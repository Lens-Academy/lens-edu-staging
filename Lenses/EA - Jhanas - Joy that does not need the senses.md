title: "Joy that does not need the senses"
tldr: "Two teachers who brought the jhanas to Western students, in their own words: Ayya Khema on what the jhanas teach about happiness and the senses, and Rob Burbea on why practise them at all."
summary_for_tutor: "Primary voices on the jhanas and happiness. Ayya Khema (Inquiring Mind interview, 1996): begin with a concentration practice (breath, visualisation or kasinas) until thinking stops; entry into the first jhana is 'utter delight'; after it, review how you got there, see that it is impermanent, ask what you are learning. First lesson: the delight we look for in the world lives inside us. The senses are 'only survival systems', not an amusement park. In the second jhana joy comes to the front; Westerners find it hard to be joyful without outside stimulation. She claims the joy sought through the senses has 'absolutely no comparison' to the joy inside, that people then stop looking for sense pleasures but still enjoy them, more so because they do not grasp them. Third jhana: contentment. Fourth: stillness and peace, the observing ego in the background. Rob Burbea (talk 'An Introduction to the Jhanas', 18 Dec 2019, retreat 'Practising the Jhanas'): the jhanas give resources that make letting go easier ('I don't need the food to be nice'); they show how the mind fabricates perception; and they open the sense of existence (the 'kiss of the mystic'). Jhanas alone will not liberate, nor insight alone; he names samadhi, insight, psychological work and the cultivation of virtues together. The question asks the learner to turn Khema's claim into something testable."

Ayya Khema was born in Berlin in 1923, learned meditation in Asia, was ordained as a Theravada Buddhist nun in 1979 and taught in Europe, the US and Australia ([Brasington's page on her](https://www.leighb.com/a_khema.htm)). She was one of the few teachers in her tradition who taught the jhanas to lay students, and Leigh Brasington, whose instructions come later in this module, is her student ([Brasington](https://www.lionsroar.com/entering-the-jhanas/)). In this 1996 interview she says what the jhanas teach about happiness.

#### Question: Open
id:: e5ea2ded-1589-4e51-9cf2-bf29854b3ee3
content::
Ayya Khema makes a claim about joy and the senses. Write it in one or two sentences, as precisely as you can, so that it could be tested on a person.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Ayya Khema (1996 interview) and a passage of Rob Burbea's 2019 introduction to the jhanas, and now restates Khema's claim so that it could be tested. A separate question after this one asks for predictions, so do not ask for them here.

What Khema says: the first lesson of the jhanas is that the delight we look for in the world lives inside us, and a skilled meditator has it "at their fingertips anytime they want it". The senses are survival systems, not an amusement park. In the second jhana one learns that the joy sought through the senses has "absolutely no comparison" to the joy inside. The result: people stop going looking for sense pleasures, still have pleasant sense contacts, are grateful for them, and enjoy them more because they do not grasp them. Burbea says something close: with these resources "I don't need the food to be nice".

A good restatement is specific: who (someone who can reliably enter at least the second jhana), what changes (less seeking of sense pleasures, not less enjoyment of them), and over what time. Name the most precise part of their version and one way to sharpen it (often: separating "seeking" from "enjoying", or naming a time frame). Point out that "you can produce delight at will" and "you stop needing outside pleasure" are two different claims if they merged them. Do not say whether the claim is true. 80 to 130 words. At most 2 replies. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open
id:: 7d365ef9-acb5-4c49-a97d-b56c560246e6
content::
If your version of the claim were true, what would you expect to see in someone's daily life? And what would you see if it were false?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner restated Ayya Khema's claim (after reaching the jhanas, people stop going looking for sense pleasures but still enjoy them, because the joy inside has "absolutely no comparison" to the joy sought through the senses) and now gives observable predictions for true and false.

Good predictions are observable and could come out either way. If true: for example fewer impulsive reaches for food, phone or shopping, less irritation when a pleasure is denied, and pleasant things still enjoyed. If false: seeking unchanged, or enjoyment flattened along with seeking, or a change that lasts only hours after a sit. Name the most observable prediction they gave and suggest how to make a vague one countable (a tally, a daily 0 to 10 rating, a time frame). Point out if their true and false predictions do not exclude each other. Do not say whether the claim is true. 80 to 130 words. At most 2 replies. No generic praise.
