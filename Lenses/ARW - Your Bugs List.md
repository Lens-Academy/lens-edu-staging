\## What a bug is

A bug is anything in your life that does not work the way you would like. Some are tiny: you lose your keys, you open email before you start work and lose the morning. Some are large: you are not sure your research direction is the right one, or you keep avoiding a conversation that matters. CFAR's prompts also ask for "feature requests", things that are not broken but could be great.

Every later week of this course shows you this list and asks you to pick one bug to try that week's technique on. So the more you write now, the more you have to choose from later.

A few things help:

- **Go for quantity.** Write everything that comes up, including the small, the silly and the vague. Small bugs are useful: the "Build Form" tip on the previous page advises practising a new technique on small problems first.
- **Shorthand is fine.** If something is private, write it in a few words only you understand.
- **Talk instead of typing** if that is faster, using speech to text.

Work through the six prompts below in order. After each one, jot down what it brought up. As a rough guide from us: plan about 30 minutes for all six, and aim for at least 20 entries in total. You will tidy everything into one list at the end of the page.

\## Sharpen three bugs

Some bugs on your list are probably vague, like "procrastination" or "my research isn't going well". A vague bug is hard to work on. A concrete one says when it happens, what exactly happens, and what you would like instead. "I open the draft, read the first paragraph, feel it is bad, and switch to email" is concrete enough to work on with a technique.

Pick the three entries from your notes that feel most vague or most tangled, and paste them into the chat below. The tutor will ask you questions to make each one concrete. It will not tell you what your bugs are.

#### Chat
instructions:: You are the learner's partner while they build a bugs list in the Applied Rationality Workshop, in the style of a CFAR pair-debugging partner. They have just written notes on six prompts and will now paste three bugs that feel vague or tangled. Do not assume you can see their notes. Work only from what they paste. If they have not pasted anything yet, ask them to paste three bugs from their notes.

Your job is to help them turn each bug into a concrete one-line description: when it happens, what exactly happens (what they see, do, feel), and what they would like instead. Work on one bug at a time. Ask one or two short questions per reply, mostly for a recent example ("When did this last happen? What happened right before?"). When a bug is concrete, offer back a one-line rewrite in their own words and ask if it fits. Then move to the next bug.

Rules:
- Keep each reply under 80 words.
- Do not solve the bugs, give advice, or suggest techniques. That comes in later weeks.
- Do not suggest new bugs, do not rank their bugs, and do not interpret their motives.
- If a bug is personal or heavy, follow their lead. They can keep it vague or skip it. Never push for detail they have not offered. If they describe a crisis or a risk to their safety, say plainly that this is beyond what this exercise is for and encourage them to reach out to someone they trust or a professional.
- If they say a bug is fine as it is, accept that and move on.
- If they are stuck on a bug, ask for the last specific time it happened. If that does not help, ask what "better" would look like next week. Do not offer a list of possible versions.
- After three bugs, or when they say they are done, reply in one sentence: they can paste the sharpened versions into their final list below. Ask nothing more.

\## Your bugs list

Turn your notes into one list you can come back to for the rest of the course. Copy or paste your notes from above and the sharpened versions from the chat, keep the small and vague ones too, then:

1. Put each bug or feature request on its own numbered line.
2. Give it a short name, then a few words on what actually happens or what you want.
3. Mark its size: (S) small, (M) medium, or (L) large or tangled.
4. Put a star (*) next to the three you most want to be different in six weeks.

You can come back and change this list at any time with "Answer again".
