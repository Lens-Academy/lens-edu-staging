A good information routine should leave you enough time for the work itself, whether that involves conducting research, developing new skills or pursuing other projects. Twitter can be particularly useful in this respect: posts are generally short, and researchers often respond to new developments within hours. A carefully curated feed can help you follow a research agenda without spending much time on it each day.

We realise that recommending Twitter as a source of research information may sound odd, especially to those coming from more traditional academic disciplines. Yet this is an established part of AI safety's research culture. Alongside the usual social media content, researchers share preliminary findings, discuss methodological problems, respond to criticism and articulate intuitions about open research questions. Sometimes a Twitter thread contains the clearest available explanation of a research result or the motivation behind a particular research direction.

In his LessWrong post [A daily routine I do for my AI safety research work](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/NENrmKWoEzXXmQjhr/a-daily-routine-i-do-for-my-ai-safety-research-work) (2022), AI safety researcher Stephen Casper describes Twitter as the first resource he checks in his daily research routine:

His entire routine, which also includes checking LessWrong, the Alignment Forum and new arXiv submissions, usually takes him less than fifteen minutes.

A similar recommendation appears in [How to Do a PhD (in AI Safety)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/reJYNiBAL3yC9fCkh/how-to-do-a-phd-in-ai-safety-1), which suggests following relevant researchers on Twitter to stay informed about recent developments.

\### Examples of research-related Twitter discussions

[**Owain Evans on emergent misalignment**](https://x.com/OwainEvans_UK/status/1894436637054214509) (February 2025)

An explanation of experimental results showing that fine-tuning language models on insecure code can produce undesirable behavioural changes extending well beyond coding tasks. The thread discusses the experimental setup, examples of model behaviour and comparisons with control models.

[**Owain Evans on negation neglect**](https://x.com/OwainEvans_UK/status/2055318932857459009) (May 2026)

A discussion of experiments in which models trained on documents explicitly warning against false claims sometimes ended up adopting those claims. The thread explains the findings, possible mechanisms and implications for alignment training.

[**METR on AI research capabilities and the limitations of time horizons**](https://x.com/METR_Evals/status/2123864981506798012) (November 2026)

A research update discussing difficulties with measuring increasingly capable AI systems, changes to evaluation methodology and the implications of recent results for assessing AI research automation.

These threads can be read alongside their associated papers and research reports. They provide relatively accessible accounts of what researchers themselves consider significant about their findings.

\### Curating your feed

If you are interested in a particular research agenda, start with the authors of papers you have found useful. Look at who they interact with, whose research they discuss and which organisations they follow.

You can also create separate Twitter lists for different topics, such as interpretability, AI control, evaluations, governance or digital minds. This allows you to check developments in a specific area without having to navigate the general recommendation feed.

Existing curated lists can provide a starting point:

- [AGI Safety Twitter List](https://twitter.com/i/lists/1185207859728076800)
- [AI Safety Twitter Updates](https://twitter.modelconduct.org/): an aggregated selection of safety-relevant discussions.
- [Media channels (AISafety.com)](https://aisafety.com/media-channels): includes curated lists of AI safety and AI policy accounts.

Social media is useful for discovering information, particularly when it has yet to reach more established publication channels. Claims that are important to your research or decisions deserve further investigation through the original evidence.
