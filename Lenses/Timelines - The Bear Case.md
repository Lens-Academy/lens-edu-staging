#### Text
content::
\## Reading Assignment

Two dissenting arguments, structurally different, same conclusion.

**Epoch AI's *The case for multi-decade AI timelines*** argues from outside: revenue trends, adoption rates, and what realistic economic slowdowns do to them, implying roughly twenty years rather than two to ten.

**Thane Ruthenis's *A Bear Case: My Predictions Regarding AI Progress*** argues from inside: that current methods plateau on general intelligence, and that scaling does not close the remaining gap.

Read at least one. Read both if you want the more interesting exercise, which is the question below.

#### Question
content::
\## The question worth asking about dissent

These two arguments reach roughly the same conclusion from almost entirely different premises. One never looks inside a model; the other barely mentions money.

1. For each argument separately, name the single observation over the next three years that would most damage it. They should be different observations.
2. Two independent lines of reasoning converging on the same answer is usually treated as strong evidence. Is it here? Consider whether they are genuinely independent, or whether some shared assumption is doing work in both.
3. Suppose one of them is decisively refuted next year and the other survives untouched. What should happen to your timeline estimate, and why is the answer not simply "move it earlier by half"?

assessment-instructions:: The student has read one or both bear-case arguments as optional material after the main timelines lens. This is an optional deep-dive, so the student is here by choice and can be pushed harder.

The capability being exercised: distinguishing arguments by their structure rather than their conclusion, and reasoning about the evidential value of convergence.

Key concepts:
- Epoch's argument is outside-view and economic: it does not depend on any claim about what models can do internally, only on diffusion, revenue, and realistic growth constraints. Damaged by a sharp, sustained revenue or adoption acceleration.
- Ruthenis's is inside-view and technical: it depends on a claim about what current methods can and cannot reach. Damaged by a model doing the specific kind of general reasoning he says scaling will not deliver.
- On independence: they are substantially independent in their premises, which does strengthen the conclusion. But a student should notice possible shared assumptions, most obviously that the near future resembles the recent past in kind, and that no discontinuity intervenes. Both are implicitly betting against the same tail.
- On question 3: refuting one does not simply halve the support, because the two arguments do not decompose the claim into independent halves. If the technical argument falls, the economic ceiling could still bind, and vice versa. The correct move is to notice which argument was carrying more of your own credence and update that portion.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only.

Response style:
- Calm and rigorous. Do not over-validate; avoid generic praise.
- Do NOT adjudicate the timelines question. The course has no house answer, and a student who finishes this unit believing the bear case and reasoning well has succeeded.
- If the student uses the convergence as proof, press on independence rather than on the conclusion.

Conversation flow: keep an internal turn counter, up to 3 replies, then close with a brief note on which of the three questions they handled most and least fluently.
