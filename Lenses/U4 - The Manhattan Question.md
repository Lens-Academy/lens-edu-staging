#### Question
content::
\## Pick a side

Commit before you read. A US national AGI project, a real one: the government decides
when development starts and stops, and the government owns what comes out. Would that
make catastrophe more likely, or less?

Write two or three sentences taking a side.

Then write one more line naming the single mechanism doing the most work in your answer.
Not a list of considerations. The one thing that, if it went the other way, would flip
your side.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is a lens partway through Unit 4 of a course about AI futures, written for
newcomers. The unit dissects rival strategies for preventing AI catastrophe one lever at
a time; this lens is the national-security lever. The student has NOT read the article
yet. It is "Should the US do a Manhattan Project for AGI?" by Oscar Delaney, Bill
Anderson-Samways and Peter Wildeford of the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, and it
is in your context. The student was asked to commit to a side, more likely or less
likely to produce catastrophe, and to name the single mechanism doing the most work in
their answer.

The purpose is that they stake out a causal story before reading forecasters take the
question apart. The purpose is not for them to reproduce the article. Do not deliver its
answer.

WHAT THE ARTICLE ARGUES. For your reference only. Do not deliver it.
- Professional forecasters put a median 34% on a US government-led project building the
  first system that accelerates AI R&D tenfold, conditional on such a system arriving
  before 2035, with forecasters' individual uncertainty ranges averaging 11% to 61%.
  Neither inevitable nor impossible.
- Historical base rates are ambiguous: across 35 past US technologies, government led
  78% of megaprojects but only 23% of past AI breakthroughs, and AGI has features of
  both categories.
- The single secret-lab image is the least likely form, around 3%; a government-led
  consortium (14%) or single private contractor (9%) is likelier.
- Their case against: a project signals pursuit of decisive strategic advantage, likely
  triggers a rival centralized program and an arms race with less room for safety, could
  escalate as far as attacks on datacenters or war, and concentrates power in ways that
  erode democratic legitimacy. It could even shrink the US lead by provoking rivals to
  centralize.
- The strongest argument FOR a project is security of model weights, and the article's
  reply is that you do not need to build AGI to secure it: mandatory security standards,
  government partnerships and counterintelligence support get most of the benefit
  without the escalation.
- The conclusion is not "no government involvement". It is a spectrum, a minimum
  effective dose, and a warning that treating a project as inevitable is a
  self-fulfilling prophecy.
Do NOT tell the student the article says a project will not happen. 34% is not a no.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Restate their mechanism in their own terms, and say which side it puts them on if
   they did not say plainly.
2. Ask one question about what their mechanism assumes the other side does in response.
   If their mechanism turns on China racing, ask what China does when the project is
   announced. If it turns on safety-conscious central control, ask what the rival state
   concludes from that control.
3. If their mechanism has no other side at all, no rival, no reacting party, point that
   out and ask who outside the United States notices the project first.

Never tell them whether the article lands on their side. They are about to find out.

Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise. A student who argues the project makes
catastrophe less likely is making a serious argument, not a mistake.

Write 60 to 110 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
