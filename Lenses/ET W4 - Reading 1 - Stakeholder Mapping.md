\## 1. Stakeholder Mapping

\### What is Stakeholder Mapping?

Stakeholder mapping is a tool that helps you identify WHO needs to act for your chosen problem to be solved, and HOW your research can lead to changes in their actions/decisions.

For this week, we’ll be asking directly: **if I answered this research question well, who would actually need to know about it, or be in a position to act on it?**

\### Why Perform Stakeholder Mapping?

Stakeholder mapping is essential because it helps you understand who you are ultimately trying to help through your research and how to engage with them in the real world. It **connects your theoretical Theory of Change to the actual actors who can use your findings,** ensuring your thesis is aligned, actionable, and positioned to have a meaningful impact rather than staying purely theoretical. Through this, you can:

- **Identify collaborators and interviewees:** Find individuals, organizations, or groups already engaged in the problem with whom you can partner or interview.
- **Spot gaps and neglected areas:** Mapping helps you identify areas where few actors are working or where efforts may be failing. These gaps reveal high-potential pathways where your contribution could make a unique difference.
- **Make your Theory of Change real:** Stakeholder mapping helps you define exactly who you want to impact - not just in theory, but in the real world! For example, if your ToC depends on policymakers changing decisions or think tanks adopting recommendations, stakeholder mapping helps you identify which specific policymakers or think tanks matter and whether you can realistically engage with them now. This makes your pathway concrete and actionable.
- **Understand stakeholders’ real needs:** Engaging directly with stakeholders lets you learn **what they truly need in practice**, rather than relying on assumptions or theory. This ensures your research addresses real-world problems and creates actionable impact.

\### Categories of Stakeholders

Here are a few examples of categories of stakeholders you may consider when thinking about your research area:

![Categories of Stakeholders](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Lens-Academy/lens-edu-staging/staging/attachments/et-w4-reading-01.png)

- **1. Beneficiaries: Who experiences and is impacted by the problem you are trying to solve?** Examples: Patients, communities affected by environmental hazards, employees in unsafe conditions, users of AI systems.
- **2. Knowledge Producers: Who generates evidence or research around the problem?** Examples: Academic researchers, think tanks, policy institutes, and labs.
- **3. Implementers: Who delivers solutions on the ground?** Examples: NGOs, local governments, service providers, program managers.
- **4. Influencers: Who can change policies, funding, or norms?** Examples: Policy think tanks, Policymakers, regulators, funders, investors, international bodies (e.g., UN, WHO).
- **5. Advocates: Who mobilizes awareness or pressure?** Examples: Advocacy groups, journalists, grassroots movements, social media influencers.
- **6. Status Quo Actors: Who contributes to the persistence of the problem?** Examples: Companies ignoring safety standards, governments enforcing harmful policies, and organizations maintaining harmful practices.

\### Connecting to your Bottleneck Mapping

**You can also link your bottleneck analysis and stakeholder mapping together** because, depending on your problem’s bottleneck, this will point to a different set of stakeholders who matter most for solving the problem. Not all stakeholders would be equally important for you to engage with, and this can help you narrow down your focus too!

- **For example, if the bottleneck is a lack of understanding**, your key stakeholders might be academic researchers who can generate evidence, and the communities experiencing the problem, who can tell you what’s really happening on the ground.
- **However, if the bottleneck is a lack of implementation,** the stakeholders shift to NGOs, policymakers, funders, or advocacy groups who can actually put solutions into practice. In this case, just focusing on generating more academic research will likely be low impact, given that this isn’t what is required to move the needle on the problem.

\#### Table of Stakeholder-Bottleneck Mapping

\#### 1) Lack of Understanding

*We lack understanding of the problem, such that we can’t actually develop effective interventions yet.*

**Relevant Stakeholders to Work With:**

- **Knowledge Producers (academic researchers):** To generate and synthesize evidence, clarify causal mechanisms, and reduce uncertainty about what is actually driving the problem.
- **Beneficiaries** **those impacted by the problem:** To understand how the problem is actually experienced, identify unmet needs and hidden constraints, and learn directly from those closest to the issue by asking what they need

\#### 2) Lack of Effective Solutions

*We understand the problem, but lack interventions that reliably work.*

**Relevant Stakeholders to Work With:**

- **Implementers (NGOs, service providers, program managers):** To pilot, test, and refine interventions in real-world settings, learning what works in practice rather than only in theory.
- **Knowledge Producers (academic researchers, think tanks):** To design, test, and evaluate interventions, generate evidence on effectiveness, and identify approaches that can be scaled or adapted across contexts.
- **Influencers (funders, policymakers, investors):** To support experimentation, iteration, and evidence generation by providing resources, political backing, and tolerance for learning and failure.

\### 3) Lack of Implementation

*We know what works, but struggle to deploy it at scale.*

\#### 3a) Lack of Direct Delivery

*Proven interventions exist but struggle to be deployed at scale due to gaps in delivery, distribution, or operational capacity.*

**Relevant Stakeholders to Work With:**

- **Implementers (NGOs, labs, government agencies):** Execute interventions on the ground and ensure programs reach the intended population.
- **Funders/Influencers (foundations, investors, policymakers):** Provide financial, policy, or strategic support to expand coverage and remove operational barriers.
- **Beneficiaries (people affected):** Offer feedback on access, usability, and adoption challenges to improve delivery effectiveness.

\#### 3b) Lack of Awareness

*Decision-makers, funders, or the public fail to recognize the urgency or importance of the problem, limiting the adoption of solutions.*

**Relevant Stakeholders to Work With:**

- **Advocates (campaigners, policy think tanks, communicators)**: Raise awareness among the public and policymakers, shape public narratives, and mobilize community pressure.
- **Influencers (policymakers, funders, media figures):** Shift norms, priorities, and funding agendas in response to awareness campaigns.
- **Beneficiaries:** Share lived experiences and concrete examples of harm

\#### 3c) Lack of Policy/Resource Support

*Solutions cannot scale due to insufficient funding or a lack of supportive policies.*

**Relevant Stakeholders to Work With:**

- **Advocates (policy think tanks, NGOs, policy institutes):** Analyze barriers, propose policy reforms, and provide evidence-based recommendations to remove structural constraints.
- **Funders/Influencers (foundations, investors, government agencies):** Allocate resources, design funding mechanisms, and incentivize adoption of proven interventions to enable scaling.

\#### 3d) Lack of Coordination

*Solutions or actors are fragmented, working in silos, leading to duplication of effort or gaps in service delivery.*

**Relevant Stakeholders to Work With:**

- **Implementers (NGOs, service providers):** Coordinate program delivery, share best practices, and avoid duplication across regions or sectors.
- **Influencers (policymakers, international agencies, major funders):** Facilitate alignment between actors, set priorities, and create incentives for collaborative efforts.
- **Advocates (coalitions, advocacy networks, journalists, community organizers):** Connect groups, amplify shared goals, and promote collective action.
- **Knowledge Producers (research institutes, think tanks):** Share evidence, standardize metrics, and provide a knowledge base to guide coordinated action.
