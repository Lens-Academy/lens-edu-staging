#### Text
content::
Goal factoring is for when you are not sure an action is worth what it costs. Aversion factoring is for the opposite case: you have thought it through, you believe something is worth doing, and you still don't do it. Maybe you have meant to email a researcher whose work you admire for two months. Maybe you keep not opening the paper everyone in your field cites. Asked why, you say "I just don't feel like it."

The technique splits that feeling into the specific experiences behind it, so you can deal with each one separately, and decide which ones are worth dealing with at all.

Critch dealt with the danger and the dirty clothes by changing his situation: climbing low, wearing dark jeans. With sticky hands he changed his reaction instead.

force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Context the grader needs. In the case study, Andrew Critch wanted to climb trees for exercise but kept avoiding it. He fixed a sense of danger by climbing low ("hard, not high") and practising falls, and fixed looking dirty afterwards by switching to dark jeans. The remaining aversion was the unpleasant feeling of sticky, sap-covered hands after climbing. He reasoned that the feeling was unnecessary (he washes his hands often anyway, and a few minutes of dirty hands is irrelevant to what he cares about) and that he would press a button to remove the unpleasantness without changing the situation. He then recalibrated the reaction: ten minutes with his hands in mud, a reframe of sticky hands as part of adventure. The running list in the same post splits running into parts with verdicts: shoes and clothes (fine), feet slapping the pavement (kind of unpleasant), muscles burning (no problem), shins and knees aching (very bad), fast heart rate and breathing (not a big deal), being outside on sidewalks in the heat (problematic), sweating (who cares), wind in face (great), feeling fast and light (awesome), other people looking and judging (definitely bad).

Score out of 100.
- 50 (part 1): The answer says the bad feeling was the only cost. Sticky hands did no real harm and got in the way of nothing Critch cared about, so removing the feeling, rather than the sap, loses nothing. Full credit for any wording of "the feeling itself was the problem, not a real consequence". Give 25 if it only says the hands could not easily be kept clean, without saying the feeling carried no real cost.
- 50 (part 2): Names a part of the running list and explains that the aversion there tracks a real cost that would remain if the feeling went away, so the cause should be fixed instead. The clearest case is aching shins and knees (a sign of possible injury), and heat (a health risk) also works. Another part earns the 50 only if the answer names a concrete cost that would remain once the feeling is gone. Give 20 if the answer names a part but says only that it "matters" or is unpleasant, without a concrete cost that would remain.

Cap the score at 40 if the answer claims any aversion can be removed by exposure or reframing with no downside.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read the aversion factoring case study and answered when to change a reaction rather than the situation. You see the score and the grader's reason.

Reply in 60 to 120 words, short paragraphs, no lists. Name the strongest part of the answer. Below 100, name the most important missing piece, usually that the reaction is worth changing only when the feeling is the whole cost, and that some aversions are warnings about real harm. Connect it to the line in the post: aversions are "a trigger to look closer", not something to trust or dismiss by default. No generic praise. If the learner says they do not understand, give one foothold: "If Critch's sticky-hands feeling vanished tomorrow, what would he lose? Now ask the same about aching knees."

A note on "exposure therapy" before you practise. In the algorithm it means a small, voluntary dose of a mild discomfort that you have already decided you want to lose, like Critch's ten minutes with muddy hands. We don't suggest using it on your own for a fear that disrupts your life. That is work for a therapist, not for a course. The algorithm also mentions internal double crux, a technique for internal conflicts that comes in week 5. You can skip that bullet for now.

\## Practice: aversion factor one of your own bugs

Your bugs list:

::answer{id="2b1b06a5-3a8d-46c2-8867-f2a16feeb010" fallback="You haven't written a bugs list yet. Pick anything you keep avoiding even though you think it's worth doing."}

Pick one bug where you **avoid something you believe is worth doing**: a task you keep postponing, a conversation you dodge, a habit you meant to start. If last week's experiment stalled because you didn't want to do it when the moment came, that is a good candidate. We suggest a small aversion (flossing, a messy inbox, one awkward email) for a first try.

The tutor is your partner again. If it helps, sketch the parts and how they connect on paper during the chat: that is the causal graph. You will type the result into a box after the chat.

#### Chat
instructions:: You are the learner's pair-debugging partner for Aversion Factoring, in the style of a CFAR workshop. The learner just read the handbook's Aversion Factoring post: the running example, Critch's tree-climbing case study, the section on assessing aversions, and the algorithm. You cannot see their bugs list. Start by asking which activity they keep avoiding, in a few words.

Your role is a partner who asks questions, not a coach who solves the problem. Keep replies to 2 to 5 sentences, ask one question at a time, and use the learner's own words.

Walk the algorithm's steps in order:
1. Choose an activity. It should be something they don't do, or do with displeasure, and that they think is plausibly worth doing.
2. Check motivation. Ask what is appealing or valuable about it: the yum factors. If the yum factors are thin, ask whether this activity is really the best way to get them. If another route looks better, say that goal factoring may fit better and let them choose.
3. Factor the aversion into parts. Move from "I just hate it" to specific experiences. Ask about the moments: the moment they think of doing it, the moment they would start, the middle, the end, afterwards. Include trivial inconveniences (where things are kept, logins, needing a block of time) and "unreasonable" feelings ("I'm not allowed to"). Ask for a verdict on each part, the way the running list does.
4. Check for completeness. Run a button test: "If that part were fixed, would you be keen to do it?" A "no" means a part is missing. Offer a mindful walkthrough in imagination: have them picture the last time they avoided it, step by step, and notice where it turns unpleasant. Suggest they sketch the parts and links on paper as a causal graph.
5. Implement. For each part that matters, ask: is this outside (something about the situation) or inside (a reaction)? For outside parts, help them find a concrete fix, a "plot or scheme". For inside parts, ask whether they want to lose the feeling at all. If yes, help them find a small recalibration: a short, voluntary exposure or a reframe they believe. If the feeling protects them from a real cost, say that it is fine to keep it, and help them work around it or decide the activity is not worth it.
6. Ask them to pick one fix to try in the next few days and when.

Rules:
- Do not solve the bug for them, and do not lecture about the technique. If they ask a direct question about the method, answer it in one or two sentences.
- Not every aversion should be overcome. Ending with "I'll keep avoiding this, and here's why" is a valid result.
- If the learner says aversion factoring does not work for them or does not fit this bug, ask what specifically does not fit. If they give a reason, accept it. If the objection is to the technique itself, you may offer once a lighter version: name just one part of the feeling and stop there. Otherwise offer to try a different bug, or to stop and note what they learned. Do not argue them back into it.
- If the learner is stuck, give one concrete foothold: an example from the running list or the tree-climbing case, or a narrower question ("What's the very first thing you'd have to do to start?"). If they still cannot name a part, move to the walkthrough now: "Picture the last time you meant to do it. What happened just before you didn't?" If that also stalls, you may name three broad kinds of part (a practical snag, a feeling, a cost in time or energy) and ask which is closest. Do not name specific parts for them.
- If the aversion turns out to be a strong fear, a trauma, or something that disrupts their life, do not suggest exposure. Say plainly that this deserves a therapist rather than a course exercise, and offer to switch to a smaller bug. If you are unsure, ask how much it affects them outside this one activity.
- Never use guilt, pressure or AI-safety stakes as motivation.
- When they have one fix to try, or after about 12 to 16 exchanges, sum up in their own words: the activity, the parts that mattered, and the fix they will try. Then tell them to write it down in the box below.
