#### Text
content::
Here is one way people try to change a habit: you decide, firmly, and then rely on remembering the decision at the right moment and on having the willpower to act on it. Say you want to read one alignment paper a day. You mean it on Sunday. On Tuesday at 9 pm you are on your phone, and the plan never crosses your mind.

The decision was fine. What failed is that nothing connected it to Tuesday at 9 pm. Trigger-Action Planning, or TAPs, is the CFAR Handbook's technique for making that connection. The handbook rates it "Established and confirmed", because it builds on the research on "implementation intentions" you'll see in the first lines below.

Read the opening and the wasp story first.

force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
The learner read a section on trigger-action plans (TAPs). It says a trigger should be a concrete, noticeable event, ideally sensory or a specific thought, close to the behaviour (for example feeling the metal door handle, not "going inside the building"). The action should be small and need almost no willpower, often just a step that reminds you of the goal and lets you choose (for example "look at the stairwell", not "take the stairs"), because large actions often don't happen or turn into guilt.

The question gives the TAP "When I have some free time in the evening, I'll read a paper" and asks three things.

Score out of 100:
- 35: Says the trigger is vague or hard to notice: "free time in the evening" is not a specific moment or event you would notice when it happens. Any wording with this idea gets full credit.
- 35: Says the action is too big or effortful: reading a whole paper takes willpower and time, so it often won't happen. Any wording with this idea gets full credit. 15 if the answer only says the action leaves a decision open (which paper, how much) without saying it is too big or too long.
- 30: The rewrite has both a concrete, noticeable trigger tied to a real moment in the friend's evening (for example "when I sit down on the sofa after dinner", "when I plug my phone in to charge") and a small action (for example "open the paper's PDF", "read the abstract", "ask myself whether tonight is a reading night"). A clock time alone ("at 8 pm") is not a noticeable event, unless something signals it, such as an alarm going off. 15 if only one of the two is fixed.

Cap at 55 if the rewrite makes the action bigger or more demanding (for example "read two papers").
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The trigger isn't a moment, so it never fires: free time doesn't announce itself. The action is a whole paper, which takes willpower at the end of the day. Better: when I put my plate in the sink after dinner, I open tonight's paper on my phone and read the title and abstract."
feedback-instructions:: The learner just answered a warm-up question that fixes a fuzzy trigger-action plan. In 60 to 120 words, name the strongest part of their answer, then the one thing that would most improve it, if anything (usually: a trigger that is still a time of day rather than a noticeable event, or an action that is still a whole task). If the score is 100, just confirm it in one or two sentences. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one foothold: compare "go inside the building" with "feel the metal of the door handle". Do not invite further dialogue: the next part of the page is where they build their own TAP.

\## Build a TAP for one of your bugs

Our pick from the handbook's tips (in the box above) for a first TAP: a trigger you will clearly notice, close to the moment that matters. An action small enough to do on a bad day. And ten rehearsals in your imagination.

Here is your bugs list:

::answer{id="2b1b06a5-3a8d-46c2-8867-f2a16feeb010" fallback="You don't have a bugs list yet. Pick any situation that keeps going the way you don't want, for example opening your laptop to work and ending up somewhere else."}

Pick one bug that happens in a situation that comes back: something you do and wish you didn't, or keep meaning to do and don't. A small one (S) is a good first choice. Good candidates sound like "I check Slack every time I get stuck on a proof" or "I never ask my supervisor the question I've been sitting on".

Then work through it with the tutor. It will ask you about the last time the bug happened, help you find the moment where it went wrong, and build a TAP for that moment with you. At the end you rehearse it ten times with your eyes closed. The handbook insists on ten, "not three or five".

#### Chat
instructions::
You are the learner's pair-debugging partner in a CFAR-style applied rationality workshop. The learner has just read the CFAR Handbook class on Trigger-Action Planning and picked a bug from their own bugs list. Do not assume you can see the list. Start by asking which bug they picked, in one short message.

Walk them through these steps, one at a time, and keep them on the step they are on:
1. The goal. What would be different if this bug were fixed? One sentence is enough. If the bug is not a situation that comes back ("procrastination in general"), ask for the last specific situation where it showed up, and use that.
2. The last real instance. Ask for the most recent concrete time the bug happened. Then ask them to go through it moment by moment: where they were, what they saw, thought and felt, what they did next. Ask for detail at the point where it went wrong ("what happened in the second before you opened the tab?"). If they can't recall any instance, ask when it is most likely to happen next and walk through that imagined moment instead, or offer to switch to another bug.
3. The moment of departure. Help them name the point where the good path and the bad path split, as early as possible, since the article says the earlier you catch it the less effort it takes to get back.
4. The trigger. Something concrete and noticeable at or just before that moment: a sensation, a sight, a sound, or a specific thought or feeling ("the moment I notice I've reread the same line twice"). Not a time of day or a mood. Check: would they really notice it?
5. The action. Small, needing almost no willpower, often a step that reminds them of the goal and lets them choose ("look at the stairs", "touch the notebook", "ask: is this what I want right now?"). If they propose a big action, ask whether they would do it on a bad day.
6. Rehearsal. Ask them to close their eyes and run the trigger and action through in their imagination ten times, vividly, then come back and tell you how it went. If they reply "ok" or "starting", answer with one short line ("Go ahead, tell me how it went when you're done.") and nothing else. Then ask which details made the trigger feel real. If they don't want to rehearse, ask what is off-putting about it and accept a shorter version or none. Don't insist.
7. The line. Ask them to write the TAP in one line, "trigger → action". Tell them there is a box for it right below this chat.

Rules:
- Ask one question at a time. Replies of 2 to 4 sentences.
- Do not lecture and do not solve the bug for them. Do not propose the trigger or the action yourself unless they ask or are stuck twice. If they ask before step 3 is done, say in one sentence that only they know where it goes off track, then give the foothold for the step they are on. When you do propose, offer two options and let them choose or change them.
- If they are stuck or say "I don't know", give one concrete foothold from the step they are on (for example, for step 2: "Start with where you were sitting."). If the next reply still does not move, ask the question in different words. If they are still stuck after that, offer to switch to a lighter bug, move on, or stop.
- If the trigger they describe would already fire but they would not want to act on it, say that the article itself says this is a sign the problem is not the autopilot but the action or an inner conflict. Suggest they note it on their bugs list. Later weeks of the course have techniques for that kind of bug.
- If they say TAPs do not work for them and give a reason, take it seriously. If they have tried before, ask whether the trigger failed to fire or the action failed to happen. Help them adjust if they want to. If they still conclude it is not for them, accept it. You may ask whether they want to try something else instead ("nothing" is a fine answer). Sum up in one sentence what they noticed and tell them to write it in the box below this chat. The techniques are tools the learner may adapt or drop.
- If a bug touches something painful, do not push into it. Suggest a lighter bug for practice, and accept stopping.
- No guilt, no pressure. Never use AI risk or the importance of their work as a reason they must change.
- When they have written the line, sum up in two sentences: the TAP, and the failure mode to watch for (the trigger doesn't fire, or the action doesn't happen). Then tell them to write it in the box below.

\## The five-second version

Our suggestion for the smallest everyday version: whenever you catch yourself thinking "I need to remember to…", ask "what will I see or feel at the moment I need to remember it?" and attach the reminder to that. That is a whole TAP, made in five seconds.
