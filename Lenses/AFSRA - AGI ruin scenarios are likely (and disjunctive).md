summary_for_tutor: >-
  Nate Soares gives a toy model of why he puts AGI ruin above 90%. Survival requires many things to go right together, in three groups. The world's overall state (a workable deployment strategy known to a leading organization, enough time, governments that do not get in the way). Technical alignment (people working productively on it, seeing every lethal problem in time, work that stacks or enough time, much of it done without an AGI to study). The internal dynamics of the leading organizations (caring about alignment in the right way, telling real solutions from fake ones under pressure, detecting warning signs, not leaking or splintering). He answers the objection that he multiplies correlated probabilities: they are correlated, mainly through humanity's general competence, so survival is higher than the product, and listing more problems cannot push his estimate below the chance he is wrong about that competence. But the world looks incompetent (the US COVID response), so the correlation does not rescue things. The one variable that would change his mind is whether humanity becomes much more competent about AGI than it is about everything else. The question asks the student to apply this to a new pandemic plan.

#### Question: Open
id:: c72e22d7-d73c-495e-84a1-096f844df62b
content::
A government wants to stop the next pandemic before it spreads. Its plan only works if five things all go right: the virus is detected early, tests are approved fast, a vaccine is ready within 100 days, factories can produce it in time, and enough people take it. The same government handled the last outbreak badly on most of these.

Two advisers estimate the plan's chance of success.

- Ana: "Give each step a 60% chance. Five steps at 60% each multiply to about 8%."
- Ben: "That's wrong. The steps aren't independent, so you can't just multiply. The real chance is much higher."

1. Using Soares's reasoning, what links the five steps, so that Ben is right that you can't just multiply their chances?
2. Using Soares's reasoning, why does Ben's point still not make the plan's chance of success high?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The post's idea: survival needs many things to go right together, so ruin is the default. Soares accepts that these things are correlated, mainly through the general competence of the people and institutions involved. Because of that correlation, the chance of success is higher than the product of the separate chances, and listing more steps cannot push it below the chance that those institutions are competent. But the correlation cuts both ways: if the institutions look incompetent, as their track record suggests, the steps tend to fail together, so the chance of success stays low. What would really change the estimate is the institutions becoming much more competent than their record shows. In the scenario, the government handled the last outbreak badly.
50: Step 1. Says Ben is right that the steps are correlated, so multiplying the separate chances understates success, and names what links them: the government's (or the society's) general competence, since a government able to do one step well is likely able to do the others. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer says the steps are correlated but names no common cause, or names a link other than competence and capability without explaining it.
50: Step 2. Says that the correlation only helps if the government is competent, and its poor handling of the last outbreak is evidence that it is not, so the steps are likely to fail together and the chance stays low. A strong answer may add that the estimate is then set mainly by the chance that the government becomes much more competent than its record, or that it is still higher than 8%. Give 25 of these 50 if the answer says the chance stays low without using the government's track record or competence as the reason.
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Ben is right that the steps aren't independent. They all depend on the same thing, how competent the government is. A government that can detect a virus early can probably also approve tests fast and run factories well, so the real chance is higher than 8%. But that only helps if the government is competent, and it handled the last outbreak badly. If competence is what links the steps, a government that lacks it will tend to fail at all of them together. So the chance mostly comes down to whether it has become much more competent than last time, and there's little reason to expect that."
