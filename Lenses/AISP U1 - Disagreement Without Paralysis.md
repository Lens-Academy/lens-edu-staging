\## A forecasting exercise that was supposed to help

The Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament brought together people with two different kinds of epistemic credentials. Domain experts knew particular risk areas. Superforecasters had demonstrated unusually strong forecasting performance. Participants were given incentives, time to deliberate, and opportunities to persuade one another. One natural hope was that expertise and forecasting skill would complement each other and the estimates would converge.

They did not. In several categories, domain experts remained substantially more pessimistic than superforecasters after the process. The disagreement was particularly striking for extinction and other unprecedented risks, including AI.[^cite-alexander-xpt]

The important educational fact is not one historical percentage. The exact estimates were produced at a particular time, with a particular participant pool, before later AI developments. The more durable problem is that a carefully structured attempt to create convergence left serious reasoners far apart. If the disagreement survives, a decision-maker still has to decide what to do with it.

\## Sometimes disagreement is ordinary

Not every disagreement deserves a philosophy seminar. One person may have missed a paper. Another may have misunderstood the question. A forecaster may be using an inappropriate reference class. An expert may know a mechanism that the forecaster has never encountered. In those cases, the disagreement can shrink when the missing information is supplied.

Scott Alexander's discussion of the tournament points out that experts and superforecasters agreed much more closely on questions with stronger historical data and diverged more on events with little direct precedent. This included global nuclear war, engineered pandemics, and AI.[^cite-alexander-xpt] When a question has repeated comparable cases, base rates and calibration have something solid to work with. When the event has never happened, both domain expertise and forecasting skill operate farther from their training distribution.

This does not tell us that unprecedented-event forecasts are useless. It tells us to look more carefully at what produces them.

\## One top-level number can hide several disagreements

Suppose one person says the probability of AI extinction is 2 percent and another says 20 percent. The obvious description is that they disagree by a factor of ten. That description is true and often not very informative.

They may agree that advanced AI will probably exist this century while disagreeing about whether human-level capability quickly leads to much greater capability. They may agree about capability but disagree about whether misalignment is common. They may agree about misalignment but disagree about whether humans can contain misaligned systems. They may agree that loss of control is possible but disagree about whether it produces extinction, permanent disempowerment, partial catastrophe, or negotiated coexistence.

These disagreements imply different research priorities. If the crux is rapid capability growth, better evidence about automated AI research matters. If the crux is deceptive alignment, evaluations and interpretability matter. If the crux is institutional response, governance and control may dominate. If the disagreement is moral, more capability data may do almost nothing.

This is one reason decomposition is not merely a way to make an argument look tidy. It changes what evidence is useful and what actions are responsive to the uncertainty.

\## Inside views and outside views

A second source of disagreement concerns what kinds of evidence deserve weight. An inside view builds a model from the mechanisms of the particular case. You might reason from training dynamics, scaling properties, strategic incentives, and institutional response to a conclusion about AI risk. An outside view starts from a broader reference class. How well have people predicted transformative technologies? How often do compelling disaster narratives come true? How accurate are experts or forecasters in related domains?

Both can fail. An inside view can become an elaborate story whose individual steps sound plausible while the conjunction is badly calibrated. An outside view can force a genuinely new event into a misleading historical category and ignore the mechanism that makes this case different.

The difficult question is how much each should move us. Alexander describes this tension directly. He finds the tournament's epistemic credentials difficult to ignore, yet he also thinks some concrete skeptical arguments about AI are weak. His response is not complete deference and not complete dismissal. He updates downward while retaining a substantial disagreement with the tournament.[^cite-alexander-xpt]

You do not have to endorse his final number. The useful point is that "respect the outside view" is not an algorithm. We need to understand what information the other view contains.

\## What should disagreement do to confidence?

Serious disagreement should usually lower confidence unless we have a good explanation for why the other side is mistaken. If a person with relevant information, good reasoning ability, and different incentives reaches a different conclusion, that is evidence about the robustness of our own view.

But this does not imply that rational people should average every pair of probabilities. The estimates may be correlated because both teams use the same data. One team may have information the other lacks. Their models may be differently well calibrated. They may interpret the question differently. Or they may disagree about values rather than empirical facts.

Averaging can be sensible in some settings, especially when forecasts are sufficiently independent and similarly informed. It becomes much less defensible when the numbers compress different questions. Before combining estimates, ask what generates the difference.

This is closely connected to the cluster-thinking idea from Module 1. Independent lines of evidence add more robustness than several estimates generated by the same underlying model. Agreement is most informative when the routes to agreement are genuinely different.

\## Deference has a stopping point

There is also a personal question. How much should you defer when someone else is much more expert?

The tempting extremes are familiar. One says "I should trust my own reasoning no matter who disagrees." The other says "I should copy the consensus because I am not the expert." Neither rule works across domains.

Deference is strongest when the relevant expertise is well defined, the experts have demonstrated success on closely related questions, incentives are reasonably aligned, and the question lies inside the field's mature methods. It is weaker when the category "expert" is broad, the target event is unprecedented, experts themselves disagree, the relevant field has little forecasting feedback, or the question crosses several disciplines.

AI catastrophic risk has many of these difficult features. The problem mixes machine learning, economics, security, institutional behaviour, philosophy, forecasting, and assumptions about technologies that do not yet exist. There may be deep experts on particular pieces without anyone having a privileged view of the whole chain.

This should make us modest. It should not make us unable to act.

\## Disagreement can become a research agenda

Suppose two groups disagree because one expects AI research automation to create a rapid feedback loop and the other expects experimentation, hardware, or organisational bottlenecks to dominate. That disagreement can be translated into measurable intermediate questions. How much AI research work can current systems complete autonomously? Which parts of research are bottlenecked by cognition rather than experiments? Does AI assistance shorten iteration cycles? What happens to the slope over time?

Now the disagreement is useful. Instead of debating one aggregate number, researchers can identify observations that move particular links.

The same move works for alignment. If one group believes systems will generalise human-approved behaviour robustly and another expects strategic deception, ask which experiments would discriminate between those hypotheses. If the dispute concerns governance, identify what institutional capacity would actually be required under the competing scenarios.

A disagreement with no identifiable crux is harder. It may indicate diffuse differences in priors, values, or accumulated judgment. Even then, naming that fact is better than pretending one missing statistic will settle the argument.

\## Action does not have to wait for consensus

A common response to unresolved disagreement is to wait. Sometimes that is sensible. If an intervention is costly, coercive, irreversible, and supported only by one fragile model, waiting for better evidence can have high value.

But waiting is itself an action. In fast-moving domains, options can disappear. Investments are made, infrastructure is built, standards become entrenched, and capabilities change. Evidence may become clearer only after decisions that were cheap to make earlier have become expensive or impossible.

This suggests a practical distinction between actions that are highly belief-sensitive and actions that are comparatively robust across plausible views. A belief-sensitive action only looks good if a particular contested model is close to correct. A robust action remains useful across several models or creates information that helps decide between them.

Examples could include building evaluation capacity, improving incident reporting, developing methods that make systems more interpretable, studying concrete cruxes, or creating institutions capable of responding to new evidence. Whether any specific example is actually robust still needs argument. The category is not a free pass.

Reversibility also matters. When uncertainty is high, a reversible action lets us update later. An irreversible intervention may require a stronger evidential bar because future information cannot undo it. This does not mean reversible actions are always better. Some threats themselves involve irreversible loss, and delay can remove options. The point is to treat reversibility as one decision-relevant property rather than as a slogan.

\## What the course asks from you

You will encounter confident people throughout AI safety. Some will tell you the risk is overwhelming. Others will tell you the central arguments are speculative. Some will have strong credentials, impressive forecasting records, or deep technical knowledge. Others will have useful perspectives precisely because they come from outside the dominant community.

The aim of this programme is not to transfer one group's probability into your head. It is to improve the structure of the questions you ask. What is the claim? What mechanism would make it true? What evidence supports that mechanism? What would change the relevant belief? Is the disagreement empirical, conceptual, normative, or strategic? Which actions still make sense while the disagreement remains unresolved?

That is enough to improve decisions even when it is not enough to remove uncertainty.

The final lens asks you to put your own current view into that structure. You will keep it and revisit it as later units challenge the parts concerning values, moral status, agency, evidence about minds, control, power, and the future we are trying to protect.

\## Phase 1: Recall

Without scrolling back, write down three structurally different reasons two informed people might disagree about AI risk. Do not just say that one person has a higher probability. Explain what could be different underneath the numbers.

Possible categories include different evidence, different reference classes, different beliefs about a threat-model link, inside-view versus outside-view weighting, different normative assumptions, different interpretations of the same evidence, or different priors.

\## Phase 2: Processing

Think of one important AI-safety claim on which you currently feel uncertain. Do not give your probability yet. Instead answer: **What do you think the smartest person who disagrees with you is seeing differently?**

If you genuinely do not know, say so.

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions::
The goal is perspective-taking, not forced agreement. Do not invent an opposing view immediately. If the learner gives a plausible crux, help them state it more precisely. If they genuinely do not know, offer two plausible candidate cruxes relevant to the claim and ask which is closer.

Keep an internal turn counter and close after at most two tutor replies.

\## Phase 3: Learning Question

Suppose two forecasting teams estimate the probability of a catastrophic event as 2 percent and 20 percent. A policymaker says: "The rational response is obvious. Average them and use 11 percent."

Why might this be a bad rule? What would you want to know about the disagreement before deciding how much to update?

force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

35 points: The answer recognises that disagreement can arise from different information, models, assumptions, priors, or normative premises, so the two numbers are not interchangeable independent measurements.

25 points: The answer explains that correlations or shared evidence matter and that averaging does not automatically create independent confirmation.

25 points: The answer identifies the value of locating cruxes or decomposing the top-level probability into specific claims.

15 points: The answer connects disagreement to decision-making, for example through information gathering, robust actions, reversibility, or a justified account of deference.

Do not require the terms "inside view", "outside view", "cluster thinking", or "crux". Grade the reasoning.

feedback-instructions::
Give 100 to 170 words of feedback. State which source of disagreement the learner handled best and name the most important missing factor if any. Do not tell them what probability they should adopt.

If they say they do not understand, give one foothold: explain that two forecasts based on the same dataset are not equivalent to two forecasts based on genuinely independent evidence. If they still do not attempt the question, rephrase it.
