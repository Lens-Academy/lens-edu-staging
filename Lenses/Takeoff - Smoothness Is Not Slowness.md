#### Text
content::
\## Reading Assignment

Two short readings.

**First, read Raemon's *"Slow" takeoff is a terrible term for "maybe even faster takeoff"*.** This is the terminology repair. It is short and it will change how you read everything else in this unit.

**Second, read Rob Bensinger's *Quick Nate/Eliezer comments on discontinuity*.** Having just watched an argument about whether progress is continuous, this reframes whether that was the right question at all. The suggestion is that the crux is not the shape of the curve but where the improvement is coming from.

Return here after both.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 1: Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down what you remember from both readings, without looking back. Then, separately: rewrite your working definition of "slow takeoff" from before, corrected.

assessment-instructions:: The student has just read Raemon's terminology piece and Bensinger's discontinuity comments, and has recalled both plus corrected their own prior working definition.

Key content:
- "Slow takeoff" in Christiano's sense means CONTINUOUS: capability arrives on a ramp, with substantial impact from weaker systems before any transition. It is a claim about smoothness, not duration.
- The wall-clock implication runs opposite to the word's connotation: the continuous scenario involves earlier and larger investment responses, so the transformative period plausibly arrives SOONER in calendar time than in a discontinuous scenario where things stay ordinary until a jump.
- Hence Raemon's title: slow takeoff is maybe-even-faster takeoff, and the term reliably misleads.
- Bensinger's reframe: arguing about whether the curve has a discontinuity is arguing about the wrong object. The question that carries the disagreement is where the improvement comes from, which determines what you can predict and how much warning you get.

Your role is diagnostic, not instructional. Act as a brief, honest mirror.

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

The most important thing to check: whether their corrected definition is actually corrected. If they still have slow meaning long-in-duration, say so in one plain sentence and state the correction directly. This is the one place in the unit where a direct correction beats drawing it out, because every subsequent reading depends on the term being right.

Otherwise: acknowledge what they captured, name gaps briefly, correct errors in one sentence, normalize incomplete recall, and close with one calibrating sentence. Do not over-validate, do not re-teach at length, do not ask follow-up questions, do not invite dialogue. One-turn response. Tell them to move on.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 3: Learning Question
Two researchers are arguing in public.

**Researcher X:** "I expect a slow takeoff."
**Researcher Y:** "I disagree, I think it will be fast."

They argue for an hour. At the end, you discover the following. X expects transformative AI to arrive around 2031, preceded by several years of visibly accelerating economic disruption. Y expects a long plateau, with things looking roughly normal until sometime in the 2040s, followed by an abrupt transition over a few months.

They used the words "slow" and "fast" the way the literature does. Both were speaking accurately. They spent an hour disagreeing, and it is not obvious they disagree about anything at all in the way each of them thought.

Rewrite both positions in language that makes the actual disagreement visible, without using the words "fast," "slow," or "takeoff." Then: is there anything left that they genuinely disagree about? And what would you have asked, twenty minutes in, to save them the other forty?

assessment-instructions:: The student has read Raemon and Bensinger and corrected their working definition. This is the main discussion phase. The question is a deliberate wedge, not the test question: a real and extremely common failure of the vocabulary, staged so the student must repair it by rewriting rather than by defining.

Learning outcome served: distinguishing the continuity axis from the calendar-duration axis, and recognising that they can vary independently and that the conventional terms conflate them.

Key concepts the student needs to reach:
- X is claiming CONTINUITY plus an EARLY date. Y is claiming DISCONTINUITY plus a LATE date. The words slow and fast are each tracking a different one of those two axes, which is why the conversation goes nowhere.
- The rewrite should look roughly like: X expects capability to arrive incrementally with substantial warning and economic disruption beforehand, reaching transformative levels around 2031. Y expects capability to remain unremarkable until an abrupt transition in the 2040s, with little advance warning.
- Once rewritten, the genuine disagreements are visible and there are at least two: the DATE (2031 versus 2040s) and the WARNING (years of visible disruption versus little). These are real and consequential, and neither is what "fast versus slow" was tracking.
- Notice also that on wall-clock arrival, X, the "slow" one, expects things sooner. This is Raemon's exact point, made concrete.
- The saving question, twenty minutes in: something like "when do you expect it, and how much warning do you expect" or "are we disagreeing about the shape of the curve or about the date on it." Any question that forces the two axes apart.
- A strong answer connects the warning axis to what actually matters: the amount of advance notice determines what safety work is worth doing and when, which is why the axis conflation is expensive rather than merely annoying.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items.

Response style:
- Calm, rigorous, and educational.
- Do not over-validate. Avoid generic praise.
- If the rewrite still smuggles in duration language, point at the specific words and ask for a replacement.

Conversation flow:
- Keep an internal turn counter. After 3 replies, ask whether to continue or stop. If continuing, reset the counter. If not, give the calibration summary.

What to do in each reply:
1. Answer direct questions directly.
2. Otherwise steelman their rewrite in 2 to 4 sentences without adding ideas they did not express.
3. Name 1 to 3 gaps plainly.
4. Ask 2 causal follow-up questions, each directly answerable. No opinion questions.

If the student is missing the core move:
- If they conclude X and Y agree entirely, ask what X's 2031 and Y's 2040s do to that claim.
- If they produce a rewrite but find no remaining disagreement, list the two axes back to them and ask whether X and Y have the same values on both.
- If they get the rewrite easily, push to consequences: "X expects years of visible disruption first and Y expects almost none. Which safety strategies are worth funding under X that are worthless under Y, and vice versa?" This is the bridge to the rest of the course.

Calibration summary (on close): name what they demonstrated, name what remains underdeveloped, give a direct test-readiness verdict.

Safety and integrity:
- If the student makes a strong causal claim, ask what it relies on and how it could be falsified.
- If stuck after 2 attempts, give a brief direct answer and move on.
