#### Text
content::
On the first page you picked a negative thought about yourself and rated how much you believe it and how uncomfortable it makes you. This experiment uses that thought. For one week you work on its content, as Beck and Ellis would. For another week you work on your relationship to it, as ACT would. Each evening you rate it again, so you can see which week moved the numbers.

A lab version of this comparison exists. Andreas Larsson and colleagues asked people to handle a personal negative thought for five days with either a cognitive restructuring technique, a defusion technique, or no instructions. Defusion lowered how believable the thought was and raised comfort and willingness to have it more than both other conditions. Restructuring also improved discomfort and willingness, though less ([Larsson et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26685210/)). That was one brief study, in people who were not patients, so your result is a real test and not a formality.

#### Callout: Week A: a thought record
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**What to do.** Use the Thought Diary from the free self-help modules of the Centre for Clinical Interventions, a public mental health service in Western Australia. Module 6 explains the evidence and disputation steps ([CCI module 6](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/-/media/CCI/Consumer-Modules/Back-from-The-Bluez/Back-from-the-Bluez---06---Detective-Work-and-Disputation.pdf)) and module 7 the last step ([CCI module 7](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/~/media/CCI/Consumer-Modules/Back-from-The-Bluez/Back-from-the-Bluez---07---The-End-Result.pdf)). When the thought shows up, or once a day if it does not, write:

1. what happened just before it (the activating event)
2. the thought, and how much you believe it from 0 to 100
3. what you feel, and how strongly from 0 to 100
4. any unhelpful thinking style you recognise, such as jumping to conclusions or labelling
5. the factual evidence for the thought and the evidence against it, and your answers to questions such as "What other explanations could there be?" and "How might someone else view the situation?"
6. a balanced thought that takes all the evidence into account
7. how much you now believe the original thought, and how strong the feeling is now

**Dose.** Seven days (our choice, close to the five days in Larsson's study). Each diary takes about 10 to 15 minutes (our estimate).
#### End Callout

#### Callout: Week B: defusion
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**What to do.** Follow the homework from a small trial of defusion with students who had distress and low self-esteem ([Hinton and Gaynor 2010](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ913509.pdf)):

1. Write the thought on an index card and carry it with you all week. The point, in the authors' words, is a daily demonstration that you can have the thought "without restricting behavior or having to control them."
2. Three times a day, take out the card and say the thought, or its key word (for example "boring"), out loud and fast, over and over, for 20 to 45 seconds, until it sounds like a string of sounds. This is the word repetition exercise that Masuda and colleagues found lowered both discomfort and believability of negative self-related thoughts more than distraction or trying to control the thought ([Masuda et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14998740/)).
3. From the third day, vary how you say it, using the other ways from the same trial: very slowly, sung, in a different voice, or as an announcer on a radio station called "bad news radio".

You can also put "I am having the thought that" in front of the thought, which is another defusion technique ACT uses ([ACBS](https://contextualscience.org/the_six_core_processes_of_act)). In one lab study this prefix made negative self-statements less uncomfortable but, against the authors' prediction, more believable ([Healy et al. 2008](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/4954/1/YBH_experimental%20test.pdf)). So rate both numbers if you use it.

**Dose.** Seven days, three short practices a day, about 2 minutes each (our estimate).
#### End Callout

#### Callout: Measure and compare
tone:: blue
#### Text
content::
**Every evening of both weeks**, at about the same time, rate two numbers from 0 to 100, as on the first page: how much you believe the thought right now, and how uncomfortable it makes you when you think it. These are the two ratings the defusion studies use ([Masuda et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14998740/), [Larsson et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26685210/)). Also keep a rough tally of how often the thought came that day, because Larsson's study found its frequency fell with defusion and not with restructuring.

**Order.** Toss a coin to decide which week comes first, and if you can, leave a few days between them (our suggestions). Whatever you do in the first week may carry over into the second.

**Compare.** At the end, plot or list the 14 pairs of ratings. Look at the change within each week, not just the final numbers. Before you start, write down what result would count against your prediction.

**Limits.** You know which method you are using and what you expect, and expectations alone can move self-ratings. One person over two weeks cannot show which method works better in general. It can show which one moved this thought for you.
#### End Callout

#### Callout: Safety
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
These are self-help exercises, not treatment. If the thought is tied to very low mood that lasts for weeks, or to thoughts of harming yourself, stop the experiment and talk to a doctor or therapist, or a crisis line if it is urgent (our advice).
#### End Callout

#### Question: Open
id:: be6d4c9e-c2cc-4c51-8cf4-7cf6f9c9880f
content::
Before you start: which week do you predict will lower your belief rating more?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is about to run a two-week self-experiment on a recurring negative thought about themselves: one week of a CCI thought record (evidence for and against, balanced thought), one week of ACT defusion (saying the thought out loud fast for 20 to 45 seconds three times a day, carrying it on an index card), rating belief and discomfort 0 to 100 each evening. They predict which week will lower their belief rating more. In 40 to 80 words, restate the prediction, and ask them to write down, before starting, how big a difference between the weeks would count as a real difference for them (for example 10 points). Do not say which method is better. If they mention very low mood or thoughts of self-harm, tell them kindly to stop and talk to a doctor or therapist. One turn. No generic praise.

#### Question: Open

content::
After the two weeks: how did your ratings change in each week? Come back to answer this when you have run the experiment.
optional:: true

They report how the ratings changed. In 80 to 130 words: summarise what changed in each week in one or two sentences, say whether it matches Larsson and colleagues 2016 (defusion lowered believability more than restructuring over five days; restructuring also reduced discomfort), and name the most likely alternative explanation for their result (expectations, the order of the weeks, a change in circumstances, or simply time passing). Remind them that in a CBT versus ACT trial for depression both treatments worked partly through both routes (Emmelkamp's summary on the evidence page), so a mixed result is common. Do not say which method is right. At most two replies. No generic praise.
