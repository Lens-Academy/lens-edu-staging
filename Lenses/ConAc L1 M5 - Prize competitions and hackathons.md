content::
Research competitions and hackathons provide another way to follow developments in AI safety. Organisers often formulate concrete problems, explain their significance and provide starting points for investigating them. These materials can offer a useful introduction to research questions that have recently attracted attention.

The submissions are worth exploring even if you do not intend to participate. They provide examples of how other researchers approach the problem, what can be accomplished with limited resources and which questions remain open.

\### Examples from 2026

[**Apart Research Sprints**](https://apartresearch.com/sprints)

Apart Research organises recurring weekend research hackathons, usually combining online participation with local in-person meetings. Participants work in teams on a specific AI safety problem and submit an experiment, evaluation, analysis or other research output.

Topics explored in 2026 include AI collusion, incident response, digital minds and epistemics. Some successful teams are subsequently invited to develop their projects into more substantial research through Apart's programmes.

You can browse the [archive of previous sprints](https://apartresearch.com/sprints/all) and their research outputs.

[**ARC White-Box Estimation Challenge**](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Kben8CzS4awCwNw5c/announcing-the-arc-white-box-estimation-challenge)

In June 2026, the Alignment Research Center launched a competition focused on improving estimation algorithms for neural networks, announcing a prize pool of at least $100,000 for subsequent rounds.

The announcement explains the relationship between the competition's mathematical problem and ARC's broader research programme. It is a useful example of how an established research organisation can formulate part of its research agenda as an open technical challenge.

[**Corrigibility Research Fund**](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/FBqe5dt8ZjaHN4Xj9)

In July 2026, Max Harms announced a fund dedicated to corrigibility research, with at least $200,000 allocated to grants and prizes. Approximately half was intended to reward research completed during the year.

The announcement also includes the grantmaker's reasoning about why corrigibility deserves more research attention and what kinds of contributions would be valuable.

[**AI Philosophy Competition**](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/BKroooXStChonhtRe/)

Supported by Forethought Research, this competition offered $11,000 in prizes in 2026. It illustrates the opportunities available to people interested in philosophical and methodological questions arising from advanced AI, alongside more conventional technical research.

Competitions are usually announced through LessWrong, the EA Forum, organisational websites and community newsletters. The [AISafety.com events directory](https://aisafety.com/events) is another useful place to look for upcoming opportunities.
