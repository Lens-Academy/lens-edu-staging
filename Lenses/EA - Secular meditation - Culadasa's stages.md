#### Text
content::
Culadasa (John Yates, 1945 to 2021) taught physiology and neuroscience for many years and practised meditation in the Tibetan and Theravada traditions for four decades ([Simon & Schuster author page](https://www.simonandschuster.com/authors/John-Yates/2116400973), [culadasa.com](https://culadasa.com/)). His book *The Mind Illuminated* (2015, with Matthew Immergut and Jeremy Graves) is a full meditation manual. Its core is a model of **ten stages** of breath meditation, each defined by what the meditator can and cannot yet do, so that you can tell where you are and which problem to work on next. The short free handout below is his 2008 version of the same model.

First, the four **milestones** that group the ten stages, and a warning.

#### Callout: Weighing the claims
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
The handout opens by calling this "the easiest and most effective method ever taught". It gives no comparison with other methods to support that. We found no trial that tests the stage model or how long the stages take, so the evidence is Culadasa's teaching experience and his students' reports.

In August 2019 the board of Dharma Treasure, the organisation he led, published a letter denouncing him for sexual misconduct, wrong speech and "taking what is not freely given". He disputed the allegations and in 2021 released a 33-page account of his side ([Guru Viking](https://www.guruviking.com/podcast/ep78-culadasa-denounced-in-the-dharma)). This says nothing direct about whether the stages are accurate. It is relevant to stage 10, which says anger, ill-will and strong desire "virtually disappear", and he says himself that the stage is not permanent freedom.
#### End Callout

#### Question: Open

Pick one feature Culadasa gives for stage 10. How could you check whether someone actually has it, without relying on what they say about themselves?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read Culadasa's 2008 handout: the four milestones, stage 10 (calm, joy and equanimity become the normal condition, strong desire noticeably attenuated, negative reactions rare, anger and ill-will virtually disappear, others see the meditator as happy, easygoing, non-competitive, perhaps passive, relatively immune to disturbing events, not much bothered by physical pain; but not permanently free, and a prolonged break in practice shows the meditator still subject to suffering), and how long it takes (some people under a year, some in three years with 1 to 2 hours a day plus retreats). They also read that no trial tests the model, and that his organisation removed him in 2019 over sexual misconduct. They now pick a feature and propose a check that does not rely on self-report.

Good checks use other people's observations (partners, colleagues rating anger or reactivity over weeks), behaviour (how they act in conflict or under provocation), physiological measures of stress or pain response, or comparison before and after a long break in practice, which Culadasa himself predicts should show a decline. Confirm a workable check in one sentence. Then name one weakness of it (for example, raters who know the person meditates may expect calm, or behaviour in one situation may not generalise). If they rely only on self-report, ask how they would rule out the person wanting to see themselves as advanced. Reply in 80 to 130 words. Do not judge Culadasa as a person. No generic praise. At most two replies.
