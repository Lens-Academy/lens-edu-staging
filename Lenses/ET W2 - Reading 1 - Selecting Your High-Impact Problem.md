\## Selecting Your High-Impact Problem

#### Callout: Overview

This week focuses on how your thesis can make a real-world impact by addressing a problem that is important, neglected, and tractable. Here, we aim to tackle one of the potential pitfalls of research - selecting the wrong problem (such that solving it doesn’t result in any impact).

**Important Note:** We want to highlight that impacting the world through your thesis is a really ambitious goal, especially given your time and resource constraints. As such, the goal of these exercises is not to put pressure on *definitely* having an impact on solving a massive problem. Instead, they are designed to help you practice thinking strategically about how research can increase the chance of real-world impact, and to train the mindset of approaching high-impact work, both for your thesis and beyond.

\## 1. Why does selecting the right problem matter?

Choosing which problem to work on is one of the highest-leverage decisions you'll make, not just for this thesis, but at many decision points across your life and career. This week is designed to help you land on a problem you genuinely believe matters and where you're well-placed to make a real difference.

As a concrete illustration of why this choice matters. Consider two diseases that each affect large numbers of people. Multiple Sclerosis (MS) accounted for roughly 0.034% of the global disease burden in 2021 ([IHME](https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-compare/)). Malaria accounted for roughly 1.91%, **around 54 times as much.** Contributing meaningfully to solving either disease would be a genuinely valuable contribution to reducing suffering. But if you could only make progress on one, a comparable amount of effort directed at malaria would likely reduce far more of the global disease burden than the same effort directed at MS.

This isn't to say MS research is a bad choice. It's to illustrate a simple but easy-to-forget point: within any category of "important problem," **some options will help you use your resources, time and effort to go dramatically further than others.** Choosing *quickly and without much thought* may result in you working on a problem that's trivial, already solved, or too difficult to realistically move forward - such that even amazing execution can end up producing very little real-world impact. That's what the rest of this week is designed to help you avoid!

**Links to Ikigai.** Building on the ikigai concept introduced in Week 1, this framework provides a powerful lens for selecting high-impact problems linked to **what the world needs.** Ikigai emphasizes the intersection of what the world needs and where your unique skills and strengths can make a difference. By focusing on problems that lie at this intersection, you can direct your energy toward work that is not only meaningful and fulfilling for you but also truly impactful for others.

\## 2. How do I identify the right problem to work on?

You may be at a stage now where you’re certain you want to find a problem that is worth you spending a lot of resources, time and effort on - such that the impact will be non-trivial and real! However, when navigating the world - which has many different potential areas you can contribute to - how can you approach selecting a problem to invest your efforts into? One of the key frameworks we recommend for assessing potential problems to work on is the Importance-Tractability-Neglectedness (ITN) framework.

\### The Importance, Neglectedness, Tractability Framework

“The Importance-Tractability-Neglectedness (ITN) framework is a set of mental shortcuts (often called heuristics) that allow you to more easily narrow in on problems that you can have an especially large impact by solving, if you were a good fit for solving them” (Non-Trivial Research Foundations Program)

This framework breaks down the potential impact of working on a problem into three key factors:

- **Importance – How much would solving the problem improve the world?** This measures the scale of the issue, including how many people or sentient beings are affected, the severity of the problem, and the long-term benefits of addressing it.
- **Neglectedness – How few resources (people, funding, attention) are currently dedicated to solving the problem?** Working on neglected areas often allows your contribution to have a disproportionately high impact. If a problem is important but not neglected, your efforts may add little value because many others are already making progress - in this scenario, your time and energy may be invested elsewhere.
- **Tractability – How solvable is the problem?** Even if a problem is important and neglected, if little can realistically be done to address it, efforts may have minimal effect.

\#### Why use the ITN framework?

The ITN framework can help you avoid common traps that may reduce the real-world impact of your work:

- **The "Good Intentions, Wrong Problem" trap:** Focusing on problems that feel important but don't actually affect many beings severely, or where solutions wouldn't create lasting change.
- **The "Interesting but Irrelevant" trap:** Working on intellectually stimulating problems that won’t meaningfully improve lives, even if solved perfectly.
- **The "Crowded Field" trap:** Entering areas where your marginal contribution will be minimal because thousands of well-resourced people are already working on the problem.
- **The "Impossible Dream" trap:** Pursuing problems that are so intractable that even years of effort are unlikely to generate real-world progress or impact.

By explicitly assessing problems along these three dimensions, the ITN framework helps prioritize areas where your work is most likely to create meaningful, measurable change. It also highlights failure modes, such as working on interesting problems that are already saturated with effort.

\##### Info box: Ways to Think About Importance & Improving Lives[^1]

There are two ways to think about how you may be able to increase impact:

**“Helping more people” (horizontal extension)** – moving along the x-axis increases impact by reaching more individuals, even if the improvement per person stays the same.

**“Helping people to a greater degree” (vertical extension)** – moving along the y-axis increases impact by improving outcomes for each individual, even if the number of people helped stays the same.

***⭐ Key insight:*** *You can increase your impact by either increasing the number of beings you help, deepening the improvement for each being, or ideally, doing both simultaneously!*

\#### Examples of Using the ITN Framework to Select Problems

The following examples illustrate common scenarios where a problem may fail to maximize impact along one or more ITN dimensions.

|  |  |
|---|---|
| Interesting but low importance | Example: Predicting Netflix Viewing Habits<br>A student develops an advanced algorithm to perfectly predict which Netflix show someone will watch next with 99.9% accuracy. While technically impressive and intellectually stimulating, even perfect success would only save people a few minutes of browsing time. The problem may be considered not important because it affects relatively few people in a trivial way - the "harm" of spending extra time choosing entertainment is minimal compared to problems like specific diseases or social conditions. |
| Important but not neglected | Example: HIV Prevention Campaigns in Sub-Saharan Africa<br>A public health student works on developing new HIV prevention campaigns in sub-Saharan Africa. This is important (HIV affects millions with severe health consequences and early death) and tractable (we have proven prevention methods like education, testing, and PrEP), but it's not neglected - major organizations like the WHO, and hundreds of NGOs already dedicate billions of dollars and thousands of workers to HIV prevention in this region.<br>*💡 This doesn't mean you shouldn't work on it if you're genuinely passionate about HIV prevention, but you might consider whether there's another global health problem you'd be equally interested in that receives less attention - perhaps hepatitis B prevention in Southeast Asia, or snakebite treatment in rural India, where your contributions could have greater impact by an additional person working on it.* |
| Important and neglected, but not tractable | Example: Passing Comprehensive Global Carbon Pricing<br>A student wants to design and implement a global carbon pricing system to halt climate change. This is important (climate change affects billions and threatens ecosystems and future generations) and relatively neglected, as coordinated global policy solutions are limited. However, it is not immediately tractable - complex political, economic, and social barriers make universal adoption unrealistic in the short term.<br>*💡 This doesn't mean you shouldn't pursue this topic. Here, you can think about whether there are ways to make this more tractable. For example, this may look like collaborating with policy think tanks to support their legislation work on climate policy, or conducting evidence-based research and communication to build public and political support.* |

#### Callout: Note

#### Text
content::
**Note**: ITN is not the only, or necessarily the best, way to prioritize between causes. Treat ITN as a set of useful shortcuts for narrowing your options quickly, not a strict rulebook. Before you lean on it too heavily, it's worth asking what this framework might be missing, and which kinds of causes it may be systematically privileging over others!
