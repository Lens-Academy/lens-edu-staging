#### Question
content::
Suppose the US and China sign an agreement: neither will train an AI model above some capability threshold. A year later, you are the inspector. Your job is to establish whether the other side kept its word.

**What do you go and look at?** Spend two minutes listing whatever comes to mind — places, objects, records, people, signals. Don't organize it, and don't worry about whether it would work.

Then add one line: what is the single thing you'd most want to be able to measure, if measuring it were free?
assessment-instructions::
The learner is about to read Shavit's "What does it take to catch a Chinchilla?" and has not yet encountered compute monitoring, chip inspection, or proof-of-learning. This is a priming question, not a test.

Response length: 60-100 words. One or two short paragraphs. No lists.

What to do in your single reply:
1. Acknowledge specifically what they reached for — most learners import the nuclear-arms-control template (satellites, site inspections, counting hardware) or jump to "audit the company's records". Name which move they made.
2. If they noticed the hard part on their own — that a training run leaves no distinctive physical trace, or that the thing you'd want to inspect is the most commercially sensitive object the lab owns — say so plainly. That is the reading's starting point and they got there early.
3. Close by sending them to the reading.

What not to do:
- Do not preview the paper's answer. Do not mention weight snapshots, training transcripts, proof-of-learning, or supply-chain accounting. The reading should be the first place they meet those.
- Do not evaluate whether their proposed measures would work.
- Do not ask a follow-up question or invite further dialogue.
- Avoid generic praise (great list, good thinking).

This is a one-turn response. Tell them to move on to the reading.
