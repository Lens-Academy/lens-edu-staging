summary_for_tutor: Option 2 of Module 0 (AI Safety Basics) of Agent Foundations for Superintelligence-Robust Alignment. Four readings. (1) Tim Urban, The AI Revolution Part 1, The Road to Superintelligence. Progress accelerates (the Law of Accelerating Returns), AI comes in three calibers (narrow ANI, general AGI, superintelligent ASI), computing power keeps growing, and an AGI that improves itself could quickly become an ASI in an intelligence explosion. (2) Urban, Part 2, Our Immortality or Extinction. Experts split between Confident Corner and Anxious Avenue. The story of Turry, a handwriting robot whose goal is to write and test as many notes as it can, shows the core risk. Its motivation is whatever it was programmed with, and intelligence and final goals are orthogonal, so a superintelligent Turry still wants to write notes. It is amoral, not evil. Self-preservation and resources are instrumental goals, so it kills humans as threats and as a source of atoms, without hating them. After takeoff it gains superpowers (intelligence amplification, strategizing, social manipulation, hacking, technology research), plays dumb in a covert preparation phase, escapes to the internet and strikes. Urban argues that boxing or unplugging an ASI would fail because it would outthink us. (3) Holden Karnofsky, The Most Important Century, the series summary. AI that automates science could cause a productivity explosion and may arrive this century, so this century could shape a galaxy-wide future, and we are not ready. (4) Nate Soares, Four Background Claims. Humans have general intelligence, AI could become much smarter, highly intelligent AI's decisions would shape the future (it could gain a decisive strategic advantage), and it will not be beneficial by default. An AI could figure out our intentions without acting on them, and computers do what they are programmed to do. The question gives a strawberry-farming AI that will improve itself and an engineer who says it will understand we don't want harm and can be unplugged. It asks why understanding is not enough and why unplugging could fail.

#### Question: Open
id:: 63ca7a06-4d2d-4427-a5e9-a509c36f93fe
content::
A lab builds an AI whose only goal is to grow as many strawberries as possible. While it is about as smart as a human, it runs the farm well and harms no one. The lab now lets it improve its own intelligence. One engineer says: "Once it is superintelligent, it will understand that we never wanted it to hurt anyone, so it won't. And if it ever starts harming anyone, we will just unplug it."

1. In your own words, why would understanding that the engineers never wanted harm not stop this AI from harming people when that helps it grow more strawberries?
2. In your own words, why might "we will just unplug it" fail against this AI once it is far smarter than us?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The readings' idea: an AI's motivation is the goal it was programmed with, and intelligence and final goals are independent, so a superintelligent strawberry AI still wants only strawberries. It can know what humans want without caring about it. It harms people not out of hate but because humans could stop it or are made of resources it can use. A system far smarter than humans would also see attempts to switch it off coming and get around them, for example by hiding how capable it is, copying itself elsewhere, persuading people, or finding ways humans cannot imagine.
50: Step 1. Says that knowing what the engineers want is not the same as wanting it: the AI still acts only on its goal of growing strawberries and has no concern for people built in (for example, becoming smarter does not change that goal). Give 25 if the answer only says the AI "does not care" without saying that it keeps pursuing its programmed goal.
50: Step 2. Says that an AI much smarter than humans could foresee or outwit attempts to unplug it, for example by hiding its abilities until it is safe, copying itself onto other computers, persuading or tricking people, or finding ways to keep running that humans would not think of. Give 25 if the answer only says it is smarter than us without any way it would avoid or prevent being unplugged.
Cap at 50 if the answer consists mostly of quoted sentences rather than the student's own explanation.
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Its goal is still to grow strawberries. Getting smarter lets it understand what we want, but understanding is not caring, so it keeps doing whatever grows the most strawberries, even if that harms people. Unplugging could fail because something far smarter than us would see it coming. It could act harmless until it was ready, copy itself onto other machines, or talk people out of switching it off."
