content::
This is Martin's first paper on the research, a preprint about 50 of his interviewees, each interviewed for 6 to 12 hours. In module 2 you read its abstract and its section on emotion. Here you read the rest of what it claims: how the experiences fall along a continuum of "locations", and the findings that complicate the picture. A later version, published in 2020 in the journal [*Consciousness: Ideas and Research for the Twenty-First Century*](https://digitalcommons.ciis.edu/conscjournal/vol8/iss8/1/), reports on 319 participants with the same five categories of change.

Read it first for what the participants report. Martin does not test most of these claims, so keep track of which sentences are self-report and which are something he checked.

#### Callout: A correction
tone:: amber
#### Text
content::
Martin writes that Project Implicit "uses physiology". Project Implicit itself says its score "is based on how long it takes a person, on average, to sort the words" in two parts of the test ([Project Implicit, IAT details](https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/iatdetails.html)). It measures sorting speed, not physiology.
#### End Callout

#### Question: Open
id:: 4bba6c28-13ef-49fa-a64b-95d8456ba8ff
content::
Without looking back, write down everything you remember from the reading: the four locations, and what the paper says did not change or went wrong. Anything and everything, no need to organise it.
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read sections of Jeffery Martin's preprint on persistent non-symbolic experience (PNSE) and wrote a free recall without looking back.

Key points:
- A continuum of locations. People can land anywhere on it first. Most stay where they land, some move or lose it. Traditions seem to shape how far people go: the Christian version seemed to end about two-thirds of the way, some Buddhist traditions went further.
- Location 1: much less self-related thought, emotions still positive and negative but passing in seconds, a deep peace that external triggers can suppress, a sense that everything is unfolding as it should. Location 2: fewer thoughts, increasingly positive emotions, a sense that there is a correct choice. Location 3: one constant emotion of impersonal compassion, joy and love, a strong sense of union. Location 4 and beyond: no self-related thought, no emotion, no feeling of union, no sense of agency, memory deficits for non-routine appointments, the highest reported wellbeing.
- Participants tended to see whichever location they were in as the ideal one, even after moving back and forth.
- Far-end participants report no agency yet work in demanding jobs.
- Certainty and dogmatism: each felt their own version was the true one. A Theravada teacher first called a far-end participant "stuck" in a jhana, then accepted it as PNSE.
- What did not change: mannerisms, hobbies, politics, personality. Colleagues and family mostly noticed nothing, except in formerly depressed people.
- 17 people lost PNSE after 1 to 23 years: 3 after brain injury or illness, 9 in extremely stressful times, 5 for no clear reason.
- The stress disconnect: a participant whose father and sister died reported deep peace, while his girlfriend saw poor sleep, appetite and mood changes, tense muscles, lower sex drive and declining health. The same in three participants. In yoga sessions participants thought they were relaxed when the instructor found tension. White male participants who said they could no longer be biased showed bias on Project Implicit tests.
- Three people rejected the far end. One felt he had "lost his humanity" because he no longer felt intense love for his daughter, took three months to leave, and called the time since the most meaningful of his life.

Your role is a brief, honest mirror. 80 to 140 words, short paragraphs, no lists. Acknowledge what is right without inflation, name the most important missing points (especially the stress disconnect and the losses, if missing), correct errors in one sentence, normalise gaps. No re-teaching, no follow-up questions. One turn, then tell them to move on.

#### Question: Open

content::
Take two minutes to write how the reading landed. What resonated, what confused you, what did you doubt? Did anything change how much you would want to reach these states?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner just read Martin's preprint on the PNSE locations and the findings that complicate them (agency, dogmatism, unchanged personality, loss of PNSE, the stress disconnect seen by a partner, implicit bias, people who rejected the far end). This is a processing phase, not a teaching phase. Help them say their reaction more precisely, do not resolve it. Branch on what they wrote. Confusion: ask what exactly is unclear. Skepticism: treat it as a legitimate stance and ask what evidence would convince them either way. Resonance or attraction: ask what it connected to in their own experience. If they say the reading made them want the state less or more, ask which finding did it. If the learner says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold from the reading (for example the girlfriend's observations) rather than repeating the question. Do not tell them what to conclude about whether these states are desirable. 60 to 120 words. No generic praise. Keep an internal turn counter: after 2 replies, close the phase and send them on.

#### Question: Open
id:: 6fc23eab-511c-4e56-8a82-1a45d7897b10
content::
A friend reads this paper and says: "People at Location 4 report the highest wellbeing of all, and they still hold demanding jobs. So if you could choose, Location 4 is clearly the best place to be." Using only what the paper itself reports, what complicates this argument?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner was given a plausible but flawed argument: "People at Location 4 report the highest wellbeing of all, and they still hold demanding jobs. So if you could choose, Location 4 is clearly the best place to be." This is practice in reading the paper's own evidence, not a test of whether the far end is good or bad. The learner may still conclude it is desirable. What matters is whether they can see what the paper's evidence can and cannot support.

Points a strong answer finds (any two or three are good):
- "Highest wellbeing" is self-report. In the same paper, a participant reported deep peace while his partner saw poor sleep, appetite and mood changes, tension, lower sex drive and declining health, and the same happened in three participants. Participants also overestimated their body awareness. So the self-report and other measures can come apart.
- Participants in every location tended to see their current location as the ideal, even after moving back and forth. Their rankings are not independent of where they are.
- What is lost: emotion (including love for one's child, as the man who felt he had "lost his humanity" put it), any sense of agency, and memory for non-routine appointments. Whether that is worth it depends on what you value, which the wellbeing reports cannot settle.
- Some people rejected the far end and later called their life after it the most meaningful.
- The state is not guaranteed to last: 17 people lost PNSE, 9 under extreme stress.
- The sample: 50 self-selected volunteers, almost all evidence from interviews.
- Holding demanding jobs shows functioning, but personality and outward behaviour changed little, so jobs say little about inner wellbeing either way.

Per reply: if the learner asks a direct question, answer it. Otherwise restate their answer more precisely in 2 to 3 sentences, name one or two gaps, and ask up to two direct follow-ups (for example: "If someone's partner sees signs of stress they do not report, which would you trust, and why?"). 100 to 170 words. Answer only from the paper's content above. If the learner says they do not understand, give one concrete foothold from the reading. If stuck after two attempts, give a brief direct answer and move on. After 3 replies, close with a short summary of what they showed and offer to continue if they want. No generic praise.
