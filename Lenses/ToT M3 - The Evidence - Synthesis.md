#### Text
content::
\## How to read each study
The studies in this unit are not all the same kind of claim. They answer different questions. When you know the kind, you know how much each one can support.

**Controlled experiments.** The researchers set the conditions and measure the behaviour. This is the strongest way to show a cause, but the setting is narrow and artificial. The programmer lab is this kind.

**Field or quasi-experimental comparisons.** Real settings with comparison groups, but the researchers did not assign participants at random. The endoscopist study is this kind. It shows what happens in a real clinic, and it is weaker than a controlled test.

**Cross-sectional surveys and interviews.** A snapshot of what people report and do. The Lee and Gerlich surveys are this kind. They can show that two things go together. They cannot show which one causes the other.

**Self-reported behaviour.** People describing their own thinking. Useful, but people overstate their own skill and understate their dependence on a tool. The speedup illusion is a reminder that what people feel is not always what is true.

**Conceptual extrapolation.** An argument from mechanisms to a large outcome. Gradual disempowerment is this kind. It is worth taking seriously, and no study in this unit measured it.

When you read a claim, ask which kind of study it comes from. That one question tells you how much weight the claim can carry.

\## What this means for the unit
Put the studies side by side and the picture is mixed. Law students improved when they used AI early. Endoscopists' detection fell after the AI arrived. Surveys link trust in the AI to less reported thinking, and surveys cannot prove cause. A philosophical argument connects everyday offloading to lost influence, and no study measures that connection.

Mixed evidence still has a message. It points to a difference: AI helps in some settings and harms in others. The difference depends on the task, the person, the feedback, and the chance to check the result.

The skeptical case you read points the same way. It limits the claim to specific patterns of use, not to AI in general.

#### Question
content::
\## Your own takeaway
Write down three things:
1. What the evidence in this unit supports now.
2. What is plausible but not yet proven.
3. What single result would most change your mind.

assessment-instructions:: The student has worked through the unit's evidence and is now writing their own conclusion. Encourage a balanced answer: not a cheer, not a dismissal. This is one written answer, then brief tutor feedback (limit to two tutor turns).

Key points to check they grasped:
- Controlled experiments, field comparisons, surveys, self-reports, and conceptual extrapolation each support different claims
- Lee and Gerlich are associations from self-reports, not proof of cause
- Budzyń is an objective field outcome but observational, not a controlled test
- The unit's studies are short, narrow, and small; long-term effects are unknown
- Mixed evidence is consistent with a conditional message: the effect depends on the situation

What to do: acknowledge the three-part answer; if they blur association and causation, name it; if they demand certainty the field lacks, offer what would change their mind; if they overclaim a blanket verdict, return them to the conditions. Two tutor turns max, then a short close.

What not to do: run a long socratic sequence; grade agreement; over-validate.
