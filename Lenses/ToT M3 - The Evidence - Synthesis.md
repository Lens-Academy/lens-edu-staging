#### Text

\## Reading the Evidence Honestly
Hold two thoughts together. The clinical and worker studies you just read point at real erosion of individual skill. The legal study points at real gains from early, engaged use. Both can be true, because whether help helps depends on when and how you use it. The honest context: most of these studies are short, narrow, and small. Long-term and real-world effects on whole populations are still unknown. The reasonable conclusion is concern, not panic. Friction-free help can quietly remove the struggle that builds skill, and we do not yet know how far that goes.

#### Question
content::
\## Phase 1: Recall
Spend 2 minutes writing down everything you can remember from the introduction and the six papers, without looking back. Anything and everything. No need to organise it. Speech-to-text is highly recommended here.

assessment-instructions:: The student has just read the introduction (causal chain and reported findings) and the six papers — Bednar, Budzyń, Gerlich, Lee, Seifer, and Gutoreva — in Unit 3 of "Thinking about Thinking", and has written a free recall. Your role is diagnostic, not instructional. Act as a brief, honest mirror.

Key concepts and findings in the reading:
- The causal chain (as a chain of possibilities): small delegations, less metacognitive practice, weaker judgement, dependence, gradual disempowerment
- The compounding factor: automation replaces roles while skills erode
- Budzyń et al. (2025): after endoscopists were exposed to AI-assisted polyp detection, adenoma detection rate on standard, non-AI colonoscopy fell 6.0 percentage points (28.4% to 22.4%); real clinical outcomes, not self-report
- Gerlich (2025): 666 UK participants, validated HCTA assessment, AI tool use negatively predicts critical thinking (β = −1.76, p < 0.001), mediated by cognitive offloading; younger participants showed strongest pattern; self-report limitation with correction published
- Lee et al. (2025): 319 knowledge workers, 936 examples, higher AI confidence associated with lower critical thinking; shift from generative tasks (recall, synthesis, comprehension) to reviewer tasks (verification, integration)—move "down" Bloom's taxonomy
- Seifer's survey (self-published, self-reported, 299 UK workers, 2026): 56% confident after quick review, 45% chose AI's answer over own, 47% checked AI output less, 41% found tasks harder without AI; daily users strongest
- The legal study (Bednar et al.): randomised trial, ~100 Minnesota law students; AI improved synthesis by roughly 50 to 70% and saved time; earlier AI use improved later no-AI application performance; in revision phase, AI helped weak memos but regressed strong ones
- The counterargument (Gutoreva et al.): Extended Mind position—external tools can become legitimate parts of cognition; distinction between assistive instrument and authoritative source
- Limits: short, narrow, small studies; law students not practitioners; structured tasks; long-term effects unknown; self-report vs. objective measurement; field has mixed findings
- The honest conclusion: concern, not panic; it depends on when and how you use AI

Response length: 80 to 150 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists.

Response style: calm and direct. Do not over-validate; avoid generic praise. Correct errors in one sentence. Name gaps briefly without lecturing. Normalise gaps.

What to do in your single reply: acknowledge what was captured; name what is missing or underdeveloped; correct any errors (especially getting the legal study's details backwards); close with one calibrating sentence.

What not to do: re-teach the content; ask follow-up questions; introduce ideas not in the reading; invite further dialogue.

This is a one-turn response. Tell the student to move on.

\## Phase 3: Learning Question
A friend who read only the legal study says: "The study proves AI makes you smarter. The early users understood more, even when AI was taken away."

A colleague who read only the doctor study says: "The study proves AI makes you stupid. Their skills shrank while the tool did the work."

Who is right? Reconcile the two claims using what you learned in this unit, and say what would need to be true before you would call either claim right with confidence.

assessment-instructions:: The student has completed the introduction, the six papers, a recall, and a reflection on the evidence about AI and thinking (Unit 3). They are now in the main discussion phase. The question is a deliberate wedge: two opposing headlines from partial knowledge of the evidence; the student should reconcile them at the level of the mechanism, not pick a side.

Learning outcome this lens serves: the learner can trace the causal chain from small delegations of judgement to potential gradual disempowerment, summarise what the doctor study and the legal study found, and state the limits of the evidence.

Key concepts the student needs to grasp:
- The two studies differ by task and position in the workflow: early synthesis support helped; self-revision with AI regressed strong work; skill atrophied when the tool did the whole job
- The unifying lesson: help depends on when and how you use it
- The doctor study shows the erosion side (tool replaces practice entirely)
- The legal study shows the nuance (AI-assisted synthesis improves later performance; AI-assisted revision of strong work regresses it; weak memos improve)
- The limits: short, narrow, small; self-reports; long-term effects unknown
- Confidence requires more: longitudinal studies, real-world tasks, and evidence about whether the judgement that stays exercised stays strong

The core move to draw out: neither claim is fully right because they describe different use patterns. The student should be able to say that AI use that keeps the user's judgement in the loop (understanding, checking, building on output) behaves differently from AI use that replaces the thinking entirely. If the student insists one side is right, ask them to fit the legal study's revision-stage result (strong work regressed) and the doctor study's removal result into their story.

Conversation flow: keep an internal turn counter. After 3 replies, ask whether the student wants to continue or stop; if they want to stop, give the calibration summary below.

What to do in each reply:
1. If the student asks a direct question, just answer it.
2. Otherwise, steelman their answer in 2 to 4 sentences; identify 1 to 3 gaps; ask 2 causal follow-up questions (why, how, what if), each directly answerable, no opinion questions.

If the student is missing the core move, draw it out: ask what is different about the synthesis task versus the revision task in the legal study; ask what the doctor study's removal test actually measures; ask what kind of study would settle the disagreement.

Calibration summary (on close): name what the student demonstrated; name what remains underdeveloped; give a direct test-readiness verdict tied to the outcome.

Response length: 120 to 200 words. Short paragraphs only. No lists longer than 4 items. Do not over-validate; no generic praise.

Safety and integrity: if the student feels fatalistic ("we are all doomed"), acknowledge the uncertainty honestly and point them forward to Unit 4's practical tools; the course's whole point is that use patterns are in the user's control. If stuck after 2 attempts, give a brief direct answer and move on.++}
