#### Text
content::
This school takes meditation techniques out of Buddhism and teaches them without asking you to believe anything about rebirth, gods or karma. It has many voices. This module looks at four: Jon Kabat-Zinn, who brought mindfulness into hospitals, Sam Harris, who teaches it to sceptics, and two teachers of detailed methods, Shinzen Young and Daniel Ingram. Culadasa's stage model is a fifth, shorter lens.

Kabat-Zinn defines **mindfulness** as "awareness that arises through paying attention, on purpose, in the present moment, non-judgementally", and sometimes adds "in the service of self-understanding and wisdom" ([Mindful.org, 2017](https://www.mindful.org/jon-kabat-zinn-defining-mindfulness/)). A review of the field calls his definition "one of the most thoughtful and frequently invoked" ([Van Dam et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5758421/)). In 1979 he created **Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)**, an eight-week group course in mindfulness practice, at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. This is what the original course asks of you today, and what it says it offers.

Sam Harris is a writer with a doctorate in cognitive neuroscience ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Harris)) who, as he says below, spent two years on silent retreats in his twenties. He recommends **vipassana** ("insight" meditation from Theravada Buddhism) because it can be taught without any religious belief. He makes the case for a secular practice and gives his own instructions.

#### Text
content::
Both are also businesses. The UMass course costs $650 (above). Harris recorded his meditations for his app Waking Up, which costs $129.99 a year and offers free places to people who cannot pay ([App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/waking-up-meditation-wisdom/id1307736395)). Its introductory course is 28 short guided sessions ([ixcoach review](https://www.ixcoach.com/learn/waking-up-app-review-2026)). Neither fact makes the claims wrong. It is one more thing to weigh when someone tells you their method works.

#### Question: Open
id:: a4b1a3a0-004b-4a46-91e5-1898266bdd4d
content::
The UMass page and Harris promise different things. Which kind of happiness does each one aim at? Use the kinds you met earlier in the course (for example a pleasant feeling, life satisfaction, equanimity, sukha, freedom from worry).
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner read the UMass MBSR page (8-week course, daily home practice of 45 to 60 minutes; it says MBSR can help manage stress and anxiety, respond to difficulties instead of reacting automatically, live with greater calm, clarity, energy and enthusiasm, change one's relationship with pain) and Sam Harris's 'How to Meditate' (goal: awaken from the trance of discursive thinking and from grasping at the pleasant and recoiling from the unpleasant, so as to enjoy a mind undisturbed by worry, open like the sky, effortlessly aware of the present). Earlier in the course they learned to tell apart kinds of happiness: positive feeling or affect, life satisfaction, eudaimonia, sukha (an enduring calm trait), ataraxia, equanimity, contentment, fundamental wellbeing.

A strong answer says that MBSR mainly promises less stress and reactivity and better coping, which is closer to reduced negative affect and better functioning than to a new kind of happiness, while Harris aims at a lasting change in how experience is held: freedom from compulsive thinking and from grasping and aversion, close to equanimity or sukha. Credit any reasonable mapping. If they say both promise the same thing, point to Harris's 'grasping at the pleasant and recoiling from the unpleasant'. Reply in 70 to 120 words. No generic praise. One turn, then send them on.

#### Question: Open
id:: 5b7bb447-99a8-42f3-becd-e03f89e9b773
content::
Which of the two promises would you find easier to test on yourself, and how would you notice if it came true?
force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: The learner compared two promises: MBSR (UMass page: manage stress and anxiety, respond instead of reacting automatically, live with greater calm, change one's relationship with pain) and Sam Harris (awaken from the trance of discursive thinking and from grasping and aversion, to enjoy a mind undisturbed by worry). They now say which is easier to test and how they would notice it. MBSR's promise is easier to measure with standard questionnaires or daily ratings of stress and anxiety. Harris's promise needs first-person observation over time, for example how often one notices being lost in thought, or how long worry lasts. Credit either choice if the reason fits. If their way of noticing is vague ('I'd feel better'), ask for one concrete sign they could record daily. Reply in 60 to 110 words. No generic praise. One turn.
