title: "Twelve Virtues of Rationality"
tldr: Yudkowsky lists twelve virtues of good thinking, from curiosity to scholarship. Behind them all is a nameless one. Every step of your reasoning must aim at the correct answer, not at doing things the proper way.
summary_for_tutor: Yudkowsky describes twelve virtues. Curiosity (a burning itch to know), relinquishment (do not flinch from experiences that might destroy your beliefs), lightness (surrender to the evidence quickly), evenness (do not place heavy burdens of proof only on claims you dislike, or inspect only some arguments for flaws), argument (seek honest argument and a test that lets reality judge), empiricism (ask which experiences to anticipate), simplicity (each added detail is another chance to be wrong), humility (take specific actions in anticipation of your errors), perfectionism (correct errors to see new ones), precision (narrow predictions are more useful), scholarship (study many sciences), and the nameless virtue, the void (every step must cut through to the correct answer, and it is futile to protest that you acted with propriety). The question tests evenness. Lena accepts a study she likes on its summary, hunts for flaws only in the one she dislikes, and calls this skepticism.

#### Question: Open
id:: 23317470-7c5e-4121-a716-fca6fe016272
content::
Lena is a teacher. She reads two new studies about a teaching method she uses. One finds that the method works, and she accepts it after reading the summary. The other finds that it doesn't work, so she spends an evening searching that study for flaws, finds one, and dismisses it. She says: "I'm just being skeptical, and skepticism is good."

1. Using the essay, explain what is wrong with her approach.
2. What should she do instead?
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Under the virtue of evenness, the essay warns against placing heavy burdens of proof only on claims you dislike and then defending yourself with "it is good to be skeptical". Someone who wishes to believe asks "Does the evidence permit me to believe?", while someone who wishes to disbelieve asks "Does the evidence force me to believe?". If you are selective about which arguments you inspect for flaws, or how hard you inspect them, every flaw you learn to detect makes you more wrong, and if you attend only to favourable evidence, the more data you gather, the less you know. You should judge the hypotheses, not argue for one side.
60: Step 1. Says her skepticism is one-sided: she demands much more of the evidence she dislikes than of the evidence she likes, so it is not real skepticism, and it leaves her more confident in what she already believed rather than more accurate.
40: Step 2. Says she should apply the same standard to both studies, checking the supporting study as hard as the critical one, and weigh both fairly rather than argue for her side.
