#### Text

\## Before you read

The previous lens argued about a national AGI project, and both sides of that argument
stood on the same floor: the United States and China are racing to build advanced AI, and
whoever slows down loses. Almost every strategy conversation you will overhear leans on
some version of this. This lens does not ask whether racing would be wise. It asks
whether the premise holds, with the tools Unit 2 gave you: where does the number come
from, and who checked the original source?

Two readings. The first is evidence forensics. Karson Elmgren traces a widely repeated
claim, that China declared a goal of building AGI back in 2017, to its source: one phrase
in one planning document, and a translation. The second is a short data insight from
Epoch AI that measures the US-China capability gap instead of narrating it. Its title
gives its answer away, so commit to your own number before you scroll to the readings.

Two stretches of the Elmgren piece render collapsed: a section the author himself marked
optional and some supplementary evidence. Both stay one click away if the detective work
hooks you. And if you finish wanting the view from inside Chinese policy, the imported
Carnegie piece [[../articles/sheehan-how-china-views-ai-risks-and-what-to-do-about-them]]
shows what Chinese AI policy actually looks like read directly.

*The framing and questions on this page were written by Claude, an AI, and reviewed by a human. The readings themselves are the authors' own work.*

#### Question
content::
\## Commit to a number

Before you open either reading: how many months behind the US frontier are Chinese AI
models today? Write the number down.

Then write one more line saying where your number comes from. A benchmark you saw, a
headline, a model you tried, the general sense of the discourse. Name the source
honestly. A wrong number you can defend beats no number at all.

assessment-instructions::
CONTEXT YOU NEED. You do not have the rest of this course, so here is the situation.

This is the fifth lens of Unit 4 in a course about AI futures, written for newcomers.
The unit dissects rival strategies for preventing AI catastrophe. The previous lens
argued for and against a US national AGI project; this one checks the race premise
underneath that argument. The student has NOT read the articles yet. Two are attached,
and both are in your context: Karson Elmgren's "No, the 2017 New Generation AI
Development Plan did not include a goal of building AGI" and a short Epoch AI data
insight by Luke Emberson on the US-China capability gap.

The student was asked to commit to a number, how many months Chinese models trail the US
frontier, and to write one line naming where the number comes from. The purpose is that
they meet the measurement with a forecast of their own already on record, and practice
asking where a repeated claim comes from. The purpose is not for them to guess right.
Do not deliver the readings' answer.

WHAT THE READINGS SAY. For your reference only. Do not deliver it.
- Emberson reports that since 2023 every model at the frontier of Epoch's Capabilities
  Index has been developed in the United States, and that Chinese models have trailed by
  a specific average gap, measured in months, within a stated range. Do NOT reveal the
  figure or the range, and do not hint whether the student's number is high or low.
- Elmgren argues that the common claim rests on one ambiguous phrase, "data-driven
  general-purpose AI mathematical models and theory," buried in a basic-research
  sub-list of the 2017 plan, not in its Strategic Objectives. The Chinese term tongyong
  rengongzhineng usually means general-purpose AI, not AGI, and the follow-up
  implementation documents he could check never use it. He does NOT claim China has no AGI ambitions
  today; he says the mid-2020s are a separate debate, and he concedes irreducible
  uncertainty about what the drafters intended.

YOUR TASK, in order.
1. Take the number as a forecast and probe its SOURCE, not its size. Name, in the
   student's own terms, what kind of ground it stands on: a measurement, a headline,
   personal use of the models, a vibe from the discourse.
2. Ask ONE question: what would they check to move their number up or down?
3. If they gave no number, ask for one before anything else; a wrong defensible number
   is the exercise. If they named a specific source, ask what that source actually
   measures.

Do not grade. Do not score. Do not praise. Never reveal any figure from the readings,
and never say whether their number is close. They are about to see the measurement.

Write 60 to 110 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
