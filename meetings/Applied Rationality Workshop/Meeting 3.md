### Room 2: What is it for?  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Goal factoring takes one action apart into the goals it serves, then looks for a cheaper way to get them all.

1. One volunteer names something costly they do or are deciding about (small is fine). No volunteer? Use "checking the news every morning". Examples:
    - a weekly meeting
    - a stay-or-quit choice
    - a daily habit
2. The others ask "What is it for?" until the list of goals stops growing. Include how the volunteer wants to be seen.
3. Button test: "Would you press a button that gave you all of these without doing the thing?" Hesitation points to a missing goal. Add it.
4. Brainstorm wild alternatives one goal at a time, then combine them into one plan. Keeping the action counts.

If you still have time: swap volunteers.

### Room 3: "I just hate it"  [0:52–1:10]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Aversion factoring splits something you avoid into parts. You may mind only one or two. The handbook's running example:
- shoes and clothes: fine
- aching knees: very bad
- wind in your face: great
- people watching: bad

2. Split it into five or more parts, including tiny snags (the floss sits in a drawer). Its owner gives each part a short verdict.
3. For the worst part, everyone votes: fix the situation, change the reaction, or keep the aversion because it protects against a real cost?
4. Take the most popular option. What would it cost in money, time, attention or awkwardness?

If you still have time: is that a good deal, or is keeping the aversion cheaper?

## Participant FAQ

*Quick answers for the breakout-room meeting. Everything you actually do is in the session doc.*

### 🆘 Getting help during a breakout

Your navigator isn't in every room, so:

- **Confused about the task, or want a human?** Click **"••• More” > “Ask for Help"** > Your navigator gets pinged and pops into your room.
- **Don't get the question, or something in the reading?** Ask [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor). It's for understanding, not for handing you the answer.
- **Lost the doc link, or a quick logistics thing?** Check the **Discord channel** or the Zoom chat.

### Before you arrive

- **I didn't finish (or start) the reading, should I come?** Yes. Come anyway and just say so in Room 1; it's normal, and there's no penalty.
- **I'm running late.** Join whenever; you'll be dropped into a breakout and someone will catch you up.

### In your breakout

- **Do I have to talk, or have my camera on?** Cameras-on helps everyone connect, but do what you're comfortable with. Jump in when you've got a thought, there’s no need to wait to be called on.
- **What's a "scribe"?** One person per group jots the names and your answer into the table. A line or two is plenty; rotate it each room if you like.
- **We finished early / ran out of things to say.** Call your navigator to get ideas for expanding your current conversation. Or use the spare minute to agree what you would say if someone from your group shares between rooms.
- **No one's talking, or one person is dominating.** Just start talking when you have a thought; if it's really stuck, click Ask for Help.
- **Do we need a "right answer"?** No! The point is the discussion, not a tidy answer.

### Between rooms

- **What happens between rooms?** Everyone comes back to the main room for five minutes. Your navigator asks who wants to share what their group landed on, and waits. A sentence or two is plenty.
- **Do I have to share?** No. Nobody is made to speak, and a silent pause is a normal outcome. If your navigator invites you by name and you would rather not, “pass” is a complete answer.
- **I cannot remember what we decided.** Read the last line your scribe wrote in your room’s table.

### The reading and the questions

- **I don't understand the question or a claim.** Ask [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor)! It'll explain in plain terms.
- **Where's the reading?** It's in the course on the Lens platform: the chapter text is loaded right in the lesson. Can't find it? Ask the Tutor or Ask for Help.

### Tech

- **I can't find the doc, or I can't type in it.** The link is in the Zoom chat and Discord; open it signed in with your enrolled account. Still stuck? Ask for Help.
- **My audio keeps cutting out.** Flag it in the chat, and leave/rejoin the room if it doesn't clear.

### After we wrap

- **What happens at 1:30?** We close on time. The meeting then stays open; hang around and keep talking if you'd like, no pressure.
- **How do accountability buddies work?** Buddy groups are formed in the first meeting as trios or pairs. For the rest of the course, you check in with each other before each meeting. Swap whatever the last room asked you to take away: a plan, a next step, or simply what changed your mind. If your accountability buddies are no longer taking the course, ask your navigator to place you with a new group.

## Glossary

### Module 3: Motivation

Goal factoring: Taking an action apart into the goals it serves, then looking for a cheaper way to get all of those goals. Keeping the original action is one possible result.
LEGO principle: The handbook's name for two ideas: things are made of parts, and parts can be exchanged for each other.
Button test: Imagining a button that gives you all the goals you listed without the action. Any hesitation to press it points to a goal missing from the list.
Prepare to accept all worlds: Before goal factoring, being willing to accept any answer, including keeping the action or dropping it.
Five-second version: A tiny everyday use of a technique that takes a few seconds, such as asking "What is this for?" before a meeting.
Aversion factoring: Splitting an activity you avoid into its parts, so you can fix the parts about the situation and recalibrate the reactions you would rather not have.
Mindful walkthrough: Paying close attention, moment by moment, while doing an activity, to find what makes it unpleasant.
Yum factor: Something about an activity that appeals to you or that you value.
Trivial inconvenience: A small practical snag that keeps you from doing something, such as floss kept in a drawer you never open.
Recalibration: Changing your reaction rather than the situation, through small voluntary exposure or a reframe you believe.
Currency: Anything you spend or gain in a trade, such as time, money, attention, energy, sleep or goodwill.
Repeated cost: A small cost paid again and again, which adds up to a large one over months or years.
Diminishing returns: Each extra unit of effort gives less than the one before.
Value of information: How much better you expect things to go with the information you are seeking, weighing how much difference it could make against how likely it is to make it.
Arbitrage: Moving resources from where you value them cheaply to where you value them highly, so that you end up better off overall.

## Doing this meeting alone

No group? You can do this meeting on your own in 45 to 60 minutes, with [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor) as your partner. Open it in a new tab and paste in the text under each step.

### 1. Check-in (5 minutes, no tutor)

Write two or three sentences for yourself: what you tried in the experiment from the last unit, and what happened on the problem it was meant to help. If it slipped, did the plan not come to mind, or did it come to mind and you didn't want to do it? Write one change you will make.

### 2. What is it for? (15 to 20 minutes)

Pick something you do, or are deciding about, that costs you a lot. Use a different bug from the one in the lens. Paste this into Lens Tutor:

> Be my partner for goal factoring, the CFAR technique. Do not solve my problem and do not lecture. Ask me one question at a time. First ask what action I want to factor. Then ask "What is it for?" until my list of goals stops growing, including how I want to be seen and how I see myself. Then run a button test: would I press a button that gives me all these goals without the action? Treat any hesitation as a missing goal. Then help me brainstorm alternatives one goal at a time, and combine them into a plan I can test. Remind me that keeping the action is an allowed result. End with a quick check of what would stop me following the plan.

### 3. "I just hate it" (15 to 20 minutes)

Pick something you avoid but think is worth doing, again a different bug from the one in the lens. Paste this into Lens Tutor:

> Be my partner for aversion factoring, the CFAR technique. Do not solve my problem. Ask me one question at a time and do not lecture. If it turns out to be a strong fear, suggest I switch to a smaller problem instead of trying exposure. First ask what I avoid and what appeals to me about it. Then help me split the aversion into specific parts, each with a one-word verdict, including tiny practical snags. Check whether I have found everything. For the worst part, ask me whether to fix the situation, change my reaction, or keep the aversion because it protects me from a real cost. Then ask what the fix costs in money, time, attention or awkwardness, and whether that is a good deal.

### 4. Stress-test your experiment (10 minutes)

Paste this into Lens Tutor, followed by the experiment you planned at the end of this unit:

> Here is a small experiment I plan to run in my life. Imagine it is a few days later and it didn't happen. Tell me the most likely reason in one or two sentences, then ask me what one change to my plan would stop that reason. Keep going for at most three rounds. Then ask me what is most likely to stop me finishing the next unit.

### Close (2 minutes)

Write one sentence: which technique from this unit you will keep using, and which you would drop.
