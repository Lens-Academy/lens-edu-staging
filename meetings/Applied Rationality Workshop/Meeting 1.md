### Room 2: Hamming circle, part 1: understand it first  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**

### Per-room notes for Unit 1

**General, all rooms.** This is a workshop, not a reading discussion: the rooms work on people's own problems, using the bugs list and the Hamming question participants wrote this unit. Everything personal is opt-in. Nobody has to share anything, and "I'll bring a small bug" is always a full answer. Never push anyone to go deeper, and do not give your own advice on anyone's problem. Many people will not have finished the unit: every room works from the doc alone, since everyone has bugs and problems whether or not they wrote them down.
**Room 1, Getting to know each other.** Keep introductions to about thirty seconds each. Ask 3 is deliberately light: one harmless bug, so people see that everyone has them. If someone has no list, they can name any small daily annoyance.
**Back together after Room 1: introduce the Hamming circle (about 2 minutes).** In a calm, slow voice: (1) Richard Hamming asked scientists what the most important problems in their field were, and then why they were not working on them. A Hamming circle asks the same of your own life, and the group gives one person its full attention at a time. (2) The agreement: what is said in a room stays in that room, people share only what they choose, and a smaller bug is always fine. (3) Invite a short quiet moment (about 30 seconds, eyes closed if they like) to think of what they want to bring. Then open Room 2. Background: the CFAR handbook's version gives each person at least 20 minutes, in groups of about four, and was held late in a workshop so people already knew each other. Today's 5-minute turns among near-strangers are a first taste, so keep the tone gentle and light. The handbook also says that in these circles understanding what is going on usually helps more than jumping to solutions, which is why Room 2 is questions only.
**Room 2, understand it first.** Groups of 3 work best here (about 5 minutes each). When you drop in, listen for advice-giving and gently redirect: "Questions only in this room. What would help you understand it better?" Good questions ask for a concrete example, what makes it sticky, or what would change if it were solved. If someone becomes upset, slow things down, let them choose whether to continue, and check in with them privately afterwards. If anyone seems in real crisis, take it out of the room and point them to appropriate support.
**Back together after Rooms 2 and 3.** Ask people to share how the format felt or what they noticed, never another person's problem. Silence is fine.
**Room 3, threads to pull.** The difference from Room 2: now the group offers, the focus person chooses. Listen for offers that turn into instructions ("you should...") and reframe them as options. The focus person can decline every thread, and that is a fine outcome.
**Room 4, Buddies and your experiment.** In the whole-group slot before Room 4, explain accountability buddies (about a minute): (1) the people in your Room 4 become your accountability buddies for the course (split groups of four or more), (2) swap contact details, (3) before each meeting, check in with each other: did you finish the unit, are you coming? Ask buddies to message each other today, so both know the channel works. Insist on real contact details in the table. Ask 2 is the unit's technique in its five-second form: the surprise rating is Murphyjitsu's "surprise-o-meter". If someone has no experiment yet, the group helps them pick one small thing to try this week and rates that.

## Doing this meeting alone

If you are taking this course without a group, do this instead: a 45 to 60 minute session with [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor), which plays the circle for you. Have your bugs list and your Hamming question answer from this unit open. Rooms 2, 3 and 4 become three short sessions. Room 1 is for meeting people, so skip it.

### Part 1: Understand it first (15 to 20 min)

Open Lens Tutor and paste this:

> I'm doing the Applied Rationality Workshop alone, and this is my version of a Hamming circle. My most important problem right now, and what keeps me from working on it, is: [write it here, or a smaller bug if you prefer]. For the next 15 minutes, only ask me questions that help me understand what is going on: ask for concrete examples, what makes it sticky, and what would change if it were solved. One or two questions at a time. No advice and no solutions. At the end, ask me what looks clearer now.

### Part 2: Threads to pull (15 min)

In the same chat, paste this:

> Now switch roles. Offer me threads to pull, one at a time: a question I may not have asked myself, a connection you noticed in what I said, or one small step I could take this week. These are offers, not instructions. After three or four, ask me which one thread I want to keep, and stop.

Write the thread you keep somewhere you will see it this week.

### Part 3: Your experiment (10 min)

Paste this, with the experiment from your "Plan your week" page:

> This week I plan to [your experiment], on [day or trigger]. Ask me how surprised I would be, from 1 (not surprised) to 10 (shocked), if next week I report that it didn't happen. If I say less than 7, ask me for the most likely reason, and help me find one change that would head it off. Don't choose the experiment for me.

Then tell one person in your life what you plan to try, or put a reminder in your calendar for the day after, to check whether it happened.

## Glossary

### Module: Opening

Applied rationality: Practical techniques for thinking more clearly about your own life and then acting on what you conclude.
Bug: Anything in your life that does not work the way you would like, from small daily annoyances to large tangled problems.
Bugs list: Your numbered list of bugs and feature requests, written this unit and used in every later unit to pick a problem for the week's technique.
Hamming question: What is the most important problem in your life right now, and what keeps you from working on it? Named after the mathematician Richard Hamming, who asked scientists this about their fields.
Pair debugging: Two people working through one person's problem together, one asking questions while the other does the thinking. In this course the AI tutor often plays the partner.
Eat the instructions: Try a technique exactly as written first, then adapt it until it fits you.
Feature request: Something in your life that is not broken but could be great: an opportunity, a new habit, a new ability.
Hamming circle: A small group that gives one person at a time its full attention while they work on their Hamming question.
Murphyjitsu: Imagine your plan has failed. If that would not surprise you, find the most likely reason, fix it, and check again until failure would surprise you.
Inner simulator: The intuitive part of your mind that predicts what happens next, based on everything you have experienced.
Next action: The first concrete step that sets a plan in motion, often as mundane as putting something in the calendar.
Surprise-o-meter: Asking yourself how surprised you would be by an outcome, for example on a scale from 1 to 10.
Pre-hindsight: Imagining that a plan has already failed and asking what went wrong.
