Internal Double Crux, from this unit: treat "I should, but I don't" as two sides of you, each partly right.

- "I should reply to that email today" vs. putting it off

## Doing this meeting alone

If you are taking the course on your own, you can do this meeting as a 45 to 60 minute session with [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor), which plays your partner. Copy each prompt below into the tutor and fill in the parts in brackets. You choose what to bring, and you can stop at any point.

### Part 1: What happened last time (5 min)

You already wrote your report on the first page of this unit. Read it again and add one sentence: what would you tell a friend about what the experiment showed?

### Part 2: What else would it mean? (15 min)

This replaces Room 2. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please act as my partner for a short exercise on bucket errors from the CFAR Handbook: sometimes a small piece of news feels unbearable because we have tied it to a bigger question, a hidden link of the form "if X, then Y". Case: Sam posts his first blog post, a reader comments "The second section is confusing", Sam replies that the reader skimmed it and doesn't open the draft again. Ask me to guess Sam's hidden link, then offer two other guesses of your own to compare. Then ask me to split Sam's bucket into separate questions and say what evidence would answer each. Ask one thing at a time and keep your replies short. If I bring in an example of my own and it touches something heavy, slow down, don't dig, and let me stop.

Write down the guess that seemed most likely to you.

### Part 3: Moderate a "should" (15 to 20 min)

This replaces Room 3. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please help me practise Internal Double Crux from the CFAR Handbook on a low-stakes should: [pick one: "I should read more papers" vs. wanting the evening off, "I should reply to that email today" vs. putting it off, or a made-up one]. I will voice both sides, starting each line with the side's name. You moderate. First check that both names are ones each side would choose for itself. Then keep it fair: each turn first says one true thing in the other side's last point, then adds one new point. Point it out when a side skips the true thing or makes two points. Never take a side and never argue for one. After six turns, ask me whether one side simply won, and whether its concern was answered or overruled. If it touches something heavy, slow down, don't dig, and let me stop.

### Part 4: When exactly? (5 min)

Put this unit's experiment in your calendar with a day and a time, or write down the trigger you will watch for. Set a reminder for the day after, to write down what happened. If there is anything likely to stop you finishing the next unit, write down your plan to prevent it.

### Per-room notes for Unit 5

**General, all rooms.** This unit is inner work: Focusing (listening to vague bodily feelings), Bucket Errors, Againstness (stress and the body) and Internal Double Crux. That is why no room asks anyone to work on a personal feeling in front of others, and Rooms 2 and 3 use made-up or shared cases. Focusing stays private on purpose. Everything personal is opt-in, "pass" is always allowed, and a smaller or made-up example always works. The course is not therapy and neither is this meeting. If someone shares something heavy: thank them, do not dig or give advice, and offer to move on. If someone seems in real distress, check in with them privately after the meeting and point them to someone they trust, a doctor or therapist, or [Find A Helpline](https://findahelpline.com) in a crisis. The course judges a technique by whether it changed anything on a real problem, and "this technique does not fit me" is a fine conclusion when someone has reasons. Call two minutes before each room ends.
**Room 1, What happened last time.** The experiment was planned at the end of the last unit: for most people a comfort zone experiment, a small, safe try of something the person feels a bit blocked from, and for some a Turbocharging or Taste & Shaping experiment. In the handbook's framing, "the hesitation was protecting me" is a success, not a failure. Never praise anyone as brave. If an experiment did not happen, ask what got in the way at the exact moment it should have.
**Room 2, What else would it mean?** For your own use: the CFAR Handbook's Bucket Errors chapter says that when several questions share one mental bucket, evidence about one (X) seems to force a conclusion about another (Y), so the person flinches away from X. The handbook treats the flinch as protective and the hidden link as the error. Fixes it suggests: ask what would be bad about taking X in, check how strongly X really implies Y, notice that taking in X rarely forces an action. Sam's case is made up and low-stakes on purpose. Likely links: "then I'm a bad writer", "then I shouldn't write in public", "then I wasted the weekend". Different guesses are the point of ask 1, so take them all. Ask 3 connects to the handbook's view that the flinch protects the person, so loosening the link helps more than pushing the evidence. Ask 4 is optional and personal. Do not steer anyone toward sharing.
**Room 3, Moderate a "should" together.** For your own use: the handbook rates Internal Double Crux "Preliminary/tentative". CFAR stopped teaching it at its main workshops shortly before it paused workshops in 2020, and left it out of its 2025 workshops ([Salamon 2025](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Ww6Mv8bkMCMcdKDRm/more-details-on-cfar-s-new-workshops), footnote 5). Anna Salamon, a CFAR co-founder, wrote in 2022 that in practice it often worked as a tool for people to convince themselves of things they had an ulterior motive for ([Salamon 2022](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/B9kP6x5rpmuCzpfWb/comment-reply-my-low-quality-thoughts-on-why-cfar-didn-t-get)). The lens teaches it with that warning, and ask 3 is the room's point: a side that was overruled rather than answered is a warning sign, while a real dissolution is a good outcome. The example labels take sides on purpose ("putting it off", "staying on the couch"), so renaming them is the first exercise. That is why the room uses a shared example, not anyone's own should. Rules: names each side would choose for itself, one point per turn, acknowledge a true thing before objecting.
**Room 4, When exactly?** Push for a day and time or a clear trigger, not "this week". If someone's plan aims an inner-work technique at something heavy, suggest a lighter target. Ask 3 is optional.

## Glossary

### Module: Inner work

Focusing: Eugene Gendlin's technique, in CFAR's version. You pay attention to a vague feeling, try out words for it, and check whether they fit.
Felt sense: A bodily or vague sensation that carries information you have not put into words yet, like a sinking feeling when you think about your schedule.
Handle: A word, phrase or image for a felt sense, like a title for it.
Resonance: Whether a handle fits the felt sense. In Focusing you hold the words up against the feeling and check.
Going meta: Asking how you feel about a feeling, to get some distance from it when it is too big.
Bucket error: Several questions stored in one mental bucket, so that evidence about one seems to settle another. The handbook writes it as a hidden "if X, then Y".
Flinch: Pulling away from a piece of information. The handbook says the flinch protects you, and the error is the hidden link behind it.
Question substitution: Answering an easier question in place of the one you meant to ask, without noticing.
Againstness: Making worse decisions under stress while feeling sure you are thinking clearly, and the handbook's body-based routine against it.
Sympathetic nervous system (SNS): The body's accelerator: fight, flight or freeze, and also excitement.
Parasympathetic nervous system (PSNS): The body's brakes: rest, relaxation and calm.
Metacognitive blind spot: A flaw in your thinking that you cannot find by looking inward, because the flaw affects the looking.
Internal Double Crux (IDC): A technique for an inner conflict. Two sides of you take turns, each first acknowledging something true in the other's point and then adding one point. The handbook rates it preliminary, and CFAR no longer teaches it.
Should: In IDC, an urge or pressure to do something other than what you are doing by default.
Moderator: The part of you that runs an IDC and keeps it fair, without taking a side.
