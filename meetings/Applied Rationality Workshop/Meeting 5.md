### Room 2: Which question are you really answering?  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Question substitution, from this unit's Bucket Errors page: we often answer an easier question in place of the one we meant to ask, without noticing. Examples from the CFAR Handbook:
- "Is my plan likely to succeed?" becomes "Can I imagine it succeeding?"
- "Did my partner do something wrong?" becomes "Am I mad at my partner?"
- "What's my best next move?" becomes "What can I do with the tools I already have?"

1\. One person names a question they have been trying to answer lately, at work, in study or in life. Low stakes is fine, for example "Should I learn this tool first?"
2\. The others each guess one easier question that might be getting answered in its place.
3\. The person says which guess fits, if any, and what would actually answer the real question.
4\. Swap, so that everyone gets a turn. About 4 minutes each.

If a question is unclear, ask your navigator or copy it into [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor).

### Room 3: Moderate a "should" together  [0:52–1:10]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Internal Double Crux, from this unit: treat "I should, but I keep not doing it" as a disagreement between two sides of you, each with part of the truth. A moderator keeps it fair. Each turn first says one true thing in the other side's last point, then adds one new point.

Use a shared example, not anyone's own. Pick one:
- "I should read more papers" vs. wanting the evening off
- "I should reply to that email today" vs. putting it off
- "I should go to the gym" vs. staying on the couch

1\. Two people each voice one side. One person moderates. Give each side a name it would choose for itself.
2\. Run six turns. The moderator stops any turn that skips the true thing or makes two points.
3\. Stop. Did one side simply win? If so, was its point better, or was it the side the group expected to win?
4\. If you still have time: swap roles and run it again with the other side going first.

If a question is unclear, ask your navigator or copy it into [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor).

## Doing this meeting alone

If you are taking the course on your own, you can do this meeting as a 45 to 60 minute session with [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor), which plays your partner. Copy each prompt below into the tutor and fill in the parts in brackets. You choose what to bring, and you can stop at any point.

### Part 1: What happened last time (5 min)

You already wrote your report on the first page of this unit. Read it again and add one sentence: what would you tell a friend about what the experiment showed?

### Part 2: Which question are you really answering? (15 min)

This replaces Room 2. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please act as my partner for a short exercise on question substitution from the CFAR Handbook: we often answer an easier question in place of the one we meant to ask, for example "Can I imagine my plan succeeding?" in place of "Is my plan likely to succeed?". The question I have been trying to answer lately is: [your question]. Suggest three easier questions I might be answering in its place, then ask me which one fits, if any, and what would actually answer my real question. Ask one thing at a time and keep your replies short. Do not answer my question for me.

Write down the substitute that fit, if any.

### Part 3: Moderate a "should" (15 to 20 min)

This replaces Room 3. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please help me practise Internal Double Crux from the CFAR Handbook on a low-stakes should: [your should, for example "I should read more papers" vs. wanting the evening off]. You voice one side and I voice the other. Give your side a name it would choose for itself, and I'll do the same. Each turn, first say one true thing in the other side's last point, then add one new point. Point it out when I skip the true thing or make two points. After six turns, stop and ask me whether one side simply won, and whether it was the side I expected to win. Keep your turns short.

Then swap sides and run it once more.

### Part 4: When exactly? (5 min)

Put your experiment for this week in your calendar with a day and a time, or write down the trigger you will watch for. Set a reminder for the day after, to write down what happened. If there is anything likely to stop you finishing the next unit, write down your plan to prevent it.

### Per-room notes for Unit 5

**General, all rooms.** This unit is inner work: Focusing (listening to vague bodily feelings), Bucket Errors, Againstness (stress and the body) and Internal Double Crux. That is why no room asks anyone to work on a personal feeling in front of others. Focusing stays private on purpose. Everything personal is opt-in, "pass" is always allowed, and a smaller or made-up example always works. The course is not therapy and neither is this meeting. If someone shares something heavy: thank them, do not dig or give advice, and offer to move on. If someone seems in real distress, check in with them privately after the meeting and point them to someone they trust, a doctor or therapist, or [Find A Helpline](https://findahelpline.com) in a crisis. The course judges a technique by whether it changed anything on a real problem, and "this technique does not fit me" is a fine conclusion when someone has reasons. Call two minutes before each room ends.
**Room 1, What happened last time.** The experiment was planned at the end of the last unit: a comfort zone experiment, a small, safe try of something the person feels a bit blocked from. In the handbook's framing, "the hesitation was protecting me" is a success, not a failure. Never praise anyone as brave. If an experiment did not happen, ask what got in the way at the exact moment it should have.
**Room 2, Which question are you really answering?** For your own use: the CFAR Handbook's Bucket Errors chapter describes question substitution (from Kahneman's work, the handbook says) as swapping a hard question for an easier one without noticing. Its examples include "Do I love this person?" becoming "Does this person make me happy?", and "Should I buy this item at this price?" becoming "How unpleasant is imagining buying it later at a higher price?". The substitute is usually relevant but not the same question. The room deliberately stays with question substitution, not the self-worth side of bucket errors (when news about one small thing seems to settle a big question about yourself), which is more personal. If someone goes there by choice, let them, but do not steer there. The person whose question it is decides which guess fits, and "none" is allowed.
**Room 3, Moderate a "should" together.** For your own use: the handbook rates Internal Double Crux "Preliminary/tentative". CFAR stopped teaching it at its main workshops shortly before 2020 and left it out of its 2025 workshops. Anna Salamon (CFAR co-founder) wrote in 2022 that in practice it often worked as a tool for people to convince themselves of things they had an ulterior motive for. The lens teaches it with that warning, and ask 3 is the room's point: a quick win for the side people expected to win is a warning sign. That is why the room uses a shared example, not anyone's own should. Rules: names each side would choose for itself, one point per turn, acknowledge a true thing before objecting.
**Room 4, When exactly?** Push for a day and time or a clear trigger, not "this week". If someone's plan aims an inner-work technique at something heavy, suggest a lighter target. Ask 3 is optional.

## Glossary

### Module: Inner work

Focusing: Eugene Gendlin's technique, in CFAR's version. You pay attention to a vague feeling, try out words for it, and check whether they fit.
Felt sense: A bodily or vague sensation that carries information you have not put into words yet, like a sinking feeling when you think about your schedule.
Handle: A word, phrase or image for a felt sense, like a title for it.
Resonance: Whether a handle fits the felt sense. In Focusing you hold the words up against the feeling and check.
Going meta: Asking how you feel about a feeling, to get some distance from it when it is too big.
Bucket error: Several questions stored in one mental bucket, so that evidence about one seems to settle another. The handbook writes it as a hidden "if X, then Y".
Flinch: Pulling away from a piece of information. The handbook says the flinch protects you, and the error is the hidden link behind it.
Question substitution: Answering an easier question in place of the one you meant to ask, without noticing.
Againstness: Making worse decisions under stress while feeling sure you are thinking clearly, and the handbook's body-based routine against it.
Sympathetic nervous system (SNS): The body's accelerator: fight, flight or freeze, and also excitement.
Parasympathetic nervous system (PSNS): The body's brakes: rest, relaxation and calm.
Metacognitive blind spot: A flaw in your thinking that you cannot find by looking inward, because the flaw affects the looking.
Internal Double Crux (IDC): A technique for an inner conflict. Two sides of you take turns, each first acknowledging something true in the other's point and then adding one point. The handbook rates it preliminary, and CFAR no longer teaches it.
Should: In IDC, an urge or pressure to do something other than what you are doing by default.
Moderator: The part of you that runs an IDC and keeps it fair, without taking a side.
