### Room 2: Practice that looks like the real thing  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Turbocharging, from this unit, in one line: you get good at what you actually rehearse.

1\. One person names a skill they want to improve (in research, work or anything else) and describes, step by step, how they practise it now.
2\. The others ask, one at a time:
    - How does your practice start, compared with the real situation?
    - What do you do in practice, compared with what you will do for real?
    - Does your practice ever vary how it starts or what you do?
3\. Each of the others suggests one change. The person picks one, or none, and says why.
4\. Swap until everyone has had a turn, about 4 minutes each.

If a question is unclear, ask your navigator or copy it into [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor).

### Room 3: Check each other's comfort zone experiment  [0:52–1:10]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


A Comfort Zone Exploration is a small, safe try of something you feel a bit blocked from, to learn whether the hesitation protects you or is out of date.

Share only what you want to. A made-up example works too.

1\. Each person says their planned experiment in one sentence.
2\. The group asks the person, one at a time:
    - Would both futures feel okay: one where you still don't do this, and one where you feel free to?
    - What might the hesitation be protecting?
    - Is the worst realistic outcome unpleasant, or actually dangerous?
    - What will you pay attention to while you do it?
3\. Each person says whether and how they will change their plan. Making it smaller counts.

## Doing this meeting alone

If you are taking the course on your own, you can do this meeting as a 45 to 60 minute session with [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor), which plays your partner. Copy each prompt below into the tutor and fill in the parts in brackets.

### Part 1: What happened last time (5 min)

You already wrote your report on the first page of this unit. Read it again and add one sentence: what would you tell a friend about how it went?

### Part 2: Practice that looks like the real thing (15 to 20 min)

This replaces Room 2. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please act as my partner for a short practice check based on CFAR's Turbocharging idea: you get good at what you actually rehearse, so practice should start the way the real situation starts and make me do what I will really have to do. The skill I want to improve is [skill]. This is how I practise it now: [your practice, step by step]. Ask me one question at a time: how the real situation starts compared with how my practice starts, what I do in practice compared with what I will need to do for real, and whether my practice varies either one. Then suggest three possible changes and let me pick one, or none. Do not lecture, and keep your replies short.

Write down the change you picked.

### Part 3: Check your comfort zone experiment (15 to 20 min)

This replaces Room 3. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please act as my partner checking a Comfort Zone Exploration (CoZE) experiment from the CFAR Handbook: a small, safe try of something I feel a bit blocked from, to find out whether the hesitation is protecting me or is out of date. This is my plan: [your plan]. Ask me one question at a time. Would both outcomes feel okay to me, doing it and finding out I'd rather not? What is the worst realistic outcome? What will I pay attention to, in myself and around me? How will I digest it afterwards? Do not push me toward a bigger or braver experiment. Treat "the hesitation is right" as a valid result.

If you want, run a one-minute version of your experiment straight away and tell the tutor how it felt compared with what you expected.

### Part 4: When exactly? (5 min)

Put your experiment in your calendar with a day and a time. Set a reminder for the day after, to write down what happened. If there is anything likely to stop you finishing the next unit, write down your plan to prevent it.

### Per-room notes for Unit 4

**General, all rooms.** This course is a workshop: people work on their own real problems. Everything personal is opt-in. Nobody has to share a bug, a fear or an experiment, and "a smaller or made-up example" is always allowed. Never praise someone as brave or push for a bigger experiment. The course judges a technique by whether it changed anything on a real problem, not by whether people liked it, and it treats "this technique does not fit me" as a fine conclusion when someone has reasons. Call two minutes before each room ends.
**Room 1, What happened last time.** The experiment was planned at the end of the last unit with a motivation technique (Goal Factoring, Aversion Factoring or Units of Exchange). "It didn't happen" is data, not failure: if a group gets stuck on it, ask what got in the way at the exact moment it should have happened. Ask 4, for your own use: the Taste & Shaping chapter's example is a junior executive who thinks "Stupid!" each time he spots a mistake. The chapter says the punishment lands on the noticing, so he learns to shy away from looking for mistakes, not to make fewer. Take answers without correcting them.
**Room 2, Practice that looks like the real thing.** For your own use: the CFAR Handbook's Turbocharging chapter asks four questions. How close is the practice trigger to the real-world trigger? Where they differ, does the practice vary the trigger? The same two for the action. Its examples are a police officer who reflexively hands a disarmed weapon back because they always did so in practice, and a French student with a high GPA who cannot make up even simple new sentences without pausing to think. The chapter says practice that does not resemble the skill is fine when it serves a stated purpose (squats to build strength for climbing), and that Turbocharging is for skills you do, not facts you know. If a group only critiques, push for ask 3. The person whose skill it is chooses, and "none" is allowed.
**Room 3, Check each other's comfort zone experiment.** For your own use: CoZE steps are 1 choose something with a "yum", 2 accept all worlds (both futures must feel okay, otherwise stop), 3 design a small, safe experiment, 4 try it while paying attention inside and outside, 5 digest it without forcing. The handbook separates CoZE from exposure therapy with Chesterton's Fence: the hesitation may be in the right place. A group that makes plans smaller, or concludes a fence should stay, is succeeding. Do not allow dares. The second check (what the hesitation might be protecting) is the Chesterton's Fence question: a fence that turns out to protect something is a good result. If someone shares something heavy, thank them and offer to move on. The course's view is that CoZE is not a treatment for anxiety, and that a fear which disrupts someone's life belongs with a doctor or therapist.
**Room 4, When exactly?** Push for a day and time, not "this week". Anyone without a plan picks one now: a smaller version of what they discussed in Room 3 is fine. The Plan page in the unit also allowed a Turbocharging or Taste & Shaping experiment instead. Ask 3 asks for a fallback, so that a missed day becomes a smaller try rather than nothing. Ask 4 is optional.

## Glossary

### Module: Learning and trying

Turbocharging: The CFAR Handbook's model of practice. People get better at what they actually practise, so a practice method is judged by how closely it resembles the real skill.
Practice trigger: What starts the behaviour during practice. Turbocharging compares it with what starts the behaviour in real life.
Practice action: What you actually do during practice, compared with what you will need to do in the real situation.
Declarative knowledge: Explicit information you can explain in words or pictures, like knowing that Paris is the capital of France.
Procedural knowledge: Know-how you show by doing, like riding a bicycle. The handbook says Turbocharging is for this kind of learning.
Yuck and yum: CFAR's casual names for the quick positive or negative feeling that objects, actions and experiences carry, their emotional valence.
Shaping: Training a behaviour step by step by rewarding behaviour that gets closer to the target, like teaching a dog to step on one particular tile.
Hyperbolic discounting: The delay between a behaviour and its consequence has a disproportionately large effect, so small, immediate rewards or punishments shape behaviour strongly.
Taste: An experienced person's fast gut sense of what will pan out. The handbook says to evaluate it by whether the experience behind it is rich, representative and relevant to the case.
Goodhart's Law: "Any measure which becomes a target ceases to be a good measure." Duncan Sabien's short version: proxies are leaky.
Proxy: Something tracked in place of the thing you care about, like the number on a scale in place of health.
Comfort Zone Exploration (CoZE): Small, safe experiments at the edge of your comfort zone, to gather data about whether a hesitation is helpful or not.
Explore/exploit problem: The trade-off between spending resources on known goods and searching for new possibilities.
Chesterton's Fence: The lesson not to tear down a barrier until you understand why it was put up.
Exposure therapy: Gradually more intense exposure to something, to reduce anxiety about it. The handbook contrasts it with CoZE because it works whether or not removing the aversion is a good idea.
Accept all worlds: The CoZE step of checking that both futures, doing the thing and not doing it, feel okay before you experiment.
