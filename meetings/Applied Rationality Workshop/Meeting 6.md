### Room 2: Double Crux, live  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Double Crux in one line: instead of trading conclusions, two people look together for the one belief underneath that would change both of their minds. Winning is not the goal. An if-then statement you both accept is.

1. Find a disagreement that two of you actually have. Examples:
    - Should someone new to AI safety spend their first year on technical skills before anything else?
    - Is it better to work on a frontier AI lab's safety team or outside the labs?
    - Do most people still use a technique from a course like this a year later?
    - Anything else two of you disagree on and care about.
2. Those two take the sides. Each says their view and how sure they are, as a percentage.
3. Each gives their reasons. After each one, the others ask: "If that turned out false, would you change your mind?" The reasons that get a yes are your cruxes.
4. Look for one statement that would change both minds. Write it as an if-then you both accept.

If a question is unclear, ask your navigator or copy it into [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor).

## Doing this meeting alone

If you are taking the course on your own, you can do this meeting as a 45 to 60 minute session with [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor), which plays your partner. Copy each prompt below into the tutor and fill in the parts in brackets. Two parts work even better with a real person: a friend you disagree with, or a friend you teach. Keep an eye on the clock yourself: the tutor cannot.

### Part 1: Looking back (5 min)

Reread your answers on the "Looking back" page of this unit. Add one sentence: what would you tell a friend about the course as a whole?

### Part 2: Double Crux (15 to 20 min)

This replaces Room 2. If you already practised with Jordan on the Double Crux page, pick a different disagreement. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please play someone who disagrees with me, so I can practise CFAR's Double Crux: instead of trading conclusions, we look together for the one belief underneath that would change both our minds. My view: [your view and how sure you are]. Take the other side sincerely, with two reasons that would not change your mind and one real crux that would. Only reveal what would change your mind when I ask. If I argue to win, argue back. Stop when we have an if-then statement we both accept, and then tell me what your real crux was.

Write down the if-then statement.

### Part 3: Tutoring wheel (15 to 20 min)

This replaces Room 3. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please play a curious beginner who has never heard of applied rationality. I will teach you one CFAR technique in about four minutes: [technique]. Try it on a small problem of your own, and misunderstand one step in a way a smart beginner plausibly would. Only explain your thinking if I ask what you are thinking. If I just correct you, make the same mistake again in a new form. When I say "done", tell me what your misunderstanding was and whether my questions found it.

Write down what the beginner understood differently than you expected.

### Part 4: What you keep doing (10 min)

This replaces Room 4. Copy this into Lens Tutor:

> Please act as a Hamming circle for me: give me your full attention on my most important problem, ask questions, and offer threads to pull, not instructions. My most important problem now is: [problem, or a smaller one]. The practice I will keep after this course is: [practice], starting with [first step] on [date]. Ask me one or two questions about it, then offer me two or three threads to pull, and ask which one I keep. Then ask what is most likely to stop me keeping the practice.

Put the first step and your monthly review in your calendar now.

### Per-room notes for Unit 6

**General, all rooms.** This is the last meeting. The course is a workshop: people work on their own real problems, and everything personal is opt-in. Nobody has to share a bug, a disagreement or a problem, and a smaller or made-up example is always allowed. The course judges a technique by whether it changed anything on a real problem, not by whether people liked it, and it treats "this technique does not fit me" as a fine conclusion when someone has reasons. Never use AI risk as a reason someone must keep practising. Call two minutes before each room ends.
**Room 1, Looking back.** The unit's last pages asked people to reread the bugs list and Hamming question from the first unit and say what changed in their life. "Nothing changed" is an honest answer. Do not reassure people out of it. Ask 4 is the course's own test from the other side: a technique worth dropping is useful information.
**Room 2, Double Crux, live.** For your own use: the CFAR Handbook labels Double Crux "Preliminary/tentative". A crux is a belief that would change your conclusion if it turned out false. A double crux is one statement both people disagree about that would change either one's mind. The victory condition is a shared if-then statement, not resolving the disagreement. Steps: find a disagreement, make it concrete, look for cruxes, check the if-then against your gut, repeat. If a group slides into debate, ask the arguing person: "If that turned out false, would you change your mind?" In groups of 4, two people can observe, or two pairs can work in parallel. Steer away from heated politics or personal conflicts within the group. Groups that run out of time without an if-then have still done the exercise.
**Room 3, Tutoring wheel.** In groups of 3, about 5 minutes per teacher, in groups of 4 about 4. The unit's page on Seeking PCK (pedagogical content knowledge, knowing how beginners misunderstand a topic) prepared each person to teach one technique and to get curious before correcting. CFAR ran this as the "tutoring wheel": everyone picked a technique to teach and the group rotated. If a teacher lectures for the whole turn, ask them to stop and have the listeners try one step. Two people may teach the same technique.
**Room 4, Last Hamming circle.** The first meeting's Hamming circle asked what each person's most important problem was. This one asks what they will keep doing about it. The handbook's full Hamming circle gives each person at least 20 minutes among people who trust each other, so 3 minutes is only a short version. Keep it gentle, and stop any advice that turns into instructions ("you should..."). The handbook's guide says CFAR staff and participants found a Hamming circle every 6 to 18 months valuable: if a group wants to run a full one later, point them to the guide in the Library module. Ask 4 is optional.
**Wrap-up.** The Library module is optional. People set their monthly review date on the last page of this unit. If someone did not, ask them to put one in their calendar before they leave.

## Glossary

### Module: Other people, and wrapping up

Double Crux: The CFAR Handbook's method for disagreements between people. Instead of trading conclusions, both people look for the belief underneath that would change both their minds.
Good Faith Principle: Unless there is clear evidence otherwise, assume the other person has good motives and reasons that make sense from their experience.
Crux: A belief that supports one of your conclusions and would change it if it turned out false.
A double crux (the statement): A statement two people disagree about that could change either person's mind about the original question.
Operationalize: Make a disagreement concrete: define the words, pick specific test cases, and prefer questions about actions over questions about beliefs.
Resonate: The Double Crux step of trying out both sides and checking the agreed if-then statement against your gut.
Tutoring wheel: A workshop activity where everyone teaches one technique to the others in turn, and is taught by them.
Pedagogical content knowledge (PCK): Knowledge about a topic that is also about how learners meet it, such as common misconceptions and what it is like to be a beginner.
Clinical interviewing: Asking a learner about their thinking out of real curiosity, instead of pointing out their error.
Deliberate performance: Practising a skill inside tasks you already do, by paying attention to how you do them, instead of setting aside separate practice time.
Monthly review: A short, fixed time each month to reread and update your bugs list, check whether your triggers fired, and pick the skill for the next month.
