### Per-room notes for Unit 3

**General, all rooms.** This unit's material is a treaty text and a board of seventeen actors; conversations die when people talk about "the treaty" in general. Ask for the article number, the actor's name, the ring.
**Room 1, Icebreaker.** One summariser per table; if nobody at a table read the draft, give them the one-liner yourself: a treaty text from MIRI that bans large training runs, puts covered chips under international control or disables them, sets up a verification body, and lets a party leave on twelve months' notice. The ask is feelings, not verdicts: relief, alarm, disbelief, boredom are all answers, and "which part did that" is what makes it an icebreaker rather than a debate. If a table starts arguing whether the treaty is realistic, park it: Room 2 attacks the text clause by clause.
**Room 2, Find the hole.** Expect a spread: the anatomy assignment asked for any three of four questions on MIRI's draft, all optional, and roughly half the room will not have finished the unit; the five-line summary in the prompt is all a table needs. For your own use, the sources: thresholds and training limits are Article IV, chip consolidation Article V (16 H100-equivalents, consolidation within 120 days), chip production Article VI, chip-use verification Article VII, research bans Article VIII and their verification Article IX, the Coalition Technical Body Article III, withdrawal Article XV (XV(3) makes the notice period supervised disarmament, and Article XII protective actions still apply after exit). Traps: cheating "the treaty" in general (ask which line, which clause, and how, step by step) and cheats with no clock (ask how many months it needs, and whether it beats the twelve-month notice period). For ask 3, push past "it costs money": who loses sovereignty, who leaks secrets to inspectors, and which signatory would walk rather than accept it. Good material to fish for: distributed training on many sub-16-chip clusters, algorithmic progress under the research ban, definitional carve-outs, which fail before inspection clauses do. Anyone who did the optional Anatomy Drill can test their negotiating-agenda pick against the table's cheat; there is no single right answer.
**Room 3, The smallest coalition.** The trap is listing everyone to be safe; push for smallest, and for the mechanism behind each pick (what does it control). Expect two camps: convince the firms (ASML, TSMC, NVIDIA, the clouds) or convince the governments that host them (US, China, Netherlands, Taiwan, South Korea, Japan). Ask 1 is where that difference comes out: a firm can be persuaded, a government can compel, and the governments hosting the chokepoints are exactly the ones the two-party treaty does not include, which is the actors lesson's open problem. Ask 2 is the point of the room: with China opposed, the set becomes the whole chip supply chain plus intelligence, and even then a domestic programme on smuggled chips is only slowed; with ASML or TSMC opposed, the lesson's own answer applies: the removal that bites soonest is the cloud providers (a run stops this week), the removal that matters most is ASML or TSMC (the next chip generation stops), and a coalition without them buys nothing this year. Keep leverage and evidence apart: being in the coalition is not the same as being able to check anyone. California is on the list because its reporting law already binds the leading labs; a table that picks it has to say what it compels.
**Room 4, Next unit and feedback.** Ask 1 is there so the room has something to talk about besides logistics; one sentence each, no answering. The Context Distiller is a 75-minute exercise on one report; ask people to pick their report now so Room 1 of the next meeting has something to open on.

## Glossary

### Module: Anatomy of a (pause) agreement

Structure of a treaty: Title and preamble, main body, final clauses (signature, ratification, accession, entry into force, withdrawal), and annexes, which count as an integral part of the treaty.
Vienna Convention interpretation: The order of interpretative methods under Articles 31 to 33: ordinary meaning, context, then the treaty's object and purpose; preparatory works are supplementary.
Binding vs non-binding provisions: The point where preambular language (recognizing, affirming, should) turns into an operative obligation on a named party (shall, shall not, may not). Focus on the verbs, not the headings.
Chip use verification vs research restriction verification: MIRI's draft locates the two methods differently (Articles VII and IX), the comparison the anatomy assignment asks for.
Exit clause: How a party withdraws, always read against a clock: if withdrawal takes ninety days and a covert sprint takes sixty, the exit is a hole through every other organ. MIRI's draft gives twelve months' notice, turns that period into supervised disarmament, and has no entry-into-force clause.
Seven organs: What every binding agreement has: the rule, the claims, the evidence, the confidentiality bargain, the adversary, the institution and consequences, and the gap. Advocacy text has the silhouette of anatomy and none of the organs.
Grandfather clause: A definition that exempts pre-existing activity, such as continued development of models trained before entry into force. Definitional clauses fail before inspection clauses do.

### Module: Who the treaty relies on, applies to, and constrains

Wednesday morning: The actors lesson's test: when two governments sign a pause, the people who must change behaviour are labs, fabs, clouds, and one Dutch lithography firm, not the signatories.
Incentive vocabulary: Comply, defect, hide, exaggerate, free-ride. Postures, not personality types: the same actor can comply on one obligation, hide on another, and free-ride on a third in the same quarter.
High-bandwidth memory (HBM): The stacked memory beside every frontier accelerator, made by three firms in two countries; a second countable chokepoint inside the US export-control perimeter.
Six functional roles: Capability holder, chokepoint controller, information holder, enforcement authority, evasion pathway, and victim or free-rider or beneficiary. Any actor can hold several at once, and almost every important one does.
Three questions for any actor: Where does it sit on the chain (position), what can it do inside a regime (roles), what does it want right now (posture).
Four rings: Declares, holds the evidence, verifies, outside the declaration. Four actors on the board owe a declaration (the two signatories, cloud providers, frontier labs); the third-party verifier slot on the Verifies ring is empty.
The seventeen actors: United States, China, Taiwan, Netherlands, Japan, South Korea, California; Commerce (BIS), the intelligence community; the AI verification body that does not exist; ASML, TSMC, NVIDIA, the cloud providers, the frontier labs, proxies (front companies, resellers, straw buyers), deployers.
Prover and Verifier (Baker et al.): The party that declares facts about its compute, which in an international agreement is a government constraining firms in its territory, and the government body or third party that checks the declarations are correct and complete.
Leverage vs evidence: A geographic chokepoint is potential leverage, not verification. ASML, the tightest chokepoint, completes no subgoal, because Baker's chain of custody begins at manufacturing.
Four subgoals (Baker et al.): 1.A declared uses are accurate; 1.B declared uses have the required properties; 2.A no undeclared use of a declared cluster; 2.B no undeclared clusters at all.
Evidence edge: An arrow from A to B when A can put a fact about B in front of a verifier that B did not have to volunteer. Not influence, not dependence: evidence.
The seven edges (XLab's key): Cloud providers to Frontier labs (1.A); NVIDIA to Frontier labs (1.B); NVIDIA to Cloud providers (2.A); TSMC to Proxies, NVIDIA to Proxies, Intelligence community to China, and Intelligence community to Proxies (all 2.B).
Verification layer: One mechanism per subgoal; layers can be stacked for redundancy. The on-chip layer is a chip designer's to give or withhold.
Weak link: The subgoal whose mechanisms are collectively least robust; its robustness determines the regime's overall robustness. On the board, three of four subgoals rest on a single edge each.
