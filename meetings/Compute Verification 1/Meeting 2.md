### Per-room notes for Unit 2

**General, all rooms.** Collect everyone's answer before any explanation. The unit was dense (three documents, five tasks, an eleven-bucket sort) and roughly half the room will not have finished it; every prompt is written to work from the doc alone, so do not re-teach, pair the prepared with the unprepared in conversation.
**Room 1, Icebreaker.** The cost card is a one-minute optional exercise; anyone who skipped it can do it on the spot. It is personal by design; keep it light. If someone found it "almost instant", ask which half of the card they practise saying out loud; if "genuinely hard", ask on whom outside their view the cost lands.
**Room 2, Borrow a checking system.** Keep tables on a system they actually know; the earlier version of this room stalled when tables picked a system nobody at the table understood. Ask 2 is the unit's four-step move in plain clothes (what the checker sees, what that lets it conclude, the assumption that makes the conclusion valid, what it cannot see); push for the assumption if a table skips it (for your own use, for doping: the banned substance leaves a trace the athlete cannot avoid; for audits: the books are complete). Watch the clock so every table reaches ask 3; that is the point of the room. Baker's line for ask 3: AI has no obvious analogue to environmental sampling. Good transferable principles to fish for: passive monitoring that does not need the inspected party's cooperation; corroboration across independent sensors; setting the threshold where the sensors can see, as the Limited Test Ban Treaty did.
**Room 3, Aim for the halt, or take the deal?** Both sides argue about AI verification today; the 1963 treaty is only the model for the second side, so if a table starts debating nuclear testing, pull them back. Assign sides if nobody volunteers; the take-the-deal side is the harder brief, so give it the stronger talkers. Its material: the 1963 treaty covered exactly the environments national technical means could police and left out the one they could not; halt-grade intrusiveness can poison transparency deals that are tractable now (the Baruch Plan died because neither side would accept intrusive control before trusting the other); Goodhart erodes any fixed compute line. XLab's side: mechanisms strong enough for a halt (chip registries, compute metering, inspection rights) support every weaker policy, and what gets signed first can still be transparency. For ask 2, anyone who wrote the stakeholder map can use it; accept any named actor with a reason.
**Room 4, Next unit and feedback.** Ask 1 is there so the room has something to talk about besides logistics; one sentence each, no answering. Unit 3 is the longest remaining unit (210 minutes core). Get a concrete plan, not a promise. The Anatomy Drill in Unit 3 is optional; do not sell it as required.

## Glossary

### Module: History, precedents, parallels

Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement (CSA): A state accepts IAEA safeguards on all nuclear material in all peaceful nuclear activities within its territory, for the exclusive purpose of verifying that such material is not diverted to weapons. Baker's gloss: CSAs verify peaceful use at known facilities rather than detecting secret ones.
Shavit's three components: Chip-level activity logging, inspection and analysis of the logs of a sufficient subset of chips, and supply-chain monitoring. Each solves a distinct problem; removing any one opens a distinct route for evasion.
Declared vs undeclared: Moving from "all declared material is accounted for" to "no weapons programme" supplies a premise the evidence does not: that the declaration is complete. Iraq exploited exactly this gap.
Environmental sampling: Detecting the unique particles a nuclear process scatters; the technique that made small-scale clandestine activity such as plutonium separation detectable after Iraq. Baker: AI has no obvious analogue.
Leads: Detecting undeclared activity at undeclared sites depends on knowing where to look; wider access rights are of limited value without leads, which mostly come from states' own collection.
Verified accountancy of a chokepoint: The correct nuclear-to-AI analogy: not "chips are like uranium" but the accounting of a scarce, controlled input. It supports claims about covered chips at declared locations, nothing more.
Additional Protocol: The IAEA's post-Iraq fix, widening the claim it can test to cover undeclared sites and activities; it required ratification by each state.

### Module: What kind of policy are we trying to verify?

Goal, legal rule, verification claim: The three layers of a policy. The goal (prevent premature ASI) is abstract; the rule is the obligation written into the treaty; the claim is the proposition someone can check with evidence.
Four ingredients: Actors (who the claim is about), objects (chips, clusters, models), activities (acquisition, training, transfer), and conditions (thresholds, locations, time periods) that make a claim precise.
Goodhart's Law: When a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure. A lab can train under a compute threshold and still reach dangerous capabilities through algorithmic efficiency gains.
Five candidate agreement types (Oxford AIGI): Transfer knowledge, transfer resources, pool resources, prepare for emergencies, regulate. Each buys a different amount of risk reduction at a different price.
Compute vs capability thresholds: A compute line (FLOP) is black-and-white, measurable before a run, and externally verifiable, but underinclusive as algorithms improve; a capability line measures danger more directly but relies on unreliable evals, measured after the model exists. Pause-style agreements favour compute.
Overinclusive / underinclusive: A threshold that wastes resources regulating harmless models, or one that fails to cover dangerous ones.
Effectiveness vs feasibility: Effectiveness is how much a policy deters ASI development, from symbolic to decisive; feasibility is how tractable it is under current infrastructure and politics, from off the table to already happening. A policy that fails either axis is bad.
The cost card: A one-line policy audit, "I support X at the cost of Y", where Y is a real cost of enforcing X: who pays, who is constrained, what enforcing it requires, what happens to those who refuse.
Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963): Banned tests in the atmosphere, in space and underwater but not underground, because atmospheric tests could be detected worldwide without inspection and underground ones could not be told from earthquakes. Verifiability decided what could be banned.
The eleven policy buckets: Self-governance, unilateral restraint, uncoordinated domestic regulation, transparency and information-sharing, joint emergency preparedness, knowledge and benefit transfers, compute controls, binding international regulation, nonproliferation regime, international joint development, coordinated halt. Each carries a historical parallel, from Asilomar to the CTBT.
Securitization: Treating an issue as an existential security matter, lifting it out of normal balancing because nothing can be traded against survival. Under it, the coordinated halt becomes the design target for verification.
Design for the hardest case: Verification strong enough to support a full pause supports every weaker bucket for free; the reverse is not true.
Stakeholder map: For a hypothetical pause treaty, every relevant actor placed on the supply chain with its incentive class and leverage, and the two or three whose defection would collapse the regime.

### Module: Strategic foundations (optional)

Tying hands vs sinking costs (Fearon): Two ways to make a foreign-policy signal credible: create future audience costs for backing down, or pay costs up front.
Two-level game (Putnam): Governments bargain internationally while securing domestic ratification; the set of agreements that can pass at home is the win-set.
Agency slack (Hawkins et al.): Principal-agent theory explains why states delegate authority to international organizations and how delegation can enhance credibility; agency slack is where the delegated body drifts from what the states intended.
Weaponized interdependence (Farrell and Newman): States exploit control over central nodes in global networks through panopticon effects (seeing flows) and chokepoint effects (stopping them).
