## Participant FAQ

*Quick answers for the breakout-room meeting. Everything you actually do is in the session doc.*

### 🆘 Getting help during a breakout

Your navigator isn't in every room, so:

- **Confused about the task, or want a human?** Click **"••• More” > “Ask for Help"** > Your navigator gets pinged and pops into your room.
- **Don't get the question, or something in the reading?** Ask the [Lens Coach](https://lensacademy.org/coach). It's for understanding, not for handing you the answer.
- **Lost the doc link, or a quick logistics thing?** Check the **Discord channel** or the Zoom chat.

### Before you arrive

- **I didn't finish (or start) the reading, should I come?** Yes. Come anyway and just say so in Room 1; it's normal, and there's no penalty.
- **I'm running late.** Join whenever; you'll be dropped into a breakout and someone will catch you up.

### In your breakout

- **Do I have to talk, or have my camera on?** Cameras-on helps everyone connect, but do what you're comfortable with. Jump in when you've got a thought, there’s no need to wait to be called on.
- **What's a "scribe"?** One person per group jots the names and your answer into the table. A line or two is plenty; rotate it each room if you like.
- **We finished early / ran out of things to say.** Call your navigator to get ideas for expanding your current conversation. Or use the spare minute to agree what you would say if someone from your group shares between rooms.
- **No one's talking, or one person is dominating.** Just start talking when you have a thought; if it's really stuck, click Ask for Help.
- **Do we need a "right answer"?** No! The point is the discussion, not a tidy answer.

### Between rooms

- **What happens between rooms?** Everyone comes back to the main room for five minutes. Your navigator asks who wants to share what their group landed on, and waits. A sentence or two is plenty.
- **Do I have to share?** No. Nobody is made to speak, and a silent pause is a normal outcome. If your navigator invites you by name and you would rather not, “pass” is a complete answer.
- **I cannot remember what we decided.** Read the last line your scribe wrote in your room’s table.

### The reading and the questions

- **I don't understand the question or a claim.** Ask the [Lens Coach](https://lensacademy.org/coach)! It'll explain in plain terms.
- **Where's the reading?** It's in the course on the Lens platform: the chapter text is loaded right in the lesson. Can't find it? Ask the Coach or Ask for Help.

### Tech

- **I can't find the doc, or I can't type in it.** The link is in the Zoom chat and Discord; open it signed in with your enrolled account. Still stuck? Ask for Help.
- **My audio keeps cutting out.** Flag it in the chat, and leave/rejoin the room if it doesn't clear.

### After we wrap

- **What happens at 1:30?** We close on time. The meeting then stays open; hang around and keep talking if you'd like, no pressure.
- **How do accountability buddies work?** You paired up in the first session; your buddy checks in with you before each meeting. Swap whatever the last room asked you to take away: a plan, a next step, or simply what changed your mind.

## Navigator Pro-Tips:

**1. Skeptic Unlock:** when a room stalls on **“AI won’t ever get that smart”**


Some rooms flatten when the group agrees AI will never become that capable. The discussion dies because there’s nothing left to argue. When that happens, show the trend:


- Pull up the [METR time-horizons graph](https://metr.org/time-horizons/) and discuss pace of task-length growth.
- A possible line: *“It’s not necessary for it to be smart today. Look at the slope, this is what an exponential looks like, and here’s where a few more doublings land us.”*
- Hand it back to the room: “Given this curve, what would change your mind, in either direction?”


**The goal is not to win an argument**. It is to turn a closed “no” into an open “huh, let’s take a look at this” so the discussion has somewhere to go.



**2. Doom Reframe:** when the material hits hard and **the room turns fatalistic**


Some rooms end heavy. The content genuinely rattles people (participants have talked about getting anxious and doom-scrolling after the reading), and a discussion can settle into “we’re doomed” with nowhere left to go. When that happens, don’t let it end there:


- Name it, then reframe: doom predictions assume today’s tools and limits, but the people working on this in the future will have new capabilities and methods we can’t see yet.
- Reach for the [Malthusian fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malthusianism): everyone predicted mass starvation from population growth, then farming methods leapt ahead and it never happened. A predicted catastrophe is not a guaranteed one.
- A possible line: *“It’s not all lost. The best thing any of us can do is work on it, so let’s not despair.”*
- **Hand it back to agency:** “*Given that, what’s one thing within your reach, a skill, a question, a next step?*”


**The goal is not to talk anyone out of their concern or paper over the risk.** It is to channel that energy into agency so people leave motivated rather than paralyzed.



### Per-room notes for Unit 5

**General, all rooms.** The hardware unit rewards precision; the unit's own method is state the claim, trace the trust chain, name the adversary, price the mechanism, bound the conclusion, find the remaining hole. When a room drifts into "chips can just prove it", ask which of those six steps they skipped.
**Room 1, Icebreaker.** This is the last meeting, so ask 3 is about the whole course, not the unit. Keep it personal and do not let it turn into a debate about whether verification works; that argument belongs in Room 2. Anyone who did not finish the reading can answer both asks from the parts they did read. If a room is flat, offer a candidate: a chip signing a statement about itself sounds decisive until you ask what it actually measured.
**Room 2, Who holds the key?** The two failures are in the prompt; add a third if the room is fast: one signatory refuses to renew the other's tokens. Expect fail-closed to be what a rival demands and what an operator refuses; that tension is the room, so make sure someone speaks for the operator. The lesson's questions to keep in play: can a chip vendor or one state disable lawful activity unilaterally, can emergency suspension happen before adjudication, who bears the cost of false denials and downtime, and what stops the control channel becoming an espionage or coercion channel. If the root key comes up, the lesson's answer key expects identity, attested firmware, protected counter, signed record and licence token to fail together.
**Room 3, Your questions.** The two minutes of silent writing are the room; skip them and the most confident person sets the agenda. Same format as the previous meeting, so say plainly that it is the closing sweep across all five units, not just this one. There is no answer to arrive at, and a table that finishes early can go back to the questions it did not pick.
**Room 4, Your ongoing action.** Same-vote-as-Unit-1 is a reflection, keep it to a sentence each, and expect several people not to remember Plan A; the fallback in the prompt covers them. Then insist on a date for the action. Feedback here is the last verbal primer for XLab's form; the cohort is the calibration group for this re-cut course.

## Glossary

### Module: Hardware verification

The claim ledger: Seven conclusions a lab says 20,000 valid attestation tokens prove, each to be classed as supported, possibly supported if the system was designed to measure it, or unsupported by attestation alone. The course leaves it ungraded on purpose.
Six hardware functions: Identify and account for hardware; attest state and configuration; establish location and topology; measure and classify use; authorize or restrict use; reconstruct a declared run. Not a ladder from weak to strong.
The repeated method: State the claim, trace the measurement and trust chain, name the adversary, price the mechanism, bound the conclusion, find the remaining hole.
Policy goal, legal rule, verification claim (hardware version): Prevent a dangerous training run during a pause; no unlicensed training above threshold T; no covered accelerator performed unlicensed training whose counted operations exceeded T in the reporting period. Hardware tests narrower propositions that support the claim.
Authenticity, correctness, completeness: Did the evidence come from the claimed device and is it fresh; does the activity satisfy the rule; did the evidence cover all devices, sites, periods and activities, including undeclared ones. Cryptography is strongest on the first; completeness usually needs evidence beyond the device.
Attester, Verifier, Relying Party (RFC 9334): The component that produces evidence, the one that appraises it against reference values and policy, and the one that decides what to do with the result. The signature checker need not be the institution that decides compliance.
Root of trust: The component or assumption at the bottom of the chain; every other claim inherits its integrity. A flaw here invalidates many apparently separate controls at once.
NVIDIA GPU attestation: A deployed primitive: evidence from the GPU checked against signed reference measurements and revocation data. Its Blackwell multi-GPU documentation attests each GPU independently and does not attest topology or switches.
What a signature does not establish: That the measurement observed the right property or was complete, that the reference value was correct, that the state was current, that the device stayed compliant, that it sat in the declared location or topology, that no other device existed, or that anyone may lawfully act on the result.
Adversary profile: The same product can be strong against a cloud customer without physical access and weak against a laboratory that controls its servers or a state-backed owner. "Secure" is not a mechanism property without an adversary.
Fleet completeness evidence: The hardest hardware claim is negative: no relevant compute existed outside the declared system. It draws on production, customs and shipping records, provider inventories, physical counts, power and construction evidence, intelligence, and insiders.
Offline licensing: A device performs only a bounded quantity or type of operation while it holds a valid authorization token; a protected meter reduces the allowance and a throttling mechanism responds when it expires. No publicly documented, treaty-grade regime is operating as of August 2026.
Fail open vs fail closed: What the device does when the authorization service is unavailable: keep running, or stop. Part of the distribution of authority, risk and failure a control mechanism imposes.
Common-mode failure: Controls that fall together because they share one root, for example identity, metering, classification and licensing all resting on one vendor key. A signed meter, a signed classifier and a licensing system on one key give less redundancy than their number suggests.
Where trust lives: Four architectures for one verification target: on-chip monitoring, off-chip digital monitoring (taps, gateways), off-chip analog monitoring (power, thermal sensors installed under treaty access), and hybrid assurance. Moving trust does not remove it.
Confidential computing: A deployed security primitive protecting code and data in use from the host under the vendor's stated threat model; not proof of treaty compliance, since a confidential workload can still violate the treaty.
Bilateral pilot: The unit's recommendation shape: choose an architecture for a pilot, not universal deployment, and state the evidence the pilot must produce before the mechanism deserves a larger role.
