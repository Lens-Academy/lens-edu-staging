### Per-room notes for Unit 4

**General, all rooms.** The unit's single idea is provenance: whose claims am I relying on, and who acts on mine. Every room can be rescued by asking one of those two questions about whatever is on the table; Room 2's ask 3 is that question aimed at mechanisms rather than sentences.
**Room 1, Icebreaker.** Ask 3 is a short orientation, half a minute each, not a full summary; if someone starts reciting the report, ask what a reader would have done with it. Two people on the same report is fine: ask each what they clipped and what they left. Surprise is the easier way in for anyone who read the report but never reached the distilling.
**Room 2, The smallest stack.** Ask 2 is the exercise and ask 3 is the payoff, so keep ask 1 short; this room carries more work than the others and is the one to protect if you are running behind. For your own use: expect stacks like attestation plus compute metering plus licensing, which all rest on the chip vendor's root key, or satellite plus power monitoring, which share one physical signature. Three mechanisms that all rest on the inspected party's own say-so are one layer wearing three names, and ask 3 is how a room notices. The ranking in the unit was ungraded and XLab withholds its reference map until the capstone course, so do not supply one.
**Room 3, Your questions.** The two minutes of silent writing are the room; skip them and the most confident person sets the agenda. If a table has nothing written down, half of them did not finish the reading: ask what they would need in order to explain this unit to a colleague. There is no answer to arrive at, and a table that finishes early can go back to the questions it did not pick.
**Room 4, Next unit and feedback.** The hand-off from Room 3 is the conversation here, so the room only carries two asks. Make the claim-ledger instruction explicit: the hardware unit opens with a lab handing you 20,000 valid attestation tokens and seven conclusions; answer the seven before reading further and keep the answers; XLab's curriculum returns to them later in the track.

## Glossary

### Module: Upstream and downstream

Provenance: Where each claim in a report came from, and whether it was verified independently or inherited from the actor being checked. Two documents from the same side agreeing is not verification; it is one actor being consistent with itself.
Upstream and downstream: The sources a report draws on, each produced by an actor with incentives, and the readers who act on it, who will weigh the writer's incentives in turn. No verification document stands alone.
The Fordow case: In January 2023 IAEA swipe samples at Fordow contained particles enriched to 83.7 percent U-235, above Iran's declared 60 percent, and inspectors found two IR-6 cascades configured differently from the declaration. Iran attributed the particles to unintended fluctuations.
Environmental swipe samples: Surface samples sent to the Agency's laboratory, which reads enrichment level directly from uranium particles; collected by the inspectorate, so they check a declaration rather than echo it.
Determination vs raw findings: A treaty body needs a judgement measured against a standard; independent analysts need the discrepancy itself, with dates and specifics, to point their own collection at it. A useful report does both.
The three layers of a compute report: The lab's own declaration, the utilization logs kept by the cloud provider that hosted the run, and physical streams that are harder to fake, such as on-chip attestation, chip location tracking, and measured power draw.
Clip, distil, thread: Pull only the passages that would change what a reader does, rewrite each for a specific reader, and connect each point to the reader who needs it. A fact you never clipped can reach no one.
Decoys and boilerplate: Passages that are true and change no reader's decision: training-data notices, mandate recitals, methods arithmetic, and vivid findings that answer none of the readers' questions. Load-bearing is relative to audience.
Upstream dependence: What the labs granted bounds what an evaluator can say; the UK AISI tests checkpoints the labs share, sometimes pre-release or with different safeguards. Congress reads a system card and contributes nothing to it, so it is downstream.

### Module: What makes a verification mechanism effective?

Costly vs cheap signals: Robust mechanisms force an evader to attack several independent streams; weak mechanisms produce results that must be corroborated by independent sources.
Four metrics: Technical feasibility (can it be built and run at scale today), political feasibility (would the parties whose cooperation is required adopt and enforce it), verification effectiveness (how strong and broad the evidence is), and durability (how fast it decays from technical progress, adversary adaptation, or political change).
The twelve mechanisms: Chip identity and remote attestation, on-chip compute metering, hardware licensing, proof-of-learning, cloud KYC, cloud workload monitoring, satellite and infrastructure monitoring, chip supply-chain tracking, national intelligence sharing, whistleblower channels, on-site and challenge inspections, and privacy-preserving proofs.
Swiss-cheese model: Combine layers whose holes do not line up, relying on different information, different actors, and different access assumptions, so that one failure does not defeat the rest. Layers can look independent while sharing the same upstream firms.
Five evidence taxonomies: Twelve evidence streams (a slightly different set from the ranking list) sorted by layer (who controls the sensor), by access (what the target must concede), by goal (which claim it checks), by lifecycle (when it attaches), and by adversary (who it is still good against). After deployment holds none of the twelve.
National technical means: Evidence a state can collect without the target's cooperation: satellite imagery, power-draw monitoring at grid scale, signals intelligence, open-source intelligence, whistleblowers.
Verifier's paradox: How to gain enough access to confirm compliance but not enough to enable espionage. Privacy-preserving verification conveys minimum excess information to the verifier.
Remote attestation: A chip uses device-bound credentials to report its identity and measured state; the verifier receives a limited signed report, not the workload or weights. Already available on some AI hardware.
Managed access: Cleared inspectors receive limited access under agreed rules; under the Chemical Weapons Convention an inspected state may shroud sensitive equipment or offer alternative evidence.
