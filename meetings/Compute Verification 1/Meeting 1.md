### Per-room notes for Unit 1

**General, all rooms.** Rooms 2 to 4 ran long in every first-cohort group: call "two minutes" before each room ends and move people on time, because Room 4 needs its full slot for the buddy pairing. Expect silence after a prompt; count to ten before rescuing it. Collect everyone's answer before any explanation, because the first explanation anchors the rest. Some participants already work in governance or security: push them toward specifics (which mechanism, which actor, which year), not positions.
**Room 1, Icebreaker.** First meeting, so keep introductions short enough that the prevented catastrophe gets airtime. If a room is stuck for examples: the ozone layer, seatbelts, a bug caught before release, a deadline saved by unglamorous prep. Then ask a question the unit invites: how would you tell an averted risk from an overstated one?
**Room 2, Load-bearing part, weakest weld.** Every Plan A essay prompt is optional, so expect some people to have skimmed the supplement without writing; the candidate list in the prompt is for them, and Option B readers skimmed the same supplement, so they can play. Say: "Which single mechanism, if removed, breaks Plan A?" Expect clustering on the datacenter retrofit and on the 2029 implementation timeline. The amendment step is where design happens: refuse vague amendments ("more inspections") and ask what the amendment costs the 2029 schedule or a signatory's willingness. If a room wants a verdict, ask for the lens's own vote: adopt as written, amend, or reject. If a room agrees too fast, split it on the covert margin: half argue 0.5% of world AI-relevant compute is a frontier-scale cluster in disguise, half argue a covert project that size cannot keep up (A4 asks what disadvantages it would face). Contrarian line from the drill bench, held loosely: a regime that promises zero covert compute is overclaiming; one that prices the residual rate is doing game theory.
**Room 3, The official who wants no regime.** Two traps. First, the official caves in thirty seconds; tell them to be stubborn, they represent a real position. Second, people name a structure (prisoner's dilemma, security dilemma) instead of stating the mechanism; ask "and why does that make continuing the safer move for a state that fears ASI?" If a room flounders, the three things the unit gives them: why shared fear does not stop the race, why trust, punishment afterwards and mutual transparency each fail between rivals, and what verification changes about each side's incentives. On the "no time" comeback, the unit's own material helps: export controls, cloud know-your-customer, and attestation on shipping GPUs exist today (built for the chip's owner, not for a verifier); cryptographic methods are mostly papers. The honest answer is partial, and that is fine.
**Room 4, Buddies, your theory of change, feedback.** Insist on real contact details in the table. Watch for outputs dressed as outcomes ("I'll finish every unit" is an output); ask what would be different in the world, or in their work, if the chain held. Feedback here is a verbal primer for XLab's form.**Wrap-up, in your own words (one minute).** Next unit is Precedents and policy scope: about 170 minutes, the densest of the five, so start early. Message your buddy today. Feedback goes to XLab's form, link in the doc. The call stays open for anyone who wants to keep talking.

## Glossary

### Module: Why should you care about AI verification?

AI verification: The set of mechanisms necessary to enforce an agreement to pause the development of advanced AI between multiple distrustful parties. It makes commitments credible without requiring general political trust.
Pre-paradigmatic field: The course's description of AI verification: there is no correct answer yet. Some mechanisms are established technologies or institutional practices, others are emerging proposals with substantial unresolved questions.
ASI (artificial superintelligence): In this course, AI with dangerous capabilities that pose a material existential threat to humanity. A key property would be recursive self-improvement (RSI): a model able to autonomously and exponentially improve itself.
Trust, punish, transparency, verification: The four ways two rivals could know the other keeps a deal. The course's verdict: trust fails between adversaries, punishment depends on detection and arrives too late for irreversible harm, transparency invites theft and fabricated compliance, verification holds.
Ex-post regime: Enforcement after the fact. It fails when consequences are immediate, far-reaching, and irreversible: no fine can bring back the dead.
Privacy-preserving verification: Each party can verify the other's compliance without learning its secrets, knowing the other can do the same. It displaces trust in a volatile adversary toward trust in a shared, robust verification regime.
Verification landscape: The course's map of where verification work happens, six kinds of verification against four kinds of actor. Its center of gravity is think tanks and nonprofits; the one place government verification is muscular today is export control.
Invisible prevention: A prevented catastrophe looks like nothing happened, which makes the risk psychologically easy to dismiss. Y2K did not collapse infrastructure because governments and companies spent years repairing systems, not because the issue was exaggerated.
Securitization: Treating ASI as the existential risk it is and thus a top policy priority. The course warns it also triggers incentives to use the technology for self-serving ends, so it must be executed carefully to minimize militarization.

### Module: We need more theories of change

Theory of change: The chain of if-then claims from what you do to what changes in the world. If you cannot state it, neither can the people deciding whether to fund or act on your work.
Outputs vs outcomes: Outputs are tangible products you produced (a paper, a benchmark, a memo); outcomes are what changed because of them (a lab altered a training procedure). Conflating the two is the common failure mode.
Assumptions vs external factors: Assumptions are what must hold for the chain to work, internal and testable; external factors are what lies outside your control.

### Module: Building verification intuitions

Plan A / Plan S: AI 2040's two futures: Plan A permits substantial AI activity under a layered verification regime; Plan S calls for a much stronger halt on frontier AI development. Plan S has no verification supplement of its own.
Datacenter retrofit: Plan A's core machinery: optical network taps, reproducible workloads, trusted recomputation servers, and network restrictions that make large-scale training in a declared datacenter detectable.
Load-bearing mechanism: The part of a regime that carries the most verification weight: the one that, if removed, would most endanger the regime's effectiveness.
Covert-compute margin: Compute that may remain hidden even after declarations and inspections. Plan A's supplement puts it initially on the order of 0.5% of world AI-relevant compute; the regime's job is to keep it too small to overturn the agreement.
Inspection game (Dresher): The inspector has limited inspections, so the equilibrium involves randomized inspection and a nonzero violation rate. A regime that promises zero is overclaiming; one that prices the residual rate is doing game theory.
Credible commitment (Fearon): Three ways to make a commitment believable: burn resources up front (sunk cost), make backing down costly later (tied hands), or remove the choice entirely (mechanical enforcement).
Two-level game (Putnam): A leader negotiates abroad and ratifies at home. It distinguishes voluntary defection (chose to cheat) from involuntary defection (couldn't deliver the home front); a verification regime must survive both.
