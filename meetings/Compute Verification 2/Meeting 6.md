### Room 2: Demo or regime?  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


**Don't mistake a demo for a regime.** The unit's five maturity stages, from least to most mature:

- a single security feature in use today (for example, chips proving their identity)
- one component tested under set conditions
- a complete prototype system
- a pilot at the scale that matters for policy, surviving testing against a realistic adversary
- a working regime: law, institutions, appeals, enforcement and international acceptance, all in practice

Roleplay. Designate one of you as the chip vendor. The others are a delegation weighing a three-month U.S.-China pause on large training runs. The vendor's pitch: "Our chips already prove their identity. They can verify your pause."

1. The vendor pitches for two minutes, and the delegation matches each promise to a stage. Example promises:
    - counting how much compute each chip used
    - telling training from inference
    - proving where each chip is located
    - chips that stop unless an authority keeps renewing their permit
2. Out of role, agree in one sentence on a limited job for chips in the pause.
3. Name that job's biggest blind spot.
4. What evidence outside the chip would cover it?

### Room 3: Rent your way around it  [0:52–1:10]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


A cloud provider already records each rental:

- which chips
- how many hours
- how the chips are networked
- the bill

The know-your-customer (KYC) scheme proposed in this unit, borrowed from banking, adds:

- before a customer's compute purchases cross a set threshold, the provider checks who the customer is and who really owns it
- it keeps watching use after sign-up, including for one customer posing as several
- it shares information with other providers to catch customers who split their purchases among them
- it reports customers that fit the government's high-risk profiles
- it can refuse or cut off access

Your group is a well-funded lab that fits a high-risk profile, such as having strong links to a country of concern. You want to buy cloud compute above the threshold for one training run, without being reported.

1. Plan your route around the scheme.
2. Which provider record could still give you away?
3. Change one rule of the scheme to close your route.
4. Price your change: what does it cost honest customers or the providers?

### Per-room notes for Unit 6

**General, all rooms.** This is the first meeting of the second course. Most participants did Compute Verification 1, some with another cohort or a while ago, so keep references to it light. The unit closes the hardware material and opens the cloud, and both halves ask the same question: what does this evidence support, and what does it still not establish? The unit is about 130 minutes, a third of it the written brief, so many people will have done only part of it. Every prompt works from the doc alone, so do not re-teach.
**Room 1, Getting to know each other.** Keep introductions to about thirty seconds each so asks 3 and 4 get time. Asks 3 and 4 are the unit's cloud idea in miniature: a provider's billing, identity and usage records reveal a lot about a customer, but not everything. If a room is stuck: a bank statement shows where you were and what you paid, not why or for whom. A phone bill shows who you called, not what was said. Do not let it turn into a privacy debate.
**Room 2, Demo or regime?** The vendor argues in good faith, and the delegation's job is to place each promise on the five stages. The unit's own assessment, dated August 2026: hardware can already authenticate supported devices and selected state or configuration claims, protect some measurement and confidential-computing functions under stated threat models, and anchor logs and evidence from cloud or inspection systems. All four promises in the list (compute accounting, telling training from inference, location checks, chips that stop unless their permit is renewed) are promising but incomplete. No public evidence shows a deployed, treaty-grade system that works against a state-level owner, universal coverage of legacy, custom, smuggled or nonparticipating hardware, reliable detection of all undeclared compute, or an accepted international authority for keys, suspension and enforcement. A good one-sentence job is narrow, for example authenticating declared chips and anchoring a cloud provider's logs. The blind spot is usually compute outside the declared set. The covering evidence comes from outside the chip: power and satellite monitoring, intelligence leads, inspections or insiders, and cloud records for rented compute only. If a group's job is authenticating declared chips, push on what attestation cannot show: it tests identity and configuration, not what was computed, so training disguised as inference on declared chips needs on-site inspection of workloads (the lens's model answer). In ask 2, invite anyone who wrote the brief to offer their recommendation as a candidate, but do not let the room become a read-out. If a room finishes early, ask: would you sign the pause with chips doing only that job, and what would change your vote? Or: does the job still hold when the chips' owner is a state with physical access to them?
**Room 3, Rent your way around it.** No single right route. Likely routes: splitting the run across accounts or providers so that none crosses the threshold (the readings call this structuring, after banking), a front company, a provider in a country outside the scheme, chips the lab owns, or a workload disguised as something else. The scheme already answers some of these in part: checks on who really owns a customer before the threshold is crossed, watching for one customer posing as several, and information sharing between providers, which Egan and Heim call a possible key tool against compute bought in pieces from several companies but which U.S. antitrust law may restrict. Egan and Heim expect malicious actors to keep trying to hide their identity, so the routes stay arguable, but each group must say how its route gets past the checks. On the jurisdiction route, Heim et al. say oversight works only with multilateral adoption, or the activity moves to places with weaker oversight. Records that might still give the lab away: the hardware configuration requested (the number of accelerators and the networking setup suggest the workload) and the hours in use, which customers can hardly alter, plus power draw (Heim et al., Table 4). Customer identity data (their Table 3) is the easiest to spoof. When a group names a record, ask: does it prove a training run happened, or only that one was possible? Heim et al. say hardware and hours show whether a workload was possible, which is grounds for a closer investigation. Push on ask 4: a group that proposes more sharing between providers should price it in customer privacy and antitrust risk, and a lower threshold catches more honest customers. If a room needs a nudge on enforcement, banking's own history helps: the Bank Secrecy Act's reporting rules went unenforced from 1972 to 1985 and compliance stayed low. A $500,000 penalty in 1985 brought a sharp rise in reporting, and more evasive behavior from customers (Egan and Heim). Early-finish twist: the lab now owns its chips. Which records still matter? The cloud lens says self-hosted clusters sit outside a provider's reach.
**Room 4, Buddies and your goal.** In the whole-group slot before Room 4, walk through the buddy setup above, and ask buddies to message each other today, so both know the channel works. Insist on real contact details in the table. Push the goal toward a specific judgment ("tell whether a provider's records prove a training run happened"), not "learn more about verification". Unit 7 is longer than Unit 6, so push for a concrete plan in ask 3.

## Glossary

### Module: Course Overview

Evidence stream: One kind of verification evidence, such as hardware, cloud records, intelligence or human sources. The course asks of each what claims it can support and what it cannot establish on its own.

### Module: Hardware Judgment

Maturity inflation: The most common error in judging hardware verification: inferring a later maturity stage from evidence at an early one, such as calling a demo a regime.
Five maturity stages: Deployed primitive, empirical component demonstration, end-to-end prototype, relevant-scale pilot, operating governance regime. Do not infer stage five from evidence at stages one or two.
Deployed primitive: A real security feature or service in use today, such as supported GPU attestation or confidential-computing functionality.
Confidential computing: A deployed security feature that protects code and data in use from the host, under stated threat models. Heim et al. see a possible way for customers to prove a workload's properties without revealing its data, while Egan and Heim note that it can stop providers from seeing customer data at all.
Workload classification: Determining whether a workload falls within a category relevant to regulatory requirements, such as a large training run (Heim et al.). Telling training from inference is one such classification.
Compute accounting: Measuring how much compute a workload used. Heim et al. treat it as a standalone check against a compute-based reporting threshold or as an input to workload classification.
Relevant-scale pilot: A system that survives operational and adversarial testing at the scale, access level and threat model that matter for policy.
Operating governance regime: Institutions, legal authority, deployment, maintenance, appeals, enforcement and international acceptance working in practice.
Bounded job: The unit's shape for a hardware recommendation: a limited role inside a layered regime, with named corroborating evidence for its blind spot and a condition under which the recommendation expires.
Hardware assurance brief: The unit's written output: 700 to 1,000 words for a national delegation considering a three-month U.S.-China pause, recommending a bounded use of one hardware architecture.
Collapsing weakness: The unresolved assumption that would defeat the role proposed for a mechanism.
Residual blind spot: What a mechanism still cannot see, which cloud, intelligence, inspection or human evidence must corroborate.
Update condition: The evidence, trend or political change that would raise or lower a recommendation.
Claim ledger: Seven proposed conclusions a lab's valid attestation tokens might support, first classified at the start of Unit 5 and classified again at the end of the hardware material to see what moved.
What attestation alone does not establish: Cumulative compute, workload class, declared cluster topology, absence of unregistered hardware, or legal authority to suspend. Those need further measurement, aggregation, policy and institutions.

### Module: Cloud Records

Compute provider: A company that owns, operates, meters and bills for the compute it rents out, which puts it between AI developers and the machines they use.
Four governance capacities: Heim et al.'s roles for compute providers. Securers safeguard AI systems and critical infrastructure, record keepers give policymakers visibility, verifiers check customer activities, and in some cases enforcers act against rule violations.
Multilateral adoption: Heim et al.'s condition for cloud oversight to work: without it, AI activity migrates to jurisdictions with less stringent oversight.
Identity verification: Ensuring a customer or user is who they say they are.
Structuring: Breaking one transaction or job into smaller pieces to stay below a reporting threshold, such as a training run spread across several accounts or providers. Banks met it once they had to report transactions over $10,000 a day.
Observable data attributes: The signals a provider can see about a workload, such as the hardware configuration requested, the hours resources are in use and power draw. Heim et al.'s Table 4 maps each to the verification task it could support.
Know your customer (KYC): In finance, rules that make institutions identify customers and their beneficial owners, assess risk, apply enhanced due diligence to higher-risk customers and report suspicious activity. Egan and Heim propose a KYC scheme for compute providers.
Beneficial owner: The person who ultimately owns or controls a customer, behind any front or shell company.
Bank Secrecy Act: The U.S. law behind banking KYC, whose reporting rules went unenforced from 1972 to 1985. A $500,000 penalty in 1985 brought a sharp rise in reporting, and more evasive behavior from customers.
KYC threshold: Egan and Heim's dynamic compute threshold for which customers the scheme covers, set to capture high-risk frontier development while imposing as little as possible on everyone else.
Ongoing monitoring: High-level usage monitoring after sign-up to catch changes, such as new contracts that bring a customer above the threshold.
