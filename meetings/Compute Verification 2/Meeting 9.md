### Per-room notes for Unit 9

**General, all rooms.** The unit turns to the adversary: first a worked case built by a red team and taken apart by a blue team, then a close reading of Naci Cankaya's working paper, *A System Overview for Near-Term, Low-Trust AI Compute Verification*. The worked case sets the unit's standard: an evasion made inconclusive rather than invisible, and a response that tells suspicion from proof. The paper's attribution problem is the same issue for a technical checker. The unit is about 190 minutes, much of it the paper and its questions, so expect uneven reading. Every prompt works from the doc alone, so do not re-teach.
**Room 1, Worth the risk?** A warm-up for the logic behind Room 3. Cankaya builds on the covert adversary threat model (Aumann and Lindell): cheating is deterred when the chance of detection times the cost of getting caught exceeds the expected gain. Ask 4 previews the attribution problem, where a caught cheater blames an accident. Keep it light: small, everyday rules, and nobody needs to confess anything.
**Room 2, Suspicion or proof?** The case is the unit's fictional Training Through the Pause example. In the lens, the engineer's role reaches investigators only as a fragmentary intelligence report, and the small company has no obvious public connection to Sable. The declared purposes in the lens are protein-model inference, benchmark evaluation and safety research. The lens's debrief: the red team did not hide a new data center, it tried to keep training on legitimate infrastructure and spread the evidence across an AI company, a cloud reseller, a data center, shell companies and people. The blue team did not rely on one perfect detector: it preserved records, rebuilt the timeline across layers, tested alternatives and recommended action in proportion to the evidence. On ask 2, push toward evidence that ties two layers together rather than one more signal from a single layer. On ask 3, Unit 7's ladder still applies: preserve records and ask for clarification before anything costlier. Ask 4 tests the same point from the other end: the intelligence report is a tip, and a tip is not a finding. Two optional real cases in the lens fit here: Linwei Ding moved protected Google files past a control by changing their format, and Chinese organizations sought access to restricted chips through cloud services and intermediaries rather than importing them. Some people will have opened the widget's model answers. Let them share their own view rather than read the model answer out.
**Room 3, Beat the checker.** The five lines summarize the paper's own overview in section 1. Its key argument for ask 2 is the inversion of the fortress problem: an openly malicious adversary can attack as often as it likes, but a covert adversary is caught if it fails to stay hidden even once, so random sampling only needs to catch one instance. Routes groups may find: running work the taps do not see, tampering with the taps or the evaluation environment, or hiding work inside declared jobs. The paper's own defenses: physical security and inspection to make sure devices are wired correctly, redundant re-computation on several diverse trusted computing bases, resource accounting, memory wiping and side-channel defenses. The paper also avoids chips both sides must trust: it notes that there is no accepted method for proving an electronic system has no vulnerabilities. On ask 4, this is the paper's attribution problem: a failed check does not say whether it came from evasion, a bit flip or a software error, treating every bug as a violation would sink the agreement in false positives, and the parties must agree in advance on an escalation procedure that ends in attributing the failure to error or malice. Cankaya invites exactly this kind of red-teaming on the drawing board, so a strong route counts as a contribution, not a gotcha.
**Room 4, Your weak point and the next unit.** Keep ask 1 quick so ask 2 gets time. Push for evidence that could actually be gathered, such as a lab test or a public record. The next unit is the last of the course and ends in its main written output, so if a room reaches ask 3, push for a concrete plan.

## Glossary

### Module: Covert Development and the Low-Trust Architecture

Unexpected format or channel: The lesson of the Linwei Ding case: a regime may fail when an authorized person moves protected information through a format or channel its controls do not cover.
Move the activity, not the object: The lesson of the cloud route around chip export controls: when moving a regulated object is difficult, an actor may leave it in place and move the activity to it.
Training Through the Pause: The unit's fictional worked example. During a twelve-month pause, Sable Systems appears to continue a paused frontier run using an insider at Northstar and cloud accounts kept below the review threshold.
Red team, blue team: In the worked example, the red team explains the most plausible evasion, its assumptions and its weakest point. The blue team combines evidence from the hardware, cloud, intelligence and human layers and recommends a proportionate response.
Inconclusive, not invisible: How the worked example's evasion works. The red team did not hide a new data center but tried to keep training on legitimate infrastructure, spreading the evidence across an AI company, a cloud reseller, a data center, shell companies and people.
Low-trust verification: Verifying AI compute use between parties who do not trust each other, the problem Cankaya's working paper addresses.
Mutually trusted silicon: Chips that both parties must trust. Cankaya's design does without them, since there is no accepted method for proving the absence of vulnerabilities in an electronic system.
Trusted computing base (TCB): Minimalist, security-critical logic, for example for hashing, comparing and gating information. In Cankaya's design each one is trusted by one party only.
Network tap: Cankaya's default mechanism for capturing evidence of what workloads ran on the prover's hardware (the prover is the party whose compute use is checked).
Commitment: A signed hash of captured evidence. The verifier receives only commitments, while the prover stores the plaintext evidence, which can later be checked against them.
Secure evaluation environment: An air-gapped environment where randomly selected records are opened and evaluated, releasing only a disclosure in a pre-agreed format. It is Cankaya's tentative plan A, with zero-knowledge proofs as plan B if they mature.
Covert adversary threat model: Aumann and Lindell's model, which Cankaya builds on: cheating is deterred when the likelihood of detection times the cost of getting caught exceeds the expected gain. A covert adversary is caught if it fails to stay hidden even once.
Attribution problem: A technical mismatch does not say whether it came from a covert evasion attempt, a random bit flip or evaluation software misjudging an edge case. The parties must agree on an escalation procedure that ends in attributing it to error or malice.
Replay: Re-running a sampled computation to check it against the committed record. Cankaya names non-determinism as the challenge it faces.
