**New group. Names first, then straight in.**

### Room 3: Write the sharing clause  [0:52–1:10]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


From the part of MIRI's draft AI treaty on each side's own sensors (satellites, aircraft, cyber, signals, imagery and other remote sensing):

- each side uses them to check that the other complies
- each side promises not to interfere with the other's sensors
- each side promises not to hide activity from them on purpose
- helping each other watch countries outside the treaty is encouraged, not required
- nothing obliges either side to share what its sensors see

Roleplay. One of you speaks for Washington, one for Beijing, and the rest chair the drafting.

1. Draft one sentence obliging both sides to share something their sensors see.
2. Washington and Beijing each strike what they can't accept.
3. Test what survives against the rule of thumb: states sign what binds both sides the same way, costs little and can be checked. Does yours pass?
4. If nothing survives, defend leaving sharing voluntary in two sentences.

### Per-room notes for Unit 7

**General, all rooms.** The unit has two halves: where cloud oversight runs out, then intelligence, the one evidence stream that works without the watched side's cooperation. The line to hold all meeting: intelligence identifies, the regime resolves, and a tip is not a finding. The unit is about 180 minutes, so expect uneven reading. Every prompt works from the doc alone, so do not re-teach.
**Room 1, Traces of a secret.** A warm-up for the intelligence half. A secret program leaks through purchases, hiring and schedules the way a surprise party does: the intelligence lens says a state can lie in its declarations but cannot opt out of thermodynamics, customs, payroll or the power grid. Ask 3 is the unit's hardest problem in miniature: most traces have an innocent explanation. Keep it light, and do not draw the AI parallel for them unless a room is stuck.
**Room 2, Lead or noise?** No single right answer. Facts to nudge with: snow-free rooftops in winter are a thermal signature (Baker), and imagery alone rarely tells an AI data center from another kind, since cooling is the key difference. Around 500 data centers worldwide exceed 10 MW, so a big substation sits in a large haystack. Scher and Thiergart rate power-grid analysis only Medium feasibility: covert generation at about 130 MW is plausible, and chips' performance per watt improves about 1.6 times a year. A GPU-import surge far above declared needs is Wasil et al.'s canonical indicator, and Scher and Thiergart rate financial intelligence High feasibility. A hiring wave is dual-use: the same engineers serve permitted work. On ask 2, the three options are the unit's chain, anomaly → verification lead → suspected non-compliance, each with a different evidentiary bar. Escalating too fast burns credibility, and escalating too slowly loses the window in which the activity can be seen. Push groups to name the assumption their inference rests on. On ask 3, ask what each step costs if they turn out to be wrong. The first rung, asking the country to explain against its declarations, is the cheapest, and most anomalies die there. Each rung up costs more politically. Ask what they would record with their step: their confidence, any dissent, and what nobody could see. On ask 4, the cloud lens's point is that a rule counting compute per account misses a run split across accounts, so the provider reports nothing unless the rule adds up accounts with one owner. A rented run may also leave no new building to photograph, as the model answer in the empirical-anchor lens notes. Provider records can tie an account to an identity and estimate resource use, but on their own they do not establish intent, model contents, capability or a violation. Early-finish twist: the country says the site is a video-streaming company's new region. How would you test that story? Cover stories can be checked: Iran's denial of activity at one site was contradicted by commercial satellite imagery.
**Room 3, Write the sharing clause.** The unit's asymmetry: treaties are weak on sharing and strong on protection. Nuclear treaties oblige no intelligence agency to share with verifiers. Sharing is voluntary, informal and inconsistent (Baker), and the lens names the protection of sources and methods as the reason. Protection clauses are standard and easy by contrast: Rose Gottemoeller, the U.S. chief negotiator for New START, called noninterference one of the earliest and easiest points of agreement. Clause (iii) of MIRI's Article X ¶1(b) encourages cooperation only against countries outside the treaty, so nothing in the draft obliges the parties to share with each other. Article X ¶1 does call the parties' own information gathering a key source for the coalition, and sets up a division ready to receive it. *Six Layers* argues, in a footnote, for relying on national agencies rather than an international body that spies, so weaker states free-ride, and in the U.S.-China case each side trusts its own agencies. Pushes: does the clause require sharing raw collection, which exposes sources and methods, or only conclusions? How would the receiving side know shared evidence is not fake? The lens points to chain of custody, and to Glaser, Barak and Goldston's warhead test (Nature, 2014), which confirms a match without revealing the design. Invite anyone who wrote the unit's red-line to offer their clause as the first draft, but do not let the room become a read-out. If a room finishes early: does "cyber" in the definition legalize hacking? Is behind-the-meter power generation deliberate concealment, or ordinary industrial practice? In the whole-group slot after Room 3, ask one or two groups to read out their clause and say which capital struck what. Room 4 does not come back to the clauses.
**Room 4, Your habit and the next unit.** The clauses were shared just before, so this room starts straight on the habits. The three habits in ask 1 come from the cloud-evidence and assessment lenses. Push each person to name where they will use theirs first, and have the others push back if a habit stays vague. Unit 8 is the longest unit of the course, so if a room reaches ask 2, push for a concrete plan.

## Glossary

### Module: Cloud Limits

Detection gap: RAND's term for the training runs a cloud provider could fail to detect under compute-threshold rules. In their model, runs between 10²³ and 10²⁶ operations slip through when an evader spreads them across accounts or sessions that each stay under the thresholds.
Mandatory metrics: The few metrics, such as total power or FLOPs, that a regulation obliges a cloud provider to report on. A reading is recorded when a metric exceeds its threshold.
Cloud controls: Rules restricting who can rent cloud compute. Tan names the two loopholes they target: Chinese companies can legally lease compute from third-country clouds or from U.S.-based ones.

### Module: Intelligence

Intelligence: As this course uses the word, collecting and reading the traces a secret program leaves, without the cooperation of the actor being watched.
National technical means (NTM): The treaty term for a state's own collection, such as reconnaissance satellites, signals interception and remote sensors. MIRI's draft treaty defines it to include satellite, aerial, cyber, signals, imagery (including thermal) and other remote-sensing capabilities.
Identify and resolve: The unit's division of labor. Intelligence identifies a possible violation, and the regime resolves it with its own tools, so a tip is not a finding.
Signature: An observable trace of undeclared development, described by what it tells a verifier, what access it requires and its main caveat.
Four signature families: Facility (what the building is and looks like), resource-flow (power, water, chips and money moving in), organizational (who is hired, who contracts with whom) and operational (what the site does, and when).
Behind-the-meter generation: A site making its own electricity, so its load never shows on the grid.
Empirical anchor: Baker's finding that of four IAEA investigations of undeclared facilities (Iraq, North Korea, Iran, Syria), three began mainly or entirely with national intelligence tips, and none with the IAEA's own safeguards.
Observation, inference, judgment: What a sensor or source recorded, what that implies given some assumption, and what an analyst concludes given the inference, the alternatives and their own confidence.
Dual-use ambiguity: The same building, chips and people serve permitted and prohibited activity, so ambiguity is the normal case, not the exception.
Base rate: How many facilities of a kind exist, and how many are violations. Around 500 data centers worldwide exceed 10 MW, so a signature that fires on all of them tells you nothing.
Anomaly, verification lead, suspected non-compliance: The chain from a signal to a case. Each move along it is a decision someone has to justify, and each has a different evidentiary bar.
Quality of Information Check: A technique from the CIA's *A Tradecraft Primer* that evaluates the completeness and soundness of available information sources.
Indicators or Signposts of Change: A technique that periodically reviews a list of observable events or trends to track events, monitor targets, spot emerging trends and warn of unanticipated change.
Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH): A technique that identifies alternative explanations and weighs the evidence that would disconfirm, rather than confirm, each.
Weak on sharing, strong on protection: The asymmetric precedent of arms-control treaties: they protect each side's collection but oblige nobody to share what it sees.
Noninterference clause: A standard arms-control commitment not to interfere with the other side's national technical means, such as by blinding its satellites.
No-deliberate-concealment clause: A commitment not to use deliberate concealment measures that impede verification by national technical means.
Free-riding: Weaker-intelligence states relying on stronger states' collection, a consequence of relying on national rather than international intelligence agencies.
Symmetric, cheap, checkable: The unit's rule of thumb for what states sign: it binds both sides the same way, complying costs little, and each side can tell whether the other complies.
Escalation ladder: What a regime does when clarification fails: task additional collection, seek corroboration, then trigger an audit or challenge inspection. Each rung costs more than the one below.
