### Per-room notes for Unit 8

**General, all rooms.** This unit is the human layer: whistleblowers, interviews, audits and inspections. The intro lens calls these the most enforceable and the least reliable mechanisms in the course: a person can be compelled to speak but not to be truthful. The unit is the longest of the course, about 220 minutes, so expect many people to have done only part of it. Every prompt works from the doc alone, so do not re-teach.
**Room 1, Who would notice?** A warm-up for the unit's first question: who could know, and would they tell? The TED talk in the reporting lens is about why people often don't say something when they see something. Keep it light, and do not push anyone to share a real case. Ask 3 tends to surface the barriers the unit's protections target: fear of retaliation, contracts that forbid disclosure, and doubt that anything will happen after a report.
**Room 2, Your best source.** No single right pick. The insiders lens's line: a job title tells you where a person stood, not what they saw. Push groups to separate what their pick saw directly from what they would only have heard. On ask 3, the unit's Project Lattice case is a good example: in the audits lens's version, independent power-allocation and procurement records showed a six-week expansion under the project code, but they identify a facility and a period, not the workload. A human source can tell an investigator where to look and which records to preserve, and technical or physical evidence is still needed for what the source did not see. On ask 4, the lens names four ways a consistent account can still mislead: selective truth (the expansion is real, the prohibited workload is not), a coordinated cover story (matching accounts from people managers selected or briefed), management staging (a clean tour shows what management chose to show) and suppression (few reports means little if staff have no safe channel or fear retaliation). Baker et al.'s premise for this layer: large groups find it hard to collude without someone disclosing or leaking. Early-finish twist: the source is truthful and allowed to report. Where could the report still die? The lens lists: no independent corroboration, a recipient that cannot legally share with the verifier, a report that names no facility or account, records outside the verifier's mandate, and a channel that strips what follow-up needs.
**Room 3, Who judges?** In the nuclear regime, IAEA inspectors find, the Director-General reports, the Board of Governors judges and the Security Council enforces. Carlson's point: the Board has never defined noncompliance, it decided five cases one by one, and the system's credibility rides on consistency. The institutions lens calls the judge the role still open for AI: every candidate trades legitimacy against capture, through who funds it, who staffs it and who clears its reports. The unit maps four kinds of capture: financial, informational, cultural and political. On ask 2, the others pick a winner and say which argument landed. On ask 3, push both ways: what would Beijing need to accept an acquittal from the same judge? Ask 4 is the lens's breach-versus-noncompliance line in plain words, and the lens's test for any such rule: would two rivals, reading it in advance, predict the same verdict on the same facts? If a room is fast, ask about the enforcer. The lens suggests AI's lever may be denying chips, cloud capacity or interconnect through a few supply-chain chokepoints, with the same strain as the Security Council: the likeliest violators sit inside the coalition that would have to act. Invite anyone who answered the lens's question on the judge to offer their candidate first, and anyone who wrote a standard in the lens to read it out on ask 4, but do not let the room become a read-out.
**Room 4, Your question and the next unit.** The three questions come from the insiders and reporting lenses. Keep ask 1 quick so ask 2 gets time. People did Room 2 in different groups, so each will have a different source in mind. Unit 9 is shorter than Unit 8 but ends in a long close reading, so if a room reaches ask 3, push for a concrete plan.

## Glossary

### Module: Insiders and Reporting

Human-based verification: Verification through people, such as personnel interviews and whistleblower programs. The unit calls it both the most enforceable and the least reliable kind, because people can obfuscate, lie and hide motivations.
Personnel-based verification: Baker et al.'s layer that relies on the difficulty of having large groups of people collude without disclosures or leaks. Verifiers seek those through whistleblower programs, interviews of personnel and national intelligence.
Project Lattice: The unit's running case. A cooling contractor reports that the project added power and cooling for 1,024 accelerators over six weeks, and a separately obtained utility record carries the same work-order code.
Four ways a consistent account misleads: Selective truth (part of the account is real, the prohibited part is not), a coordinated cover story (managers selected or briefed the speakers), management staging (a tour shows what management chose) and suppression (staff have no safe channel or fear retaliation).
Covered employee: Under California's whistleblower chapter for AI (SB 53), an employee responsible for assessing, managing or addressing risk of critical safety incidents.
Catastrophic risk: In the same chapter, a foreseeable and material risk that a frontier developer's model materially contributes to more than 50 deaths or serious injuries, or more than $1 billion in damage, in a single incident. The incident must involve expert help with weapons of mass destruction, certain conduct without meaningful human oversight, or the model evading its developer's control.
Four levers: The modes of leverage the unit maps whistleblower mechanisms to: a legal right and remedies, the reporter's incentives, a duty on the developer, and escalation as professional conduct.
Quality Standards for Investigations: The standards of the Council of the Inspectors General on Integrity and Efficiency (CIGIE). Its four qualitative standards are planning, execution, reporting and information management.
Protection finding, evidence finding: Two different thresholds. A statute can protect a person, subject and recipient while leaving identity safeguards, competence or authority unresolved, and preserved records may support one fact without supporting the larger allegation.
Capture: Control over a verifying institution by an interested party, along the lines of who funds it, who staffs it and who clears its reports. The unit maps four kinds: financial, informational, cultural and political.
Managed access: The Chemical Weapons Convention's rule that the extent of access within an inspection perimeter is negotiated, to protect information unrelated to compliance. Done well, it masks unrelated information without blocking the evidence the inspection needs.

### Module: Audits and Institutions

Independent audit: A test of defined claims and practices against a standard, within a stated engagement scope.
Routine inspection: A check of declarations and recurring obligations on an agreed calendar. It does not rule out undeclared activity elsewhere.
Challenge inspection: Short-notice access when a specific concern cannot be resolved through consultation. The concern supplies the target, not a pre-judged violation.
Level of assurance: How confident an auditor is in their conclusions about a company's safety and security practices. Brundage et al. propose four AI Assurance Levels, and the lower ones can detect errors but are less likely to detect fraud.
Access ceiling: The most an inspector may conclude from the access granted. Black-box access supports a behavioral statement about the tested system, gray-box supports selected system and practice claims, and deep access can support scoped organization-level findings.
Finder, judge, enforcer, standard: The roles of the nuclear regime. IAEA inspectors find the facts, the Board of Governors judges whether they amount to noncompliance, the Security Council enforces, and a standard says what counts.
Noncompliance: In Carlson's account, a judgment the IAEA Board makes, not a checklist. The Board has never defined it and decided its cases one by one.
Standard of proof: In Carlson's account, a judgment based not on certainty but on the balance of probabilities. The unit's dockets ask when the evidence is enough to move from investigating to judging.
