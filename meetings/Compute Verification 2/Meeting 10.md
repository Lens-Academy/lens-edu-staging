### Per-room notes for Unit 10

**General, all rooms.** This is the last meeting of the course. The unit makes two points before its main exercise: define cheating before trying to detect it, and layered defenses work only when the layers fail differently. It then maps ten routes around an agreement and ends in the unit's main exercise, an expert review of which obligations in Articles IV to VII of MIRI's draft treaty Cankaya's system from Unit 9 could carry. The unit is about 200 minutes, so many people will be partway through the review. Every prompt works without it, so do not re-teach.
**Room 1, More than one lock.** A warm-up for the Swiss-cheese model. The lens's point is not more evidence but layers that rely on different information, different actors and different access assumptions, so that the failure of one does not automatically defeat the rest. Ask 3 is the test: safeguards that one trick can beat all at once are really one layer. Keep it light, and nobody needs to share real security details. Ask 4 closes the loop with Meeting 6's Room 4, where each person named one thing they wanted to be able to judge or argue by the end of the course. Anyone who missed that meeting can name what they hoped for when they started.
**Room 2, Holes that line up.** The three routes are 1, 5 and 10 of the unit's ten. The taxonomy lists the layers each one mainly targets: route 1 targets identity checks, customer screening, procurement, payments and beneficial ownership, route 5 targets workload declarations, scheduler and cloud logs, research oversight and code and data review, and route 10 targets the hardware root of trust, monitoring devices, evidence systems, inspectors and reporting channels. The A. Q. Khan network is the lens's archetype of route 1, and the reason beneficial-ownership checks, reseller-chain analysis and cross-border information sharing matter. On ask 3, the nuclear stack shows layers answering different questions: a report says where to look, imagery identifies a facility, accounting reveals a gap, logs reconstruct activity, an inspection tests the declaration and enforcement changes incentives. Ask 4 is the unit's first lesson: a compute cap, a declaration requirement, a research ban and a model-security obligation create different evasion opportunities, and the same activity can be prohibited under one agreement and permitted under another. The lens also says a realistic scheme combines one primary route with several supporting tactics, and the scoring widget in the review lens rates seven of the ten routes relatively on feasibility, verification effectiveness and durability, not as probabilities. Routes 1 and 5 are among those scored, route 10 is not.
**Room 3, The panel's verdict.** The lens's framing: the treaty says what must be verified, the architecture says what can be evidenced, and the review is about the gap. It also asks reviewers to treat the system as one component of the regime, not a complete implementation of Articles IV to VII. Article VII, on-site verification of chip use at declared clusters, is closest to what the system captures. It also lists inspectors, cameras, off-chip measurements and on-chip mechanisms among its methods, so the system is one way to carry it, not the only one. For the others, the lens's Question 3 names what else might carry an obligation: inventories, inspections, supply-chain controls, legal authority, intelligence or other institutions. Its Question 7 is a good push: does evidence about workloads inside a monitored facility establish total compute, prohibited purpose, chip location, production and transfer, attribution and enforcement? On ask 4, ask each side for the system's strongest feature and its most consequential weakness behind the vote, and have anyone in the minority state their dissent in one sentence. Invite anyone who has written the review to offer their coverage classifications first, but do not let the room become a read-out.
**Room 4, Your ongoing action.** Push for a first step with a real date. The Compute Verification Capstone is the next course in the track, and finishing and sharing the expert review is a concrete step for anyone mid-way through it. Ask 2 keeps the accountability going after the course: people who swap details can check in on each other's first step. Do not ask for feedback here: the survey and the feedback page collect it.

## Glossary

### Module: Evasion Routes and the Red Team Review

Define before you detect: Before asking how to detect cheating, define what counts as cheating. A compute cap, a declaration requirement, a research ban and a model-security obligation create different evasion opportunities, and the same activity can be prohibited under one agreement and permitted under another.
A. Q. Khan network: The network that transferred centrifuge designs, components and know-how to Iran, Libya and North Korea through experts, suppliers, intermediaries and front companies. It is the historical archetype of the proxy route around supplier controls.
Nuclear Suppliers Group (NSG): A supplier-side chokepoint arrangement: states that control important upstream technology agree on common rules for transfer.
Swiss-cheese model: Combining defenses whose holes do not line up. The layers rely on different information, actors and access assumptions, so the failure of one does not automatically defeat the rest.
Primary route, supporting tactics: A realistic scheme combines one primary route with several supporting tactics.
Ten evasion routes: The unit's map: conceal the real actor, divert controlled hardware, hide an undeclared cluster or facility, split the operation across sites, repurpose legitimate infrastructure, falsify declarations or provenance, steal or copy model weights, distill or extract capability, exploit a boundary in the rule, and attack the verification system.
Distillation: Querying a model and training a new model to imitate parts of its behavior. It can transfer useful capability without reproducing the teacher model's exact weights.
Strict Threshold, Monitored Threshold: In MIRI's draft treaty, training runs above the Strict Threshold of 10²⁴ FLOP are prohibited. Runs between the Monitored Threshold of 10²² FLOP and the Strict Threshold must be approved and monitored.
Covered chip cluster (CCC): In MIRI's draft treaty, any set of AI chips or networked cluster with more than 16 H100-equivalents of effective computing capacity or accelerator memory. Article V requires every CCC to sit in a facility declared to the treaty body.
Expert review: The unit's written output: a 2 to 6 page review for an independent panel on whether Cankaya's low-trust system could make the obligations in Articles IV to VII credible and enforceable.
Coverage: The review's classification of how far the system supports each article: substantial, partial or not provided.
Route scores: The unit's relative, conditional ratings of the evasion routes on technical feasibility, organizational feasibility, verification effectiveness and durability. They are not measured probabilities.
