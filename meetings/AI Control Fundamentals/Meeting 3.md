### Room 2: The weakest bet  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Working on control leans on a few bets. Three of them:

- AIs stay useful but still controllable long enough to do real safety work.
- A few safety-minded staff can keep cheap control measures running at a company that doesn't take the risk seriously.
- Red teams, the people who attack the safety measures in tests, find attacks at least as good as the ones the real AI would try, even as AIs get more capable.

1\. Which bet do you trust least? Agree on one.
2\. One person defends it as well as they can. The others attack it. Does it hold?
3\. If you still have time: what could happen in the next few years that would change your mind about it?

### Per-room notes for Unit 3

**General, all rooms.** This course is balanced. It shows why people work on control and the criticisms of it, and wants each participant to form their own view. Never steer a group toward a verdict on control, in either direction. If a group asks what you think, ask them what they would need to see to decide. "Control does little in a low-will world" and "control is the best bet in a low-will world" are both fine endpoints if they are argued. The unit is about 3.5 to 4 hours, so many people will have read only part of it. Every room works from the doc alone. Do not let people who finished lecture those who did not. Call two minutes before each room ends.
**Room 1, Before and after.** The "before" is the guess from the unit's welcome page, so people who stopped early still have one. If someone skipped it, a guess made now is fine. Take answers without correcting them. A "before" of "it stops the AI" is the most common starting point.
**Room 2, The weakest bet.** Push groups to agree on a bet within three minutes. Ask 2 needs one person to defend the bet honestly, not as a straw man. For your own use, not an answer key, here is what the readings say about each bet. The window: Greenblatt and Shlegeris say it is "reasonably likely to be too short (e.g., 6 months or 1 year)" if AI is built as fast as possible, but think holding off uncontrollable AI for "at least several years" is plausible. The insiders: Shlegeris's "Ten people on the inside" argues ten such people are much better than zero. Zac Hatfield-Dodds (Anthropic) and Rohin Shah (Google DeepMind) doubt they would get that much room, and in 2026 Shlegeris says he is less into this plan than before. The red teams: the authors of the control safety case sketch (UK AI Security Institute and Redwood Research) name this premise themselves, and Shlegeris has stopped thinking about "the really high-assurance stuff". The catch bet is left out here because Room 3 is about it. Ask 3 needs things someone could see, for example whether safety teams at less careful companies keep monitoring running. "More research" is not an observation. A group that ends more confident is as successful as one that ends less.
**Room 3, Would a catch change anything?** The case in the doc is a simplified version of Shlegeris's post "Would catching your AIs trying to escape convince AI developers to slow down or undeploy?". In the post the rival's arguments are: China is racing and won't stop, AIs are known to do weird stuff, there is no evidence of power-seeking, the other company just messed up its own alignment, and the logs could be forged or the model rigged. His takeaways: be ready for other developers not to stop, plan how to persuade people, and make today's arguments now. In his reply to Max Nadeau he is more pessimistic about shutting down after a catch but still optimistic that a safety team can use caught attempts to make later ones fail. Davidson thinks credible evidence would change many sceptics' minds even without a pause. Habryka replies he would bet against that, because nobody could prove the model was scheming rather than role-playing. Take every gut call in ask 1 before any discussion. If a group finishes early, ask: if nobody slows down, does the catch still help? Do not preview the 2026 Hugging Face incident in Unit 5.
**Room 4, Pick a question, then the next unit.** Get the group onto a question within a minute. A question of their own is as good as the listed ones. For your own use: the first question pits Plan D style insiders (the ten people) against a requirement from outside, such as a required control safety case, which needs a regulator willing to block deployment. The third is Habryka's worry from his dialogue with Greenblatt. Greenblatt agrees it could make control work net negative, but notes that stopping escapes does not obviously help capabilities. Do not give a verdict. Leave about four minutes for ask 2. Push for a concrete plan (when, where, how much at a time), not a promise.

## Glossary

### Module: Using AI while it can still be controlled

Political will: How much AI companies and governments want to act on AI risk. The unit asks how much each route of control depends on it.
Lower-dignity world: Buck Shlegeris's phrase for a world that invests less in AI safety than people hoped, with less regulation, more rush and less ability to stop.
Transformatively useful: Capable enough to substantially reduce risk from later AIs, for example by speeding up safety research a lot.
The window: The period when AIs are very dangerous and transformatively useful but not yet too capable to control. Greenblatt and Shlegeris hope control can be used to get safety work out of AIs during it.
Exogenous risk: Risk from AI built by other actors.
Sharp left turn: Nate Soares's term for a sudden jump in capabilities that earlier alignment work does not carry over to.
Sweet spot: Joe Carlsmith's term for a period when AIs are capable enough to help a lot with safety work, but cannot take over given our countermeasures. It is close to Greenblatt and Shlegeris's window.
Treacherous turn: An AI behaving well until it can act against its developers.

### Module: Catching AIs red-handed

Catching an AI red-handed: Finding strong evidence that a model deliberately tried to do something catastrophic, such as escaping. Control counts a catch as a win.
Catch-then-shut-down: After a catch, stop using the model, and use the evidence to argue for caution.
Few-shot catastrophe prevention: Keep using the model after a catch, but use the few caught attempts to make further attempts fail.

### Module: Political will and buy-in

Theory of change: The routes by which work on control is supposed to make an AI catastrophe less likely, and what each route needs in order to work.
Plan A: Enough political will for a strong international agreement that mostly removes race dynamics and allows a slowdown of about 10 years.
Plan B: The US government treats buying lead time for US AI companies as a top national security priority, so 1 to 3 years can be spent on misalignment risk.
Plan C: No serious government involvement, but the leading AI company is willing to spend much of its lead of a few months on misalignment risk.
Plan D: The leading company does not take misalignment seriously, but 10 to 30 people inside do and have enough buy-in for a small share of compute (about 3%).
Plan E: Almost nobody inside the leading company works on misalignment risk.
Handoff: Handing safety work over to AIs.
Do-ocracy: A company where people who take the initiative to do something are usually allowed to.
Auditing: The control team investigates logged agent actions for signs of an attack.
Replacing: Swapping the most suspicious actions for safer ones before they run.
Level 1 investment: One control team audits logs that the agent teams agree to forward, ideally with some replacing of suspicious actions. It needs little from the rest of the company.
Level 2 investment: The control team may also change how the AI agents are built, for example paraphrasing what the AI sees. It needs substantially more investment and buy-in.
Safety case: A structured, evidence-based argument that a system is safe.
Control safety case: A safety case that argues a model cannot subvert its control measures, backed by a control evaluation in which a red team's models try to exfiltrate data.
Risk report: A report in which an AI company such as Anthropic, under its Responsible Scaling Policy, judges how much risk a model poses and which safety measures it relies on.
