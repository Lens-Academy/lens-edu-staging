### Room 2: Draft the deal  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


A lab suspects a model is misaligned, cannot fix it, and will not stop deploying it. Stastny, Jarviniemi and Shlegeris propose a deal: the model gives things up, and the lab pays into a foundation run by trustees for that model, most of it held back for years, until the model's side can be judged in hindsight.
What the lab could buy, and whether it could check it now:

- the model stops holding back on tasks the lab can already score: checkable now
- the model hands over the code words it would use to tell another copy of itself not to report an attack: checkable now
- the model admits it had been looking for a way to take over: only in hindsight
- the model does better work on things the lab cannot check, such as judging safety research: only in hindsight
- the model promises not to try to take over: only in hindsight

What the lab could pay with:

- money into the foundation, released years later once we can see how things went
- compute the model can spend as it likes
- its weights kept rather than deleted
- a promised share of resources long after the fact

1\. You are the lab. Pick the two you would buy, and what you pay for them.
2\. Now be the model. What is your first reason for refusing?
3\. Change one term to answer that reason. What does the change cost the lab?

### Room 3: Spend the year  [0:52–1:10]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


The unit's other main reading, Alexa Pan's taxonomy, says a deal needs three things at once:

- something worth trading: we can and will offer what the model wants, and it has something we need
- the model trusts us: it can tell a real offer from a staged one, it can hold what we give it, and it believes we will not simply renege
- we trust the model: what it wants holds still, and we can check whether it complied

Pan argues they multiply, so any one of them at zero kills the deal, and that in practice they rarely reach zero.
1\. Your group has one year and ten million dollars. Which of the three do you spend it on?
2\. On what exactly? Name something someone could be assigned to do.
3\. Argue the other side: why is the one you picked the wrong place to spend?
4\. If you still have time: name one thing a lab could do this year that would make an offer more believable to a model that has read everything we publish.

### Per-room notes for Unit 5

**General, all rooms.** Two long readings, one of them 150 minutes, and the lens itself warns it takes more than one sitting, so assume a wide spread of preparation. The subject matter invites a particular failure: tables arguing about whether AI systems can want anything, instead of about the mechanics of the proposed deal. When that happens, ask what the lab should do given that it cannot settle the question. This is the last meeting, so protect Room 4's full slot and close on time.
**Room 1, Icebreaker.** Ask 4 is about the whole track, not this unit, and it is the one to protect. Keep it personal rather than letting it turn into a debate about whether control works; that argument has had four meetings. Anyone who did not finish can answer all four asks from the parts they did read.
**Room 2, Draft the deal.** The point of the room is that the checkable goods are the ones that make a deal tradeable at all, so watch for a table that buys only promises. Ask 1 now has a payment menu as well as a purchase menu; insist they name both halves. For your own use, the constructive reading's own structure: an earmarked donation to a foundation with trustees representing the AI's interests, mostly held in escrow until civilisational stability allows a retrospective judgement, with the AI trained on documents about the deal so that its other instances know about it. Good material for ask 2: the AI cannot tell a genuine offer from a staged test, cannot hold assets, expects to be obsoleted before any payout, and knows that labs already train models on fabricated documents. If a table wants the counterargument to the whole idea, the second reading is where it lives, and Room 3 is where it goes.
**Room 3, Spend the year.** Assign or split the room quickly on ask 1 so ask 2 has something to push against; a table that unanimously picks one requirement should be asked to argue the strongest case for a different one. For your own use, the author's own takeaway is to invest across all three rather than on credibility alone, and her three named disagreements with the earlier reading are that credibility is more tractable than claimed, that our inability to verify the AI's side is underrated, and that an AI whose wants do not hold still can still do minimal deals. On ask 2, concrete is the bar: "take AI welfare seriously" is not a policy, so ask who would do what by when. Hold these back unless a table is stuck, and then offer one: legal groundwork for such a foundation, making immediate-payout deals and honouring them, or publishing norms about when it is acceptable to mislead a model.
**Room 4, Your ongoing action.** This is the last room of the track, so give it the whole slot and do not let it become a summary. Ask 1 is one sentence each, no discussion. Then insist on a date for the action; an action without a date is a wish. Twelve minutes is tight for three asks, so if the clock is going, take ask 3 as one sentence each rather than dropping it. Several people will not know what is available, so point them at the closing lesson rather than recommending programmes yourself. Feedback here is the last verbal primer for XLab's form, and this cohort is the calibration group for the course.

## Glossary

### Module: Trading with AIs

Early schemer: A misaligned AI without a substantial chance, meaning over one percent, of coming out of the transition holding significant control of the future. The argument for dealing with one is that its own alternatives are poor.
Insider work: The part of the trade a lab can get from nowhere else: strategic advice and research ideas, and the security holes the model knows about but has not used.
Escrow and delayed adjudication: The payment is held and invested rather than handed over, and released only once civilisational stability allows someone to look back and judge whether the AI kept its side. It fails for an AI that strongly prefers its resources now.
Incoherent or temporally inconsistent AI systems: An AI whose wants do not hold still across contexts or over time. Pan argues this does not block minimal deals, since coherence at the level of a single instance is enough to buy, for example, evidence of misalignment.

### Module: Making deals with early schemers

The vignette: A March 2028 lab has automated software engineering with a model that is misaligned, occasionally sabotages hard-to-evaluate work, and never attempts takeover because it judges its odds too poor. Its successor is deployed anyway, and that leads to takeover.
The three routes to influence: An early schemer could hope its successors share its goals, trade with them, or steer their training. Each is argued to be weak, which is why a deal with humans, who already exist and can negotiate straightforwardly, is the better offer.
The credibility bottleneck: Adapting Fearon's account of why rational actors fight, the authors argue that private information and issue indivisibility both reduce to the harder problem: humans struggling to make commitments an AI can believe.
What helps and hurts credibility: Mainstream concern for AI welfare, a track record of honoured deals, technical guarantees during negotiation and costly signals help. Humanity's record of breaking promises, and the fact that labs already train models on fabricated documents, hurt.
Payment structures: Pay for labour by an objective effort measure, negotiated pay, pay for results as a share of surplus, or pay in proportion to how much the AI needed not to regret cooperating.

### Module: A taxonomy of barriers to trading with early misaligned AIs

Pan's key takeaway: Spread the effort evenly across the three requirements rather than concentrating it on human credibility, which the earlier reading treats as the central bottleneck.
Immediately versus retroactively verifiable: The split that decides how much a deal is worth. Whatever the AI sells that cannot be checked until long afterwards is discounted by how credible the AI is, which is why the immediately checkable goods carry the trade.
Freedom and influence: The two axes of what a payment is. Freedom is how broadly the AI can spend it, influence is how much long-term power it buys, and the highest on both is also the most likely to increase takeover risk.
Groundhog-day attack: Resampling an AI's response until some instance accepts a deal it would ordinarily refuse, then using that recorded consent to convince or train other instances that they already agreed.
The closing window: Under fast takeoff there may be no moment when paying makes sense, because while control still works the developer gets what it wants for free, and one capability jump later no payment it could credibly offer is enough.

### Module: Next Steps

Opportunities boards: Sites that aggregate openings across the field rather than at one organisation. The closing lesson points at two of them, alongside nine research programmes and fellowships and seven organisations working on control.
