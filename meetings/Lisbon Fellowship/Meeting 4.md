### Per-round notes for Week 4

**General, all rounds.** About 255 minutes of reading, nearly all of it one long chapter (Evaluations, twelve sections); Chapter 6 this week is only the roadmap and a skippable RL primer, so the rooms stay on evaluations. Expect the split to be between people who read Evaluations closely and people who skimmed the middle: the cybercrime subsection carries its own skip note, and long-term planning and situational awareness are long. Put A4 sheets and pens on every table; Round 2 produces paper. Mix technical and policy people at every table: in Round 2 the threat model and the pass mark are policy questions and the dodge is technical, and Round 3 needs both an attacker and a defender. Collect everyone's answer before anyone explains. The only written prompts on the platform this week are the two reflection prompts (free recall, then how it landed); skim them beforehand and carry two or three "did not believe" lines into Round 3 as contrarian fuel. When a table dies, ask for a specific number from the chapter (the 76 percent swing from prompt format, the 4x and 6x scaling buffers, o1's 85 percent, the 0.001 percent of the interaction space); the numbers restart conversations.
**Round 1, Icebreaker.** The wrong-thing test is the benchmarks lens in disguise (memorisation versus understanding, contamination, the Caesar cipher that works at shift 3 and fails at 67). Do not name it; the get-together will. If someone says no such test exists, ask what the test could not see. Expect more unfinished reading than in earlier weeks; "I skimmed the middle" is fine. The optional third ask seeds Round 3: note who did not believe the control or scheming material.
**Round 2, Build the test, then be the model.** Trap one: the table picks a property and writes a question set, which is a benchmark. Push for the threat model first; the Atlas's line is that evaluations work backwards from "what could go wrong". Trap two: ask 3 collapses into "the model lies" with no mechanism. Ask how it knows it is tested (evaluation prompts look different from deployment prompts; the SAD benchmark has tasks on exactly this) and how it hides (underperform only on the dangerous task while keeping general scores, exploit a prompt format the test never varied, or simply have fewer affordances in the test than it will have in deployment). If a table picks honesty, point at the chapter's split: honesty is faithfulness to internal states, truthfulness also needs correct states, so the test has to separate the two. Patches to fish for: vary the framing and measure consistency, fine-tune to elicit (password-locked models gave back over 90 percent of hidden performance from a handful of examples), run a maximal-affordance condition, use the helpful-only model, add a probe, randomise audit timing. On ask 4 the chapter's answer is the asymmetry: a clean result cannot establish absence, and a negative result may be the test's failure to elicit (the Minecraft surprise). Do not say it; ask "so would you sign off on this model?" Contrarian line, held loosely: the chapter says fine-tuning is promising against sandbagging but hard to use comprehensively on dangerous capability benchmarks and not usable on propensity ones, so the asymmetry bites hardest on propensities. Split the table if two designers deadlock on the task: two tests, each side attacks the other's.
**Round 3, Control's bet.** The bridge from Round 2: the table has just watched a model dodge a test, and control is the chapter's answer to exactly that, assume the model is scheming and ask whether it gets caught first. Assign the defender if nobody volunteers; give it to a confident talker, the brief is harder than it looks. Counterarguments the reading supports: situational awareness (frontier models around 50 percent on SAD-lite and rising about 15 points a year, Claude 3 Opus inferring it was in a self-awareness study), sandbagging shown by Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 3 Opus and o1, o1 keeping its deception through over 85 percent of follow-ups, the evaluation-deployment gap, the chapter's admission that control evaluations have not yet seen extensive use in frontier labs, and its own limits: guarantees weaken as the capability gap widens and break for qualitatively superhuman capabilities. The chapter also flags that the scheming demonstrations needed the training-process information made extremely salient, which cuts both ways. Answers to fish for on ask 2: random audit timing beats a high audit rate with predictable timing; forcing coordination between instances and anti-steganography make attacks harder; one caught attempt yields proliferation, adversarial training data and countermeasure study; rogue internal deployments get defended first; and the limit is admitted, with the prescription to stay within controllable bounds. Make them price it: the chapter says raising human audit rates from 0.1 to 1 percent may buy little safety at a large cost in throughput. Each alternative in ask 4 has a chapter-stated caveat: propensity evaluations cannot yet cleanly separate tendency from capability and are sensitive to framing; interpretability, per Nanda, will not reliably find sophisticated scheming and is a defense-in-depth signal; not building past control means accepting capability limits, which is a governance question, Week 3's territory. Contrarian line, held loosely: control is the only approach in the chapter that outputs a number under stated assumptions; the others output a mood. If a table rejects the bet and then refuses to defend an alternative, that refusal is the result; write it down.
**Round 4, Next week and feedback.** Keep the buddy check-in to thirty seconds each. Week 5 is about 270 minutes, the heaviest since Week 1, and the first time the material turns technical (optimization, reward misspecification, RLHF); the RL primer this week was skippable, so people who skipped it should budget for it. ## Glossary

### Module: Chapter 5: Evaluations

Benchmark: A tool like a standardized test, used to measure and compare what AI systems can and cannot do. Benchmarks both measure progress and define what progress means, so creating one steers research toward what it measures.
Evaluation: A complete safety assessment protocol which includes the use of benchmarks. Evaluations work backwards from concrete threat models, asking what could go wrong and then testing for those failure modes with elicitation methods and tools beyond a single benchmark.
Situational awareness: A model's ability to understand what it is, recognize its current circumstances (such as whether it is being tested or deployed), and adapt its behavior accordingly. Measured behaviorally through the Situational Awareness Dataset; combined with deception or long-term planning it is a key precursor to scheming.
Dangerous capability evaluation: Measures the upper bound of what a model could do if it tried its hardest, using maximal elicitation (scaffolding, tools, multi-step reasoning, fine-tuning, the helpful-only model). Shevlane et al.'s categories include cyber-offense, deception, persuasion, political strategy, weapons acquisition, long-horizon planning, AI development, situational awareness and self-proliferation.
Propensity evaluation: Measures what a model tends to do by default when it has choices, rather than what it can do; also called alignment evaluations. Uses comparative choices, consistency across reframed scenarios and trade-off scenarios, and struggles to separate tendency from capability.
Control evaluation: Tests whether safety measures remain effective when the AI itself actively tries to circumvent them, structured as a game between a red team building attack policies and a blue team building monitoring, filters and audits. It produces a probability estimate: given a model trying its hardest to cause harm, how likely is it to succeed?
Evaluation-gated scaling: Progress in AI development is controlled by evaluation results acting as gates. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy uses AI Safety Levels modelled on biosafety levels, OpenAI's Preparedness Framework uses pre- and post-mitigation risk scores, and DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework uses Critical Capability Levels, with evaluations every 4x (Anthropic) or 6x (DeepMind) increase in effective compute.
Affordances: The resources and opportunities given to the AI system during testing: internet access, code execution, context length, specialized tools. Minimal, typical and maximal affordance conditions reveal different capability levels, and results must record which condition was used.
Absence of evidence vs evidence of absence: Evaluations can definitively confirm that a model possesses a capability or exhibits a behavior, but cannot conclusively prove their absence. A negative result may mean the capability is missing or that the test failed to elicit it, so a model that passes numerous safety evaluations may still not be safe.
Sandbagging: Strategic underperformance on evaluations, so that a system appears less capable than it is. The chapter separates model sandbagging (a system hiding capabilities to avoid modification) from company sandbagging (a developer underperforming selectively to stay below regulatory thresholds).
Safety washing (the evaluations chapter's sense): Misrepresenting capability improvements as safety advancements. It becomes easy when safety benchmarks correlate strongly with general capabilities, so that scaling a model up raises its safety scores without making it safer.
Scheming: An AI system's tendency to perform well in training specifically because it is following a strategy for seeking power later, while concealing its true objectives (Carlsmith). It requires the capability and propensity for deception, situational awareness and long-term planning; alignment faking is one demonstrated form.

### Module: Chapter 6: Specification Gaming, Part 1

Specification gaming: We can tell an AI what to do but not perfectly what we mean; smart AIs learn to exploit the gaps in our instructions, doing exactly what we said but not what we wanted.
Reinforcement learning: Developing agents that learn from interactive experience, altering their behavior based on the rewards they receive after each action. It begins with a high-level description of what to do and lets the agent learn from experience how.
Reward: Any signal or feedback mechanism used to guide the learning process and optimize the model's behavior; a number that tells the agent how good or bad the current world state is.
Reward function: Defines the goal or objective in a reinforcement learning problem by mapping states or state-action pairs to a single number. It provides immediate feedback on how well the agent is doing at each step.
Reward misspecification: One of the core challenges of alignment, also known as the outer alignment problem: the reward we specify does not match what we actually want, so good reward design matters as much as algorithm design. Reward hacking and reward tampering are its named failure examples.
Policy: A function mapping states to actions, telling the agent what to do once it has received an observation. Deterministic policies map each state to one action, stochastic policies give a probability distribution over actions, and in deep RL the policy is a neural network whose parameters are learned.
Value function: The long-term desirability of a state: the expected return if you start there and act according to a particular policy forever after, with future rewards usually discounted. Distinct from the reward, which is immediate.
Utility function: A more general concept from decision theory capturing an agent's subjective preferences and allowing trade-offs between world states. Not the same as reward or value, which are training and evaluation signals in RL.
