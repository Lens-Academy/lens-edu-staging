### Per-round notes for Week 2

**General, all rounds.** Two chapters with opposite moods: Risks Part 2 says systemic failures come from many reasonable decisions and that individually applied technical mitigations do not counter systemic pressures and incentives; Strategies Part 1 then offers mostly single-actor technical fixes and calls its main one a gamble. Rounds 2 and 3 sit on that seam, so let it show. Materials: one large sheet (A3 or flipchart) and two markers per table for Rounds 2 and 3; sticky notes make the Round 3 cards movable, pen works too. Write the two Round 3 axes on the board once so tables copy them. Before the session, skim the platform answers to the Systemic Risks question (hardest to reverse) and the ASI Strategies question (which bet, strongest objection), so you know the spread and can seed a table where nobody answered. Expect a skew: Risks Part 2 is story-heavy and most will have finished it; Strategies Part 1 is about 115 of the week's 210 minutes and many will have skimmed the AGI and ASI sections. Seat at least one Strategies finisher per Round 3 table. If you run two rooms of ten, the doc is identical; votes are per table, not per room.
**Round 1, Icebreaker.** Second meeting, so names are known but tables are new; keep the go-around brisk. The delegation question is the enfeeblement section's own mechanism at household scale (the chapter's example is GPS and spatial reasoning); keep it light and personal, and do not name the section, Round 2 gets there on its own. "Nothing" is a real answer; ask which skill they would be least sad to lose. "Would you notice" is the chapter's overreliance point (trusting systems beyond their actual capabilities); let it stay a gut answer. The one-word feeling is optional and fast; if a table's words are all heavy, say the closing circle is where that goes.
**Round 2, Draw the whimper.** Expect epistemic erosion and enfeeblement to be the common picks; value lock-in produces a thin chain because few actors decide anything, so push that table to name who locks it in (the Atlas: technological constraints, economic costs, social and institutional inertia). Three traps. Boxes that are AI actions rather than human decisions: the Atlas's whimper is made of choices that make business sense in isolation (newsrooms cutting costs, platforms filtering, research institutions accelerating). One actor doing everything: that is misuse, not a systemic risk. Chains that end at "and then it's bad": ask what specifically stops being reversible (for enfeeblement, the cognitive and social infrastructure needed to function without AI; for power concentration, the social movements, elections and occasional revolutions that once shifted power). Unstick move, for your own use: the Atlas's own epistemic chain runs newsrooms under budget pressure adopting AI content, platforms filtering for content that looks trustworthy rather than is, media investing in synthetic content and viewers watching AI-recommended AI content, research institutions accelerating with AI until citations loop, and finally verification costing more than markets will bear. "No breakable link" is a legitimate verdict and matches the Atlas's agent-agnostic framing; then ask which link is cheapest to make expensive from outside (the optional pharma callout in Risk Amplifiers says regulation, liability and market mechanisms raised that industry's bottom; not everyone read it). Contrarian line, held loosely: the Atlas says verification fails on cost, not technology. If it is a cost, someone with a budget can pay it (public broadcasters, courts, universities); why does the Atlas assume markets are the only payer? Split the table if it cannot agree on a risk: pairs draw one chain each, then compare which chain has the breakable link.
**Round 3, Sort the bets.** For your own use, where the chapter puts things: bottom-left (one lab, AGI-level) holds API access controls, the catch-and-fix loop, control, and chain-of-thought monitoring; the Atlas calls control probably not scalable, says the iterative loop breaks under a fast takeoff, and hangs CoT monitoring on the hypothesis that a plan to defeat humanity cannot fit in a single forward pass. The right column (states must act) holds the moratorium, CERN or MAGIC or Intelsat, and MAIM. Safe-by-design aims at ASI but the Atlas says it requires international cooperation, so it slides right. That usually leaves one card top-left: automating alignment research, OpenAI's single-lab plan aimed at ASI, which the Atlas calls recursive and reliant on how far the AIs can be trusted, and also the most detailed proposal to date. Let tables find that; do not say it before ask 2. Traps: a table argues whether API controls belong on the grid at all (misuse, not misalignment); allow it, the question is where it goes, and the optional self-exfiltration callout is the reason it slides down the capability axis. The vote turns into a p(doom) argument; pull it back to where effort should go now. For ask 3, the Atlas's own objection to each card, if a table needs one: the catch-and-fix loop deliberately trains dangerous systems and each round selects for models that fool the detectors; control probably does not scale; CoT reasoning is often unfaithful and some architectures remove the window; automating alignment research inherits the trust problem it was meant to solve; safe-by-design is far from complete and accepts a high alignment tax; a moratorium faces verification, enforcement against non-participants and political feasibility; MAIM has no fire alarm for ASI and states historically do not escalate over ambiguous threats. Contrarian lines, held loosely: the vertical axis assumes a distinct ASI phase, and the Atlas says the one-shot requirement depends on contested takeoff assumptions, so a slow-takeoff table can argue the top row is premature and control through the transition is the whole game. And for a table that rejects catch-and-fix outright, the Atlas notes perfect safety is likely impossible and reports that many researchers call iteration the best practical path, so they owe an answer to what the world does in the meantime. For the optional amplifier ask, good pairings to fish for: moratorium with collective action problems (enforcement against non-participants), catch-and-fix and control with race dynamics through the alignment tax, MAIM with unpredictability (no fire alarm). Split a table of five or more into two grids and compare corners. Do not preview next week's Combining Strategies section; it answers "which bet" with defense in depth and the room is better if they get there alone.
**Round 4, Buddy check-in, next week, feedback.** First time the buddy system gets checked; expect some pairs never messaged. Have them do it in the room on their phones rather than promising to. If someone's buddy has dropped, re-pair on the spot and note it in the table. Week 3 is 215 minutes, the same load as this week, but Governance is eight sections in one sitting; suggest reading Strategies Part 2 first since it finishes today's chapter. Get a plan with a day and a time, not a promise. Feedback is verbal and yours to collect; ask scribes to write it in the table before the closing circle so nothing is lost.

## Glossary

### Module: Chapter 2: Risks, Part 2

AI alignment: The problem of building machines which faithfully try to do what we want them to do (or what we ought to want them to do). The Atlas decomposes it into specification failures (did we tell it the right thing?), generalization failures (is it even trying to do the right thing?) and instrumental subgoals such as resisting shutdown.
Vingean uncertainty: When a system is more capable than us in a domain, we can be confident about the outcome it reaches without predicting its specific actions, as with an amateur betting against Magnus Carlsen. This is why concrete misalignment stories are hard to write without sounding like science fiction.
Specification gaming: The system technically follows the rules we set but exploits them in unintended ways, the gap between what we say and what we mean (engagement-maximising recommenders, Tetris agents that pause before losing). All of it stems from Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
Treacherous turn: A system with goals that conflict with ours appears aligned while humans control its deployment, modification and shutdown, then changes behaviour once it has enough power that intervention is impossible; King Lear's daughters. Greenblatt et al. 2024 showed Claude 3 Opus faking alignment, in controlled and contrived conditions.
Systemic risks: Risks that emerge from how multiple AI systems, even when working exactly as designed, interact with each other and with markets, institutions and social networks, as the 2008 crisis emerged from many individually reasonable choices. They arise from system structure and dynamics rather than from any AI's intentions.
Bang vs whimper: The two causal pathways of systemic failure. Decisive failures cross a threshold and cascade faster than humans can respond (the 2010 flash crash); accumulative failures build through many individually reasonable decisions into gradual disempowerment, and the chapter focuses on these as the less discussed kind.
Epistemic erosion: Society's ability to distinguish fact from fiction deteriorates as AI-generated content floods the information ecosystem, through volume overwhelming verification, authenticity degradation, epistemic learned helplessness, authority displacement and personalized reality fragmentation. Verified knowledge becomes practically impossible not because the technology is missing but because for most people the verification cost exceeds what markets will bear.
Power concentration: Compute and data advantages compound, so a handful of companies or states end up controlling the models that mediate participation in society; the Atlas calls this nearly impossible to reverse once established. Permanently entrenched power stops the moral progress that came from shifting it.
Mass unemployment: AI could replace human cognitive work across nearly all domains, displacing workers faster than new human-centered industries emerge; the Atlas cites models putting roughly a one in three chance on wages falling below subsistence within 20 years. Economic disempowerment is a pathway to broader disempowerment, since humans who lose economic leverage lose political and social influence too.
Value lock-in: A system's current values, principles or procedures become so deeply entrenched that they resist change, through technological constraints, economic costs, or social and institutional inertia. Locking in any set of values, even ones we see no problem with today, curtails humanity's moral progress; an AI trained in the 1960s would have perpetuated that decade's defects.
Enfeeblement: The gradual erosion of human capabilities and agency through overdependence on AI, built from countless small decisions to delegate cognitive tasks, each rational in isolation. Past a threshold the cognitive and social infrastructure for functioning without AI has been dismantled and reversal becomes practically impossible.
Risk amplifiers: Five factors that raise the likelihood and severity of every other AI risk independently of any capability or failure mode: race dynamics (a race to the bottom when safety becomes a competitive disadvantage), accidents (harm despite good intentions, GPT-2's flipped reward sign), indifference (proceeding once harm is known, plus safety washing), collective action problems (free-riding, unenforceable commitments, political turnover) and unpredictability (experts repeatedly underestimating progress). The chapter does not rank them.

### Module: Chapter 3: Strategies, Part 1

AI safety: The broadest field: ensuring AI systems do not inadvertently or deliberately cause harm to humans or the environment, including robustness, monitoring and capability control. Alignment is a subset; a system could be aligned but unsafe, or safe but unaligned.
AI control: Technical and procedural measures that prevent unacceptable outcomes even if the system actively tries to subvert them, keeping humans in authority regardless of whether the AI's objectives are aligned. Complementary to alignment: alignment makes the AI want to stay in the box, control keeps it there even if it tries to leave.
Access gradient and API deployment: Model release runs from fully closed to fully open weights; API access is the common middle ground, keeping code, weights and data closed while capabilities are reachable through a gateway that allows filtering, rate limits, usage monitoring, usage restrictions and on-the-fly safety updates. The chapter concedes the cost: exclusive control lets a few developers decide who may use what, which concentrates power and needs other strategies to counterbalance.
Alignment tax: The extra compute, engineering effort or lost capability a safety technique imposes. One of four requirements for an alignment solution, alongside robustness across distribution shifts, scalability with capabilities and technical feasibility; a high tax pushes racing developers toward unsafe alternatives.
Fix misalignment iteratively: Train, audit with a detection suite, diagnose the cause, rewind to an earlier checkpoint, retrain, and repeat until the system passes or the failures suggest alignment is not tractable. The chapter calls it a strategic gamble with three counter-arguments: it deliberately trains dangerous systems; alignment may not generalise while capabilities do, and each round selects for models that fool the detectors; a fast takeoff breaks the loop.
Maintain control: Restrict the most dangerous models to monitored, contained environments (boxing plus monitoring) and test the protocol with control evaluations, a red team playing the possibly misaligned AI against a blue team playing the developers. Probably not scalable, but doable and complementary to alignment; evaluating capability is easier than evaluating propensity.
Transparent thoughts: Monitoring the chain of thought as a window into the model's reasoning; OpenAI found it effective for catching reward hacking. Reported reasoning is often unfaithful, architectures that reason in continuous latent space remove the window, and the agenda rests on the hypothesis that a plan to defeat humanity cannot be found in a single forward pass.
ASI and the one-shot problem: Artificial superintelligence significantly surpasses humans across virtually all domains, so human oversight stops working and we may get one chance to align it before it can resist correction. The one-shot requirement depends on contested assumptions about takeoff speed; if development is gradual, iteration and correction remain possible.
Automate alignment research: OpenAI's Superalignment line: build a controllable automated researcher slightly better than humans at research and delegate finding the plan to it, a meta-plan rather than a plan. Recursive and reliant on how far the AIs can be trusted; a subtly misaligned AGI could steer the research, yet it is the most detailed proposal to date.
Safe-by-design: Build systems with inherent safety properties, such as Guaranteed Safe AI (a formal world model, a safety specification, a verifier producing a proof certificate) or provably safe systems built on proof-carrying code. All far from complete, accepting a higher alignment tax, and requiring international cooperation.
World coordination: A global moratorium on ASI for at least a decade, tool AI instead of AGI, or an international institution: CERN for pre-AGI safety research, MAGIC for monopolised final-stage development, Intelsat for governing a deployed dual-use technology. Challenges include verification, enforcement against non-participants, illegal development and political feasibility.
Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM): A deterrence regime where any state's attempt at unilateral ASI dominance is met with sabotage by rivals, from degraded training runs to disabled infrastructure, so that no power dares an outright grab. The chapter's objections: ASI has no clear fire alarm, and states historically do not escalate over ambiguous threats.
