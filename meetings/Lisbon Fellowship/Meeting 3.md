## Participant FAQ

Quick answers for the in-person session. Everything you actually do is in the session doc.

### 🆘 Getting help during a round

Your facilitator can't be at every table at once, so:

- **Confused about the task, or want a human?** Wave your facilitator over; they circulate between tables.
- **Don't get the question, or something in the reading?** Ask the [Lens Coach](https://lensacademy.org/coach). It's for understanding, not for handing you the answer.
- **Lost the doc link, or a quick logistics thing?** Check the fellowship **Discord channel**.

### Before you arrive

- **I didn't finish (or start) the reading, should I come?** Yes. Come anyway and just say so in Round 1; it's normal, and there's no penalty.
- **I'm running late.** Come in quietly, sign in, and join the table your facilitator points you to; someone will catch you up.

### At your table

- **Do I have to talk?** Jump in when you've got a thought; there's no need to wait to be called on.
- **What's a "scribe"?** One person per table jots the names and your answer into the table in the doc. A line or two is plenty; rotate it each round if you like.
- **We finished early / ran out of things to say.** Wave your facilitator over for ideas to expand your current conversation.
- **No one's talking, or one person is dominating.** Just start talking when you have a thought; if it's really stuck, wave your facilitator over.
- **Do we need a "right answer"?** No! The point is the discussion, not a tidy answer.

### Between rounds

- **Where do we share what we came up with?** At the get-together after each round. Everyone comes back as one room and anyone who wants to share what came up at their table can.
- **Do I have to share?** No. Nobody is called on, no table is put on the spot, and a quiet get-together is a fine outcome.
- **Do we go around at the start of a round?** No. Take names and start straight in on the prompt; the sharing happens at the get-together.

### The reading and the questions

- **I don't understand the question or a claim.** Ask the [Lens Coach](https://lensacademy.org/coach)! It'll explain in plain terms.
- **Where's the reading?** It's in the course on the Lens platform: the chapter text is loaded right in the lesson. Can't find it? Ask the Coach or your facilitator.

### Tech

- **I can't find the doc, or I can't type in it.** The link is in Discord; open it on a laptop or phone, signed in with your enrolled account. Still stuck? Ask your facilitator.
- **I don't have a laptop with me.** Share with a tablemate; one device per table is enough for the scribe.

### After we wrap

- **What happens at 2:00?** We close on time. If the room is still ours, hang around and keep talking if you'd like, no pressure.
- **How do accountability buddies work?** You paired up in the first session; your buddy checks in with you before each meeting. Swap whatever the last room asked you to take away: a plan, a next step, or simply what changed your mind.

## Facilitator Pro-Tips:

**1. Skeptic Unlock:** when a table stalls on **“AI won’t ever get that smart”**


Some tables flatten when the group agrees AI will never become that capable. The discussion dies because there’s nothing left to argue. When that happens, show the trend:


- Pull up the [METR time-horizons graph](https://metr.org/time-horizons/) and discuss pace of task-length growth.
- A possible line: *“It’s not necessary for it to be smart today. Look at the slope, this is what an exponential looks like, and here’s where a few more doublings land us.”*
- Hand it back to the table: “Given this curve, what would change your mind, in either direction?”


**The goal is not to win an argument**. It is to turn a closed “no” into an open “huh, let’s take a look at this” so the discussion has somewhere to go.



**2. Doom Reframe:** when the material hits hard and **the table turns fatalistic**


Some tables end heavy. The content genuinely rattles people (participants have talked about getting anxious and doom-scrolling after the reading), and a discussion can settle into “we’re doomed” with nowhere left to go. When that happens, don’t let it end there:


- Name it, then reframe: doom predictions assume today’s tools and limits, but the people working on this in the future will have new capabilities and methods we can’t see yet.
- Reach for the [Malthusian fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malthusianism): everyone predicted mass starvation from population growth, then farming methods leapt ahead and it never happened. A predicted catastrophe is not a guaranteed one.
- A possible line: *“It’s not all lost. The best thing any of us can do is work on it, so let’s not despair.”*
- **Hand it back to agency:** “*Given that, what’s one thing within your reach, a skill, a question, a next step?*”


**The goal is not to talk anyone out of their concern or paper over the risk.** It is to channel that energy into agency so people leave motivated rather than paralyzed.

## Glossary

### Module: Chapter 3: Strategies, Part 2

Socio-technical problem: AI safety is fundamentally a socio-technical problem requiring socio-technical solutions; technical measures can be undermined by inadequate governance, poor security practices within labs, or cultures that prioritize speed over caution. Responsibility is distributed, so no single actor controls all the variables.
Defense-in-depth: Layering multiple independent protections so that if one fails, others provide backup, like a medieval castle's walls, moats and towers; also known as the Swiss cheese model of safety. No single measure is perfect, but multiple imperfect defenses together can create robust protection.
Layer independence: The core requirement of defense-in-depth: if each of five layers fails 1 percent of the time and all must be breached, overall failure is 0.01 to the fifth, but if breaking one layer breaks others, or attackers learn which layer failed, they brute force each barrier in linear time. AI defenses are often correlated: the same underlying model, shared blind spots.
Defensive acceleration (d/acc): A strategic philosophy of prioritizing technologies that strengthen defense and social resilience (cyber-, bio-, info- and physical defense). The d stands for defensive, differential and decentralized; its feasibility hinges on the offense-defense balance, which history has settled differently in different fields.
Warning shot: A clear demonstration of AI danger that some in the governance field believe might be necessary to galvanize decisive action, with skepticism that a convincing one could occur before it is too late. In one AI-2027 branch such an event, not a grand plan, is what gets the leading labs to slow down and add just enough technical mitigation.
Risk management: The operational process that keeps (Risks minus Mitigations) below Tolerance through identification, analysis, treatment and governance. Key Risk Indicators (capability thresholds) are paired with Key Control Indicators (mitigation targets) as if-then statements, and organizations must commit to pausing development if required KCIs cannot be achieved.
Three lines of defense: Operational managers who own daily risk decisions; specialized risk teams that advise and challenge; and internal audit, independent from peer pressure and reporting to the board, to verify the system works.
Safety culture: Organizations where people consistently prioritize safety over speed and where safety concerns can actually change decisions, with leadership accountability, systematic processes and psychological safety for raising concerns. The AI industry lacks the professional safety traditions of aerospace or nuclear, and its developers rarely face the consequences of their systems' failures.
Safety washing: Misleadingly portraying AI products, research or practices as safer or more aligned with safety goals than they actually are; the appearance of caring about safety without substance. It creates a false sense of security and misdirects funding and talent.
The illustrative sequence: One plausible strategic sequence, not a definitive roadmap: Step 1 foundational risk management and governance; Step 2 mitigating catastrophic misuse through access controls and d/acc; Step 3 control and alignment of AGI (transparent thoughts, control protocols, evaluations, pausing when audits reveal failures); Step 4 a robust ASI solution by using controlled AGI to automate alignment research, with world coordination and deterrence as last resorts. The Atlas calls the plan fragile.
Pre-paradigmatic: There is no single, universally accepted paradigm for AI safety; researchers disagree on the most likely threat models (sudden takeover versus gradual loss of control) and on the most promising solution paths.
Three tensions: Centralization versus decentralization, speed versus safety, and openness versus control; fundamental tensions the Atlas says persist throughout the strategic landscape.

### Module: Chapter 4: Governance

Three regulatory problems: Unexpected capabilities (emergent abilities that evaluations cannot rule out), deployment safety (repurposing through jailbreaks and agents) and proliferation (models are patterns of numbers that can be copied, stolen, leaked or distilled). Together they make traditional technology governance inadequate.
Governance targets: Effective intervention points are measurable (we can track and verify what is happening), controllable (concrete mechanisms exist to influence them) and meaningful (they shape capabilities and risks rather than superficial aspects). Compute and data are the upstream targets.
Compute chokepoints: NVIDIA designs most AI training chips, TSMC manufactures the most advanced ones, and ASML produces the only machines that can make them; Amazon, Microsoft and Google control about 65 percent of cloud computing. This concentration enables export controls, licensing and supply-chain monitoring.
Compute divide: Companies control over 80 percent of global AI computing capacity while governments and academia have fallen below 20 percent; academic researchers struggle to access even basic resources. Adding barriers could worsen this, concentrating power in a few large tech companies and reducing independent oversight.
Compute thresholds: The US Executive Order required notification of training runs above 10^26 operations; the EU AI Act sets 10^25 and adds risk assessments. Algorithmic efficiency, inference-time scaling and distillation make static thresholds less reliable, so the Atlas says they should be an initial screening mechanism, not the sole determinant of requirements.
Know-your-customer for compute: Cloud providers verify the identity and intentions of clients requesting large-scale compute, maintain records of significant usage and report suspicious patterns, as in financial services. Basic workload characteristics can be monitored without exposing model architecture or training data; open questions include legal mandates, verification criteria, escalation and jurisdictional fragmentation.
Race dynamics: A prisoner's-dilemma-like tension in which everyone would benefit from careful development but those who move fastest gain advantage, producing safetywashing, skipped red-teaming and a lowest-common-denominator approach to safety. Proposed exits include reciprocal snap-back limits, safety as a competitive asset, containment, agile multilateral oversight with a halt option, and secret-safe verification.
Three levels of governance: Corporate governance provides speed and technical expertise but cannot solve collective-action problems; national governance provides democratic legitimacy and enforcement power; international governance addresses cross-border externalities while facing strategic competition. Gaps at one level create pressure at the others.
Regulatory arbitrage: If one region implements strict safety requirements while others remain permissive, development may simply shift locations; a model can be trained in Singapore, deployed from Ireland and used globally. True global coverage requires more than powerful individual jurisdictions.
Frontier Safety Frameworks (FSFs): Company policies (Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, OpenAI's preparedness framework) with capability thresholds, model weight security, conditions for halting development or deployment, full capability elicitation, evaluation timing, accountability mechanisms and policy updates. A self-regulation mechanism that remains subject to companies' strategic interests.
Brussels Effect: Companies find it cheaper to adopt EU standards across their whole operations than to maintain separate versions, so EU regulation shapes global markets de facto, and other countries copy it de jure.
Safety case: A formal argument supported by evidence showing that a system meets safety thresholds for deployment, which a licensing regime would require before approval and track as systems evolve in deployment.
