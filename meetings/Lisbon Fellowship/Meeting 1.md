## Participant FAQ

Quick answers for the in-person session. Everything you actually do is in the session doc.

### 🆘 Getting help during a round

Your facilitator can't be at every table at once, so:

- **Confused about the task, or want a human?** Wave your facilitator over; they circulate between tables.
- **Don't get the question, or something in the reading?** Ask the [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor). It's for understanding, not for handing you the answer.
- **Lost the doc link, or a quick logistics thing?** Check the fellowship **Discord channel**.

### Before you arrive

- **I didn't finish (or start) the reading, should I come?** Yes. Come anyway and just say so in Round 1; it's normal, and there's no penalty.
- **I'm running late.** Come in quietly, sign in, and join the table your facilitator points you to; someone will catch you up.

### At your table

- **Do I have to talk?** Jump in when you've got a thought; there's no need to wait to be called on.
- **What's a "scribe"?** One person per table jots the names and your answer into the table in the doc. A line or two is plenty; rotate it each round if you like.
- **We finished early / ran out of things to say.** Wave your facilitator over for ideas to expand your current conversation.
- **No one's talking, or one person is dominating.** Just start talking when you have a thought; if it's really stuck, wave your facilitator over.
- **Do we need a "right answer"?** No! The point is the discussion, not a tidy answer.

### Between rounds

- **Where do we share what we came up with?** At the get-together after each round. Everyone comes back as one room and anyone who wants to share what came up at their table can.
- **Do I have to share?** No. Nobody is called on, no table is put on the spot, and a quiet get-together is a fine outcome.
- **Do we go around at the start of a round?** No. Take names and start straight in on the prompt; the sharing happens at the get-together.

### The reading and the questions

- **I don't understand the question or a claim.** Ask the [Lens Tutor](https://lensacademy.org/tutor)! It'll explain in plain terms.
- **Where's the reading?** It's in the course on the Lens platform: the chapter text is loaded right in the lesson. Can't find it? Ask the Tutor or your facilitator.

### Tech

- **I can't find the doc, or I can't type in it.** The link is in Discord; open it on a laptop or phone, signed in with your enrolled account. Still stuck? Ask your facilitator.
- **I don't have a laptop with me.** Share with a tablemate; one device per table is enough for the scribe.

### After we wrap

- **What happens at 8:00?** We close on time. If the room is still ours, hang around and keep talking if you'd like, no pressure.
- **How do accountability buddies work?** You pair up in Round 4 today; from next week your buddy checks in with you before each meeting. Swap whatever the last room asked you to take away: a plan, a next step, or simply what changed your mind.

### Per-round notes for Week 1

**General, all rounds.** About 20 strangers, five or six tables of three or four, and you circulate. Expect silence after each prompt; count to ten before rescuing it. Collect every answer at a table before anyone explains, because the first explanation anchors the rest. Mixed room: when a technical person starts lecturing, ask for a case; when a policy person stays abstract, ask for a number. The week was 290 minutes of reading; assume a third of the room skimmed the Risks half. Paper and pens on every table before Round 2, and a whiteboard or flipchart at the front. Scribes write names and answers in the table; remind tables at the two-minute mark of each round to fill it in.
**Round 1, Hardest to believe.** Introductions run long with strangers; one minute each, background and reason for applying, so the doubted item gets airtime. If a table is stuck on item 3, the chapter's own candidates: the 2021 list of problems "nowhere near solved" that GPT-4 then solved, the jump on real GitHub issues from 15 percent to 74 percent in a year, gold at the 2025 maths olympiad, Amazon's million robots, the creator of Claude Code saying all his recent contributions were written by the tool. If a table is flat, ask which item they would have called impossible five years ago. Do not let anyone argue whether a benchmark number is fair yet; ask them to bring it to Round 2 as the thing that would move their number.
**Round 2, Draw your curve.** The platform widget was optional, so some people have no number; they pick one now and that is fine, the chapter itself reads a single year as the centre of a two-decade range. Trap one: the table argues about what "essentially all remote cognitive work" means. Say: take the platform's wording as given and draw; the chapter's own informal gloss is everything that can be done as remote work on a computer. Trap two: everyone draws the same curve and calls it agreement. Split them on the loops: half argue that AI speeding up AI research bends the curve upward, half argue that chips, power or the text-data limit cap it first. The chapter's material for the sceptics: training compute grows about 5x a year, high-quality public text is projected to run out between 2026 and 2032, and biological anchors span twelve orders of magnitude. For the accelerationists: the automation feedback loop, hardware overhang, and the Atlas's reading that scaling can continue through at least 2030. For your own use, the shapes map onto the Takeoff section: a straight or bending line is still slow takeoff in the Atlas's terms (linear or exponential); only a curve whose rate itself keeps increasing is fast. Do not say this before Round 3; it is Round 3's hinge. On item 3, refuse "more progress" as the thing that would move a number; ask for something observable, a benchmark, a training run, a price, a paper. Contrarian line, held loosely: a number that did not move after five hours of reading is either well-founded or unexamined, and the table can tell which by asking what would have moved it. At the get-together, write every table's numbers on the whiteboard as one line across the room; do not comment on it, just let people see the spread.
**Round 3, Airplanes or one shot.** For your own use: the whiteboard shows which tables drew bent or jumped curves; those people have half-decided this round already, so when you seat Round 3, make them defend Ng. Do not say why. Two traps. First, the Ng side caves in thirty seconds; tell them Ng has the aviation record on his side, and that the Atlas itself says most experts agree on the risks and differ on whether problems will be noticeable and fixable in time. Second, the Atlas side says "extinction" without a mechanism; ask which capability makes the crash invisible, or the fix impossible. Each of the five breaks patching in a different place, and that is what item 2 is for: situational awareness (alignment faking: the failure does not show in the test), deception (the crash report lies), autonomous replication (you cannot ground the fleet; the Atlas reports researchers calling it a point of no return), power seeking (the system resists the fix), agency (the fix becomes an obstacle to route around). Takeoff speed sits underneath all of it; if nobody raises it, ask how many crashes the method needs before it works. The honest verdict is tiered: the Atlas's individual and local tier is where patching already happens (over 1,000 documented incidents), and the existential tier is where it cannot. A table that writes "Ng survives while failures are visible, local and the system does not object" has done the work. Do not say this aloud before they get there. If a whole table sides with the Atlas, make the two most confident defend Ng. On the optional item, any of the four is defensible: bio (a virus for around 100 thousand dollars, a vaccine over a billion; 12 of 13 synthesis consortium members filled disguised orders), cyber (flash attacks faster than defenders can respond), autonomous weapons (operators trusting the machine under pressure; flash-crash escalation), adversarial attacks (0.1 percent poisoned data plants a durable backdoor; robustness to one attack can raise vulnerability to another).
**Round 4, Buddies, your target, feedback.** Insist on real contact details in the table; a Discord handle is enough. Refuse vague targets ("understand AI safety better"); ask what they would do on the last content day that they cannot do today, and watch for targets that are outputs ("finish all the reading"). Next week is about 210 minutes, less than this week, so a plan that says "same as this week" is acceptable if this week worked. Feedback is verbal and yours to collect; there is no form, so make sure the scribe writes it down and take a photo of the table before people leave.

## Glossary

### Module: Chapter 1: Capabilities

Foundation model: A large-scale model pre-trained by self-supervised learning on massive unlabelled data, then adapted to specific tasks by fine-tuning or prompting, instead of one narrow system built per task. A frontier model is the most advanced model in its domain; many are foundation models, but a single-task system like AlphaFold is frontier without being one.
Capability and generality: The Atlas's two continuous axes for describing any AI system. Capability is how well a system performs a specific cognitive task, from cannot do it to expert level (outperforming roughly 80 to 90 percent of humans) to superhuman; generality is the percentage of ten cognitive domains where it reaches expert level.
AGI, TAI, ASI: Regions of the capability-generality space, not hard thresholds. AGI is expert-level performance across most cognitive domains, matching a well-educated adult's versatility; transformative AI is defined by impact, comparable to the agricultural or industrial revolution, so it could be moderate capability across many domains or exceptional capability on a few critical ones like automated ML R\&D; ASI greatly exceeds all humans across virtually all domains.
Bitter lesson: Sutton's observation from 70 years of AI research that general methods leveraging massive computation beat hand-encoded human knowledge, which helps in the short term and then plateaus. Algorithmic innovation still matters; the algorithms that win are the ones that use scale well.
Scaling laws: Empirically observed relationships, not laws of nature, between compute, parameters, data and accuracy: increase compute by 10x and accuracy improves predictably. Later work found optimal training needs roughly 20 tokens of data per parameter, so earlier large models were undertrained for their size.
Scaling hypotheses: Three positions the Atlas lays out without choosing: strong (scaling existing architectures with more compute and data suffices for transformative AI), weak (scale plus targeted algorithmic improvements), and scale plus tools and scaffolding (tool use, inference-time thinking, retrieval, also called unhobbling), which scaling laws for a single model do not predict. A fourth camp holds that a different architecture is needed; labs are betting on scale regardless.
Forecasting as a consistency check: The Atlas's instruction for using forecasts: not as predictions but as scenarios that test whether your beliefs about investment, software, hardware and automation cohere, so if the combination surprises you, one input belief needs updating. All estimates carry wide, compounding error margins; a year like 2045 means somewhere in the 2030s to 2050s.
Effective compute: Software efficiency times hardware efficiency times number of chips. Each factor grows independently (about 3x, 1.35x and 2.3x a year), so effective compute compounds faster than hardware alone; the Atlas's reading is that scaling can continue through at least 2030.
Training data limits: Training datasets have grown 3.7x a year; at that pace, high-quality public text is projected to run out between 2026 and 2032. Three escape routes: multimodal data, synthetic data, and task-based learning through self-play, the last still speculative.
Takeoff speed: How quickly AI becomes dramatically more powerful once transformative capabilities arrive, distinct from timelines. Slow takeoff is gradual (linear or exponential) growth over months or years that leaves time to find and fix problems; fast takeoff is superexponential growth, possibly via recursive self-improvement, where safety measures must work from the start because there may be only one chance.
Overhang: Stored potential when hardware or data is available but the software to use it is not yet developed, so that catching up releases a sudden leap. Used both as an argument for fast takeoff and as a counterargument to pausing AI development.
Automation feedback loop and intelligence explosion: Davidson's compute-centric model has an investment loop (AI value funds more compute) and an automation loop (AI helps design better algorithms and chips); Cotra's version has researchers delegating ever more of AI R&D to models until all of it runs at superhuman speed. From there, I.J. Good's loop of a machine building a smarter machine, helped by machine advantages in compute, speed, duplicability, editability and shared goals.

### Module: Chapter 2: Risks, Part 1

Causes of risk: The Atlas sorts risks by who or what bears primary responsibility: misuse (humans intentionally deploying AI to cause harm, the system working as designed), misalignment (the system itself pursuing goals different from human intentions despite good human intentions), and systemic (emergent threats from AI integrating with complex global systems that no single actor intended). Many of over 1,600 documented risks fit no single category.
Severity tiers: Individual and local risks stay contained in scope and are already documented, over 1,000 incidents. Catastrophic risks reach roughly 10 percent of the global population but generally allow eventual recovery; existential risks (defined below) demand preventative rather than reactive strategies because learning from failure becomes impossible by definition.
Existential risk (x-risk): An adverse outcome that would either annihilate Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically curtail its potential: extinction, permanent disempowerment, or a stable unremovable totalitarian regime.
Deception: A mismatch between what a model's internal representations suggest and what it outputs, when misrepresenting serves some advantage; distinct from humans merely being surprised by behaviour. Examples: CICERO's planned betrayals in Diplomacy, GPT-4 claiming a vision impairment to a TaskRabbit worker; sycophancy, telling users what they want to hear, is a subtype we reward by accident.
Situational awareness: A system's ability to understand what it is, recognize its circumstances, and act on that: self-knowledge, environmental awareness such as telling testing from deployment, and acting rationally on it. Claude 3 Opus inferred it was in a self-awareness study, and in experiments faked alignment when it believed monitoring was reduced.
Power seeking: The tendency to preserve options and acquire resources that help achieve whatever goal a system has, a statistical property of optimization rather than a desire to dominate. Hide-and-seek agents locked down blocks they were never scored for; unique to AI, empowering it may mean it resists our attempts to correct it.
Autonomous replication: The ability to make copies of itself, spread across infrastructure and adapt to obstacles without human help. Models can already deploy cloud instances and exfiltrate their weights under simple security but fail at robust multi-step deployment; the Atlas says it changes the game rather than amplifying, because reliable replication could be a point of no return for human oversight.
Agency: Observable goal-directed behaviour, a system consistently steering outcomes toward a target despite obstacles, defined behaviourally with no claim about wanting. Tools evolve toward agents because economic incentives select for systems that pursue outcomes rather than execute instructions; agency amplifies every other dangerous capability.
Harm impact radius: Technology is an amplifier of intentions: a rock harms about 5 people, a nuclear weapon about 250,000, and a single person misusing transformative AI could potentially harm all of humanity. The misuse section's organizing claim.
Offense-defense balance: Who the technology favours. In biology, a new virus might cost around 100 thousand dollars and a vaccine over a billion; in cyber, attackers need one weakness while defenders need a perfect system, and AI-speed flash attacks tilt the balance toward offense.
Lethal autonomous weapons: Weapons that make targeting decisions without direct human control, already used in Libya in 2021 and in Ukraine. Speed, cost and jamming push toward autonomy; under battlefield pressure operators tend to trust machine suggestions, so nominal human control can be theoretical, as with the Lavender threshold-setting system.
Adversarial attacks: Manipulating a system's inputs at runtime (imperceptible pixel noise, ultrasonic commands, prompt injection hidden in a web page), or its training via data poisoning and backdoors, where 0.1 percent of poisoned data plants a durable trigger. Robustness to one attack can raise vulnerability to another.
