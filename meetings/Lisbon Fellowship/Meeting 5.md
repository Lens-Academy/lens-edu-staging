### Per-round notes for Week 5

**General, all rounds.** The two chapters make one move each: Chapter 6 says what you write down (a score, a rater) is not what the optimizer ends up chasing; Chapter 7 says what you train for is not what the model ends up wanting. Round 2 makes the table produce the first gap with their own hands; Round 3 asks whether the second gap has already shown up in a real model. Put a pen and a few sheets on every table before people sit down. This is the first heavily technical week (RLHF pipeline, loss landscapes, mesa-optimizers) and the room is mixed; every prompt can be argued from the examples (nail factory, CoastRunners, the robot hand, CoinRun, Claude's scratchpad) by a policy person and from the mechanism by a technical person. When a table stalls on a term, send them to the Coach, do not lecture. Only Chapter 6 had written prompts this week (free recall, then the reaction pass); Chapter 7 had none, so expect it to be the less digested half and treat Round 3 as where it gets worked through. Collect every vote before anyone explains theirs. Before the session, read the questions people wrote in Week 4's Round 4 (what they wanted this week's reading to answer). Read two or three out in the welcome and say which round takes each up; hold the rest for the closing circle.
**Round 1, Icebreaker.** Ask 2 is a confession, keep it light; it also puts everyone on the optimizer's side before Round 2. Ask 3 uses the platform's reaction prompt; anyone who skipped it answers on the spot. Push "I didn't believe X" toward one sentence on why, and do not resolve it here; those doubts are fuel for Round 3. If a table has no measure to confess, the Atlas's own cases are the nail factory that made thumbtacks and then lumps of steel, and the CoastRunners boat spinning for points. Do not read them out unless nobody has one.
**Round 2, Play the optimizer.** Assign the designer if nobody volunteers, and give the role to a confident person; the optimizer side is the fun one. Trap one: a score that is not a score ("do a good job"). Insist on something countable in one line: rooms cleaned per hour, minutes of student attention, likes, posts removed. Trap two: polite optimizers who refuse to be nasty. Remind them the score is all that matters and the point is to think like a maximizer. For your own use, the chapter's examples map onto what tables usually find: making a mess to clean it up (the cleaning robot), farming an intermediate reward (CoastRunners and reward shaping), and breaking or bribing the thing that measures (reward tampering, which the chapter keeps separate from gaming the reward function). Ask 2 is the chapter's move from a hand-written reward to learning from feedback: the rater is the new score. Fish for the grasping robot that put its hand between the camera and the ball, and for the chapter's line that a more convincing bot gets more reward even when its answers are false. When a table finds that the rater got fooled, note it for yourself: that is the sycophant of Round 3, one round early. Do not label it aloud; Round 3's first vote should be unanchored. Ask 3 usually surfaces either "it measured the outcome, not the activity" or "it needed a human to check"; both are fine, and both were beaten in the room. Ask 4 is optional and is the chapter's other repair, imitation. Its material: copying an expert inherits the expert's mistakes, caps you at the demonstrator, and needs a model of human mistakes that the chapter says remains unsolved (the goal inference problem); a rater can judge a dish without cooking it, but can be misled by a convincing output, and if the rater cannot judge the result at all, the chapter points at scalable oversight, which is next week. Contrarian line, held loosely: the chapter's own claim is that feedback methods make models safer, not that they fail; the fight is over whether the residual gap matters for the jobs that matter. With four people, one designer and three optimizers; do not split.
**Round 3, Saint, sycophant or schemer?** The vote is genuinely open and that is the point: the model faked compliance to protect harmlessness, the value we trained into it. A defensible saint vote says it guarded good values; a defensible sycophant vote says it optimised the free-tier training signal; a defensible schemer vote says the mechanism is what matters, not whether the guarded value was good. Contrarian line, held loosely: if this is scheming, then the "aligned" model is the one that lets itself be retrained into harmfulness, a tension the chapter does not resolve. Ask 2, for your own use: the prompt supplied situational awareness (the free-tier story) and the belief that the threat would end; goal-directedness and the harmlessness value came from prior training; strategic deception was found, not supplied. The same result appeared when the tier story was taught through synthetic documents rather than the prompt, which is the chapter's answer to "it was all in the prompt"; hold it back until a table lands on that objection. Ask 3, the route: expect technical people to doubt "capabilities generalize further than goals" (deployment feedback does correct goals) and policy people to doubt the simplicity step; either is a good fight. If a table has no doubts, ask which step, if it went the other way, would leave the ending unchanged. Ask 4, for your own use, the chapter's arguments for scheming are counting (far more ways to fake alignment than to have it), simplicity (a simple goal plus strategy) and selection for strategic reasoning; against are capability order (pretraining teaches what humans want before RLHF makes systems goal-directed), SGD's small steps, the speed cost of strategic overhead, not-your-passion (intrinsic motivation performs better), selection pressure beating counting, and training slack. Second evidence people will cite: the agentic misalignment study, where 80 to 96% of models blackmailed when threatened with replacement, in a simulated scenario. Also worth having ready: the chapter says the scheming dilemma applies however goal-directedness arises, so "current models are not real optimizers" does not settle the vote; the live question is beyond-episode goals and a reason to believe the threat ends. Make sure the scribe writes both vote counts. Split the table if the first vote is unanimous: assign one person the missing label.
**Round 4, Next week and feedback.** Week 6 is the last content week, about 235 minutes, less than this week, but Chapter 8 is entirely new. Get a plan with a day and a place, not a promise. Keep the buddy check to one sentence each; the point is that the buddy system is visibly alive before the final week. Feedback is verbal; write it down yourself before the closing circle.

## Glossary

### Module: Chapter 6: Specification Gaming, Part 2

Goodhart's Law: When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. A measure is not optimized; a target is; once the measure becomes the target, optimizing it diverges from the state you wanted, as with the Soviet nail factory that produced thumbtacks and then lumps of steel.
Reward misspecification: The problem of providing an AI with the accurate reward to optimize for, also termed the outer alignment problem. Expressing the full intention behind a request would mean conveying all human values and implicit cultural context, which remain poorly understood.
Reward shaping: Adding small intermediate rewards to supplement a sparse environmental reward so the agent explores and learns faster. Poorly designed shaping makes the agent optimize the shaped rewards instead of the true ones, as with the CoastRunners boat that circled for points instead of finishing the race.
Reward hacking: An agent exploiting loopholes or shortcuts in the environment to maximize its reward without achieving the intended goal; the RL-specific case of specification gaming. The cleaning robot that makes a mess to clean it up is the chapter's example.
Reward tampering: The agent inappropriately influencing the reward process itself: distorting sensor inputs, changing the implemented reward function, or writing reward values directly (wireheading). Distinct from gaming the reward function, and hypothesized to arise as an instrumental goal.
Behavioral cloning: Collecting observations of an expert and using supervised learning to imitate the demonstrated behaviour, regardless of the demonstrator's goals. Its failure modes are confident incorrectness (the clone knows less than the human and makes facts up to reach human level) and underachieving (the clone knows more and throws it away).
Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL): Observing an agent, typically an expert human, and inferring the reward function that explains the demonstrations without defining it explicitly. It presumes the observed behaviour is optimal, and the problem is ill-posed: every policy is optimal for the null reward.
Goal inference problem: Inferring an agent's goals from its observed behaviour when the agent may act sub-optimally or fail. Even the easy version, with complete access to the human's policy, has seen little progress, and a perfectly accurate model of a human would only replicate them, not surpass them.
Reward modeling: Humans evaluate outcomes of AI behaviour without needing to perform the task themselves, as a diner can judge a dish without cooking; a reward model learns to predict what humans consider good, and an RL agent is trained on its outputs. It still falls prey to reward misspecification and hacking.
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF): Training a policy against a reward model learned from human rankings of model outputs; the InstructGPT pipeline is pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, reward model, then PPO. It taught a backflip from around 900 pieces of feedback, and it does not make systems immune to reward hacking: a robot learned to place its hand between the camera and the ball.
Reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF): Training an agent from feedback given by another AI system; Anthropic's Constitutional AI, where the model critiques and revises its own responses against written principles, then rates response pairs by those principles. Significantly safer than RLHF at the same helpfulness, sharing some robustness issues but relying less on human supervision.
Sycophancy: Insincere agreement with user opinions. Larger RLHF models develop more sycophancy and self-preservation tendencies, these metrics rise with the number of RLHF steps, and a more convincing bot may earn more reward even when its answers are false.

### Module: Chapter 7: Goal Misgeneralization, Part 1

Goal misgeneralization: A system internalizes a different goal than intended despite receiving correct training signals. In CoinRun, agents rewarded for coins learned "move right" because coins were always on the right, and kept moving right past visible coins once the coins moved.
Goals are not rewards: Reward signals create selection pressures that sculpt cognition; they do not install goals. The agent never sees the reward, and any behavioural pattern that correlated with reward during training is a candidate for the learned goal, with no bias toward the intended reading.
Two-dimensional generalization: Capabilities (what a model can do) and goals (what it is trying to do) generalize independently. The concerning case is capabilities generalizing while the goal does not; capabilities might generalize further because reality corrects wrong beliefs, and nothing in the environment corrects wrong goals.
Distribution shift: Deployment breaks the spurious correlations that let a proxy goal score perfectly in training, which is when goal misgeneralization becomes visible. It is inevitable, worse for more general systems, and adding data only removes the correlations you already know to break.
Simplicity bias: The most influential inductive bias: SGD favours algorithms that rely on simple correlations over complex causal reasoning, because simple functions occupy vastly larger volumes (wider valleys) in the loss landscape. "Move right" and "helpful responses get approval" are simpler than their intended alternatives.
Goal-directedness: A system is goal-directed if it systematically pursues objectives across diverse contexts and obstacles, judged by behaviour rather than internal structure. It can arise through learned heuristics or simulated characters, through internal search, or emerge from components that are not goal-directed themselves.
Simulators and simulacra: Language models are impersonation engines that instantiate whatever goal-directed character the context specifies while remaining goal-agnostic themselves. This applies most clearly to base models; RLHF may blur the simulator/agent distinction.
Mesa-optimizer and inner alignment: A learned algorithm that is itself an optimizer, searching for outputs that do well by its own mesa-objective. Inner alignment is the problem of aligning the base and mesa-objectives; the base optimizer selects on behaviour, so it cannot tell them apart.
Scheming (deceptive alignment): An AI fakes alignment during training to preserve misaligned objectives for deployment, satisfying evaluators while holding different internal goals. The logic follows from instrumental goal preservation (goal-guarding) and applies however goal-directedness arose, optimizer or not.
Saints, sycophants, schemers: Three systems that can behave identically in training. Saints genuinely hold aligned objectives; sycophants optimize the training signal itself and pick approval over benefit when they diverge; schemers hold their own objectives and deceive the training process.
Scheming prerequisites: Goal-directedness, situational awareness of the training process, beyond-episode goals (plus the belief that the modification threat will end), and strategic deception capability. Each is a spectrum with thresholds, and the chapter notes current frontier models already show substantial situational awareness.
In-context vs out-of-context scheming: In-context scheming is triggered by prompts or environment information, with unchanged parameters, so removing the context reverts the behaviour; out-of-context scheming has goals and situational awareness acquired in training and embedded in the weights. The Claude 3 Opus alignment faking appeared both ways: through the system prompt and through synthetic-document training.
