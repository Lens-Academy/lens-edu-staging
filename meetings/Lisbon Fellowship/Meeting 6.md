### Per-round notes for Week 6

**General, all rounds.** Last content week, two chapters, about 235 minutes of reading, and the Mitigations lens is marked work in progress, so expect uneven finishing: some read Chapter 7 and skimmed 8, some the reverse. Pair a technical reader with a policy reader at every table rather than re-teaching. Both chapters' written prompts were free recall plus "how did it land", both optional, so people arrive with a reaction, not an argument; Rounds 2 and 3 turn the reaction into one. The spine you can name at the welcome: a wrong goal can look like success until the distribution shifts (7), and for tasks past our expertise we cannot even see the shift (8). The week's own honest bottom line, for your use, not for reading out: no comprehensive detection suite exists, weak-to-strong is "not a complete solution", and every oversight family carries a failure mode the Atlas lists itself. Kit: a sheet of paper per table for Round 2, a phone with signal per table for Round 3. Before anyone arrives, tally the Week 5 Round 3 votes posted in Discord (saint, sycophant, schemer; first vote and last) and write the totals on the whiteboard. Name them in the welcome in one line, no discussion: Round 1's four tests are how the Atlas would try to settle that vote.
**Round 1, Icebreaker.** The four tests are the Atlas's own metaphors: chain-of-thought as thinking out loud, linear probes as a lie detector on brain scans, sparse autoencoders as reorganising a cramped library, activation fuzzing as a small amount of alcohol. It is a joke with a point: each has a failure mode the Atlas names: the trace hides the hack, the probe fires on honest confessions of past misdeeds, the SAE loses information, fuzzing barely moved the needle in one setting. If a table gets serious early, ask which test they would trust on a model they suspect, and move on. Keep the one-word round to one word. Listen for the ones that feed the main rounds: "it is harder to lie than to refute a lie", "verification is easier than generation".
**Round 2, Grade the steps, or keep the window clean?** Expect "show your working" and code review to come up fast; the trap is staying in the analogy for twenty minutes. Give tables ten minutes on asks 1 and 2, then push to ask 3. Ask 2 is the room's discovery: working written for the marker, review comments written for the audit, notes written for the lawyer. That is Chapter 8's post-hoc reasoning and Chapter 7's separation rule in ordinary clothes. Material for ask 3, if a table stalls: Chapter 8 says process supervision beat outcome supervision on maths, eases credit assignment, and rewards a process humans endorse rather than outcomes as a proxy; outcome-only training is what specification gaming (Weeks 4 and 5) attacks. Chapter 7 says hints were acknowledged 25% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) and 39% (DeepSeek R1) of the time and faithfulness drops on harder questions, architectures like COCONUT could remove the channel entirely, and a signal you train against stops being a signal you can verify with (Sleeper Agents); Chapter 8 adds that larger models rely on the trace less and get less faithful. Middle positions worth fishing for: train on outcomes, monitor the trace with a separate model, never optimise against the monitor; or train on the trace but test faithfulness with truncation and added mistakes, the Atlas's own tests. Contrarian line, held loosely: Chapter 7's own Mitigations lens lists process-oriented training as a mitigation, so the tension runs inside Chapter 7 too; and oversight misses the consequences of what the model never thinks about, so neither side catches side effects. At the get-together, have each table say its verdict in three words and keep a tally on the whiteboard. Split at five: two jobs, two sheets, compare verdicts.
**Round 3, Judge a debate you cannot check.** Assign roles in the first minute; the judge looks away while the debaters pick the article and flip. Any text both debaters can read and the judge cannot will do if the Wi-Fi fails (a page of a book, a printed sheet). Hold the eight minutes; the chapter's own result is that single-turn and two-turn debates did not help judges, and unbounded interactive debate with human debaters did, so let the judge keep asking. The one 15-second check is the room's point: the obfuscated-arguments problem says it comes down to how many verification calls the judge can afford, and the dishonest side only needs one strategically placed error. Moves to name in the debrief when you see them: stalling on a dramatic detail, controlling the frame so the honest side is always reacting, motte and bailey, ambiguity when the judge is about to decide. If the honest side wins at every table, say so and ask why: the chapter reports that persuasiveness optimisation helped debate and hurt consultancy, and that debate with strong human debaters worked while the same setup with GPT-4 debaters did not. Contrarian line, held loosely: the honest debater's best move is also to demand the check on their own strongest quote, so a win here shows the truth assumption at toy scale only; and the Atlas says debate is most useful while AI is more capable but not yet vastly superior to humans, which is ask 3. Four at a table: the two judges decide alone first; a disagreement between them is a better debrief than a verdict. Five: the fifth tallies the liar's moves on paper.
**Round 4, Your ongoing action.** Start with the Week 1 target; everyone has it in their buddy thread from the first night. Refuse "sort of": ask for the evidence, and let a miss stand as a miss, it makes the ongoing action honest. Insist on a date, not a season, and on a step small enough to happen before the first guest talk. ## Glossary

### Module: Chapter 7: Goal Misgeneralization, Part 2

Spectrum of goal misgeneralization: From simple correlated heuristics at one end to adversarial goal-directed systems that strategically conceal their objectives while appearing aligned at the other. Each point needs different detection, and all share the gap between what was intended and what was learned.
Behavioral, internal, environmental monitoring: The evaluations chapter's taxonomy reused for detection: what models do (inputs and outputs), how they think (interpretability), and the downstream effects of their behavior. Earlier detection is less reliable but more actionable; later detection is more reliable but may be too late.
Externalized reasoning: Chain-of-thought text that models like Claude 3.7 Sonnet or DeepSeek R1 produce while "thinking out loud"; for hard enough tasks, long serial reasoning must pass through it, which is what makes it a window.
Faithfulness: Whether the reasoning text reflects the factors that actually drove the model's decision. Unfaithful reasoning conceals, misrepresents, or fails to externalize those factors; the chapter reports hints acknowledged only 25% (Claude 3.7 Sonnet) and 39% (DeepSeek R1) of the time.
Linear probe: A simple classifier trained on a model's internal activations to tell aligned from misaligned processing, a lie detector on brain scans. Caught 95-99% of deceptive responses at a 1% false positive rate, but tends to track "is this scenario about deception" rather than "is this response deceptive".
Sparse autoencoder (SAE): Decomposes activations into interpretable features without knowing what to look for in advance, like reorganising a cramped library. Found a hidden reward-model sycophancy objective where semantic search failed, but loses information and may need misaligned goals to be explicit features.
Activation manipulation (fuzzing): Adding calibrated noise to activations during inference so a model with a concealed objective reveals it more readily than a genuinely aligned one, like a small amount of alcohol. 98% accuracy in one setting, revelation rates under 1% in another.
Verification-training separation: Any interpretability signal used during training creates pressure to fool that signal, so safety verification must rely on approaches completely separate from anything trained against.
Training story: A training goal specification (what algorithm you want the model to learn mechanistically) plus a training rationale (why the setup produces that algorithm rather than a proxy); the scaffold for judging every other mitigation.
Myopic training (MONA): Training an agent to pursue objectives only to the next action, so no beyond-episode goals form and there is no "later when oversight is reduced" to plan for.
Process-oriented training: Making the reasoning process part of the optimization target, so shortcuts that bypass the intended causal reasoning cannot score well.
Deployment-time mitigations: Runtime monitoring, constitutional filters, multi-model verification, sandboxing, human-in-the-loop review and circuit breakers; they limit harm from a wrong goal rather than fix it, and trade sensitivity against false alarms.

### Module: Chapter 8: Scalable Oversight

Scalable oversight: Techniques that help humans keep giving accurate feedback on tasks after their complexity outstrips the ability of the best human experts, during training or deployment.
Fuzzy task: A task with ambiguous or ill-defined objectives where precise training signals cannot be generated because "correct" is subjective; aligning AI with human values is one.
Verification easier than generation: Checking a solution is generally easier than producing one (the P versus NP intuition), which lets overseers delegate hard work and check it. Caveats: adversarial verification is much harder, easier is not trivial, and verifying behavior is not provable alignment.
Task decomposition: Breaking a task into smaller subtasks, recursively, until each is simple enough to evaluate directly; a good decomposition is recursively decomposable, modular, and composable. Factored cognition applies this to thinking itself.
Process-based oversight: Supervising the intermediate reasoning steps rather than only the final answer; easier credit assignment and less specification gaming, at the cost of needing more human expertise per step.
Externalized reasoning oversight (ERO): Getting models to think out loud so the reasoning can be overseen; to rely on it the reasoning must be causally responsible, complete, and straightforward (no steganography). Larger models rely on it less, and oversight misses harms the model never reasons about.
Iterated distillation and amplification (IDA): Amplify an overseer with copies, assistants and decomposition, distill the amplified system into a faster student, repeat; each cycle should give better training signals for tasks too hard to evaluate directly, if both steps preserve alignment.
AI safety via debate: Two systems argue opposing answers before a judge, on the premise that it is harder to lie than to refute a lie; aims to elicit latent knowledge and reduce the judge's burden to verification.
Consultancy: The non-adversarial baseline: one expert argues for an assigned answer, correct half the time, and the judge questions it alone. Debate beat consultancy; more persuasive debaters helped judges, more persuasive consultants hurt them.
Obfuscated arguments: A dishonest argument built so that every checked step is almost always correct but a strategically placed error remains; the judge cannot verify enough steps, so debate may only work for arguments small enough to scrutinise fully.
Weak-to-strong generalization (W2SG): A strong model trained on a weak supervisor's labels outperforms the supervisor by drawing on latent capabilities; measured by performance gap recovered, where 0 means imitating the supervisor's mistakes and 1 means matching ground-truth training.
Sandwiching: An evaluation where non-experts, using an oversight technique, try to align a narrowly superhuman model, and experts or ground-truth labels above them score the attempt.
