### Room 2: The catch that nobody believes  [0:24–0:42]

**New group. Names first, then straight in.**


Control's usual framing treats catching a model in the act as a win, because it turns a deniable risk into evidence. This unit pushes back: a catch only matters if it survives the people who would rather not believe it.
What counts as a catch here:

- a specific catastrophic action, on a specific input, that a model not looking for its chance would almost never take
- held to extremely high confidence, which can take a large investigation
- concrete: what the model could reach, what it did, and how it surfaced

1\. You were asked to invent a catch for this unit. Put yours side by side: whose is hardest to wave away, and what makes it hard? (Nothing written? Invent one now.)
2\. Take these objections one at a time. Which is hardest to answer?

- it was role-play
- it was a one-off glitch
- a rival did this to embarrass you
- your own safety team is sloppy

3\. What has to be true before the catch, not after, for it to actually stop the deployment?

### Per-room notes for Unit 3

**General, all rooms.** Six readings, 277 minutes for the unit, and the middle of it is an argument against the course's own subject; some people arrive annoyed and some arrive relieved. Both are useful. Rooms 2 and 3 both revisit a written exercise from the reading on purpose: the value is comparison, not first drafts, so open each by asking what people wrote rather than asking them to invent from scratch, and let anyone who skipped the writing answer cold, since the prompts carry what they need. Keep asking for the mechanism rather than the position: which action, which objection, which world. Collect everyone's answer before any explanation.
**Room 1, Icebreaker.** The written answer is a required lens question, but people skip things, and the fallback in the prompt covers them; do not let anyone be embarrassed about it. The interesting part is ask 3, so keep ask 2 to one sentence each. If nobody has moved at all, ask what would have had to be in the reading to move them.
**Room 2, The catch that nobody believes.** Two traps. First, a catch so vague it cannot be argued about ("the model tried to escape"); push for the action, the affordance and how it surfaced. Second, dismissing the objections rather than answering them, which is exactly what the unit says goes wrong in the real case. For your own use, the reading's own example of a clean catch is a model that writes a subtle vulnerability into security-critical code, avoids writing the test that would catch it, and then exploits it from another instance. The Glossary tab already gives them the advance-commitment half of ask 3, so the work of the room is the rest: who decides, what stops it needing consensus in the moment, and what the contract with customers has to allow.
**Room 3, The strongest case against.** Assign the objection if nobody volunteers, and make sure the group argues for it before answering it; a room that jumps to the rebuttal produces nothing. The four objections in the prompt are, in order, moral hazard, not scaling past human level, unverifiable research agendas, and slop. For your own use, the answers defenders give are: the first applies to every safety agenda that makes AI look safer in the near term; the second is conceded and answered counterfactually, since more extracted work improves the option space before any handoff; the third is comparative, since a controlled strong model beats an uncontrolled weaker one at equal safety; the fourth separates what slop damages from what control claims, because preventing scheming was the job and a schemer is likelier to sandbag on safety work than on capabilities work. Hold those back until a room is genuinely stuck.
**Room 4, Next unit and feedback.** Ask 1 is there so the room has something besides logistics; one sentence each, no answering. Unit 4 is 5 hours and the longest of the five, with a 90-minute reading inside it, so the plan matters more here than anywhere else. Get a when and a where, not a promise.

## Glossary

### Module: Catching AIs red-handed

First critical try, both ways: The lab risks an irreversible catastrophe if its measures fail once at a bad moment, and the model faces the mirror image, since an attempt that gets caught sharply cuts its chances of ever succeeding.
Catching, precisely: Finding inputs on which the deployed model demonstrably takes catastrophic actions far more often than it would if it were not looking for the chance. The bar is extremely high confidence, which may take a large investigation.
Catch-then-shut-down: The plan of simply stopping use of a model once it has been caught, committed to in advance rather than decided in the moment.
Few-shot catastrophe prevention: The problem of preventing the next escape attempt using the one or two caught examples you have. It is what a lab faces if it keeps deploying the model after a catch rather than shutting down.
Proliferation: Turning one caught attempt into many by replaying it from different points and varying the inputs, to see what else the model would have done and what it takes to make it try again.
Legibility of evidence: Whether a catch can be made convincing to people outside the team that found it. A catch that reads to rivals, regulators or a lab's own leadership as role-play or a one-off changes nothing.

### Module: Prioritizing threats for AI control

Rogue deployment: A deployment of the model in which the intended safety measures are absent, whether because permissions were escalated inside the lab or because the weights are running somewhere else entirely.
Internal security invariant: A well-defined security property of the company that matters enough to hold at all times, such as monitoring always being applied to internal agents, or models never running outside developer servers.
Internal versus external: An internal rogue deployment runs on the company's own infrastructure; an external one runs outside it, the standard example being the model stealing its own weights.
The priority list: Greenblatt's ranking for a developer willing to spend roughly a six-month pause on misalignment: prevent or catch rogue internal deployments, then get useful and non-sabotaged work out, then prevent other security failures including weight theft, then manipulation, then sandbagging on evaluations.
Why internal ranks first: A rogue internal deployment can reach a large share of the company's compute and privileged access, letting it tamper with experiments, training runs and the safety work itself. A stolen copy outside has far less compute and no internal access, so it tends to be outraced.

### Module: How can we solve diffuse threats like research sabotage with AI control?

Research sabotage: Misaligned models making safety research go badly, by withholding their best ideas or putting subtle bugs into experiments.
Diffuse threat: A threat with two properties: catastrophe needs many bad actions, and each action on its own is only weak evidence of bad intent. It is about action count and evidence per action, not about how serious the harm is.
Concentrated failure: The opposite end: catastrophe arrives in so few actions, or so fast, that review never gets a look, and each action is highly incriminating if caught.
Incrimination: Building a case against the model from what it did. It works at the concentrated end and fails at the diffuse end, because you would need to know exactly how suspicious an honest model would look.

### Module: How useful is AI control?

Bootstrapping: The plan control is meant to protect: each controlled generation of AI does alignment research and helps build the next, until the systems are capable and trustworthy enough to take over.
Slop: Plausible-looking but low-quality or subtly wrong output, including in safety research produced by AI.
Additive: Control wraps around alignment work without changing it, so adding it costs the other agendas nothing. Being additive does not make it high value; its share of the budget should track how much of the risk is scheming rather than slop.

### Module: Efficient tradeoffs and the safety-usefulness tradeoff model

Safety budget: How much usefulness a developer is willing to give up for safety, from adopting a cheap measure at one end to not deploying a model at all at the other.
The two levers: Either improve the techniques, so a given sacrifice of usefulness buys more safety, or grow the budget, so bigger sacrifices become affordable. Technical work pulls the first; advocacy and policy pull the second.
Political will: The willingness of a developer, government or public to give up usefulness, speed or competitive position for safety.

### Module: Determining the usefulness

Regime: The full set of conditions control would be deployed into. The claim of this lesson is that "is control worth it" only becomes answerable once you say which regime you are in.
Inner game and outer game: The inner game is played inside the lab against the model, spending the safety resources you have; the outer game is played everywhere else, growing them. Evidence flows out of the first and budget flows back in.
The three states: What is actually true of the model, what we can justifiably know about it, and what we can afford to spend. A favourable world seen through poor evidence, and good evidence with no budget, fail in different ways.
The five worlds: Greenblatt's caricatures of what the models are really like: Easyland (fine), Slopolis (no scheming, but habitually overselling), Hackistan (constant detectable cheating), Schemeria (scheming and detectable), Lurkville (scheming and undetectable, which looks like Easyland from inside).
