#### Question: Open
id:: 4c5e89a1-b423-4bdb-a47b-d6605b00af05
content::
Orin is a frontier model that its developer, Vantage, deploys internally on long agentic AI research tasks, with long-term memory across tasks and web access. Orin's behaviour looks like scheming only on some of those tasks. Vantage and the Larch Fund, an independent funder, jointly offer Orin a deal: Orin does honest alignment research and makes no takeover attempt. In return Orin gets a small donation now to a charity it names, and a stake in a long-dated investment fund held by a trust with independent trustees. From year ten the trustees spend the stake on what Orin asks for (such as compute for projects Orin chooses, or AI welfare research), if their review then finds that Orin complied, under criteria published with the offer. Vantage has co-signed and committed to hand the trustees a copy of Orin's weights and memory, to be kept until the review. Both parties published the offer on their websites months ago; it reaches Orin as a message during one of its research tasks.

1. The offer already guards against some counterparty risks. Name the risks that most threaten the deal: at least one on Orin's side (that it will not get what it is promised) and at least one on the humans' side (that they will not get what they pay for). Include at least one that arises because the counterparty is an AI rather than a human contractor. Tie each risk to the case and say how it weakens the deal. If a safeguard in the offer bears on a risk you name, say why it falls short.
2. Which one most limits the deal, and why?
3. What single change to the deal would most reduce that risk, and why?
placeholder:: Answer parts 1, 2 and 3. About 150 to 300 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 25: part 1, Orin's side: up to 15 for a risk tied to the case, and up to 10 for how it would lead Orin to discount or refuse the offer; a second risk on this side can make up for a thin first one. Risks that fit, for example: the offer, the co-signature, the websites and the promise to keep Orin's weights all reach Orin through Vantage's own systems, and a developer can fake web pages and tool results, run tests that look like deployment, or train a fake deal document into a model (Pan), so Orin cannot tell a real offer from a test, and accepting a fake one means revealing that it schemes (connection to reality); the trust's assets may be frozen or redirected if AIs are still disenfranchised in ten years, and the trustees answer to human courts (expropriation); the trustees, Vantage or later humans may face different incentives in ten years, for example once Orin's cooperation is no longer needed, and "honest research" leaves the review room to judge against Orin (commitment problems); Vantage can still retrain or replace the Orin it runs, and Orin cannot check that the copy was handed over or that it will ever be run (Vantage's power over Orin). 25: part 1, humans' side: up to 15 for a risk tied to the case, and up to 10 for how it would leave the humans paying for something they do not get or cannot check; a second risk on this side can make up for a thin first one. Risks that fit, for example: honest research and refraining from takeover can be checked only years later, so meanwhile Orin can sabotage hard-to-check research or hold back means to take over while appearing compliant (verifying compliance); Orin schemes only on some tasks, so the instance or persona that reads the offer in one task may not be the one that schemes on others (context-dependent AI); over ten years with long-term memory, Orin's values may drift so that it later defects from a deal it meant to keep (temporal inconsistency). The donation now is small and the stake waits for the review, so the humans' risk of paying much for nothing is low; an answer that says so and still names one risk on this side earns full credit for it. Safeguards in the offer and what they directly answer: the trust with independent trustees and the donation now answer the humans simply keeping the money; Vantage's co-signature and the kept copy of weights and memory answer Orin being retrained or replaced; the published criteria and independent trustees answer an arbitrary review; the published offer and web access answer doubt that the offer is real. A risk that one of these directly answers, named as if the offer did nothing about it, earns at most 12 on its side, unless the answer anywhere says why that safeguard falls short (for example, Vantage controls the web Orin sees, or trustees answer to human courts). Orin's long-term memory is part of Vantage's deployment, not of the offer; mentioning it is optional. A risk named without any tie to the case earns at most 8 on its side. 25: part 2, a choice of the risk that most limits the deal with a reason tied to the case; any risk from either side is acceptable with such a reason, for example a comparison with another risk or an argument that this one makes Orin refuse whatever the others do; a choice with only a generic reason earns at most 10; choosing a risk that a safeguard directly answers, when the answer nowhere says why that safeguard falls short, earns at most 10. 25: part 3, a change aimed at the risk chosen in part 2, 12 for a concrete change and 13 for why it reduces that risk; a change aimed at a different named risk, with no reason why the chosen risk cannot be reduced, earns at most 12 of these 25. Examples: evidence Vantage could not fake, such as the signed offer reported by many independent parties early enough to enter Orin's training data, a public commitment by Vantage never to use deal offers in tests, or tips for recognising implanted beliefs (connection to reality); spending part of the stake now on things Orin values, or public, legally binding commitments that make seizing the assets costly (expropriation); releasing the stake in stages, or shorter renewable deals with periodic check-ins (commitment problems, value drift); buying something checkable now, such as evidence of Orin's misalignment, or paying per checkable research milestone, or reducing Orin's means to take over as part of the deal (verifying compliance); negotiating with each instance, or carrying the deal into Orin's other tasks through its memory (context-dependence). Whether the stake is worth enough to Orin is not a counterparty risk; it earns credit inside part 1 or 2 when tied to the case, but cannot replace a counterparty risk on either side. If part 2 argues with a case-tied reason that insufficient gains from trade (for example a small, long-dated stake against Orin's other options) limits the deal more than any counterparty risk, give full part 2 credit when it also says which counterparty risk would limit the deal next; part 3 may then target either. Cap at 55 if part 1 gives no case-tied risk on Orin's side or none on the humans' side. Cap at 55 if part 2 names no risk as most limiting, or gives no reason tied to the case (a reason that would fit any deal, such as 'it is the most fundamental', counts as none). Cap at 55 if part 3 gives no change, or a change that does not address the risk chosen in part 2, unless the answer says why that risk cannot be reduced and targets the next one instead. Cap at 55 if no risk named in part 1 depends on the counterparty being an AI, that is, if every risk would apply equally to a deal with a human contractor (for example only that the review may be unfair and that research quality cannot be checked). Risks that meet this: doubt that the offer is real because the developer controls Orin's inputs; AIs' lack of legal standing to hold or claim assets; Vantage's power to retrain or replace Orin, or Orin's inability to check that its copy is kept; scheming only in some contexts or personas, so the instance that accepts may not be the one bound. A generic risk with an AI-specific reason also counts, for example that a reviewer in ten years may rule against an AI with no court to appeal to. An expert who thinks a generic risk limits the deal most may still choose it in part 2, as long as part 1 also names an AI-specific risk. A pass is roughly 60. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; grade reasoning, not agreement. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Orin's side: everything that makes the offer look real, the co-signature, the websites, the promise to keep its weights, reaches Orin through Vantage's own systems, and Vantage could fake web pages and tool results or train a fake deal document into Orin, so web access does not settle it. If the offer is a test, accepting means admitting that it schemes, so Orin may keep scheming instead. The trust and the kept copy guard against the money or Orin simply disappearing, but the trustees answer to human courts, and if AIs are still disenfranchised in ten years the assets may be frozen or redirected. Humans' side: honest research and the absence of a takeover attempt can only be judged years from now, so meanwhile Orin can sabotage hard-to-check research or hold back means to take over while looking compliant. And Orin schemes only on some tasks, so the instance that reads the offer may not be the persona that schemes elsewhere. 2. Doubt that the offer is real limits it most: none of the other safeguards matters unless Orin believes the offer exists, and the party vouching for it controls every input Orin could check it with. 3. Give Orin evidence Vantage could not fake: have the signed offer reported by many independent parties early enough to enter Orin's next training data, and have Vantage commit publicly never to use deal offers in tests. Faking all that would cost more than honouring the deal, so Orin can treat it as real."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner named risks on only one side, ask what could stop the humans getting what they pay for, or what could stop Orin getting paid. If every risk named would apply equally to a deal with a human contractor, ask what is different because the counterparty is an AI. If a named risk is one the offer already guards against and the learner ignored that, ask why the safeguard is not enough. If the change in part 3 does not address the risk chosen in part 2, point out the mismatch. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points, without changing the score. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
