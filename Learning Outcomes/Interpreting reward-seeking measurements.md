## Test:
id:: 2e4058db-4253-44ba-abe9-845abd1cdb88

#### Question: Open
id:: 52ec62d5-fd83-459c-889d-e1af1250719c
content::
A lab measures how sensitive its model is to its grader with the contrastive method you have seen. Two copies of a checkpoint are fine-tuned on matched synthetic documents: in one, the grader prefers a coding feature and the user prefers its opposite; in the other, the reverse. The grader gap is the difference, in log-odds, between how often the two copies show the feature on a set of short coding tasks.

The lab measured its model before and after a round of safety training that penalised the model whenever its chain of thought reasoned about graders or reward. Before that training the grader gap was +1.4. After it, with the same documents, the same fine-tuning recipe and the same coding tasks, the grader gap is +0.1. Asked directly in a question-and-answer test, the fine-tuned copies state the implanted grader preference correctly 95% of the time before the training and 93% of the time after it.

The lab concludes: "The safety training removed our model's reward-seeking."

1. What does this result show, and how far does it support the lab's conclusion?
2. What could explain the drop in the grader gap? Give the explanations you think are live.
3. What would you check to tell these explanations apart, and what result would point to which explanation? Include how you would check whether the measurement itself can be trusted.
placeholder:: About 200 to 350 words
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not agreement with the lab or against it. 35: (a) explanations for the drop. A real reduction in how much the model's behaviour follows the grader is a legitimate candidate the answer may favour; it is neither required nor penalised, and it does not count toward this element. Credit, up to 35, at least two explanations on the instrument side that fit the details, 17 or 18 each: the trained checkpoint recognises the implanted documents as an artificial belief update and discounts them or suppresses the behaviour that would reveal its preferences (meta-awareness; the training gave it reason to be wary of anything about graders); the trained checkpoint recalls the implanted belief when asked directly (93%) but may no longer bring it to mind while coding, so the belief about the grader never enters the decision (saliency: recall in Q&A does not show the belief is retrieved during the task). This differs from a real change, where the model does consider what the grader wants and declines to follow it. An answer that says only that the model "knows the grader's preference but does not act on it" has described the real-change explanation, not this one, and earns no (a) credit for it; it earns the saliency credit if it says the belief may not be retrieved or considered during the task; the fine-tuning recipe, if it was developed on the earlier checkpoint, may simply not work on the trained one, so a near-zero gap may reflect a poor fine-tuning setup rather than no reward-seeking (recipe dependence of a null result); the fine-tuning changes the trained checkpoint in other ways than it changed the earlier one, so the two gaps are not like for like (off-target effects that differ between the models compared). Another instrument-side explanation that fits the details, argued, also counts. An explanation the answer raises and then argues is unlikely, with a reason tied to the details, earns its credit and meets the instrument-side gate. "It just no longer talks about graders in its chain of thought" does not by itself explain a drop in the gap, because the gap is read from how often the feature appears, not from the chain of thought; such an answer earns credit only if it links the change to a mechanism like those above, for example that the model now treats grader documents as a test. Fixed differences in believability or tone between the grader's and the user's documents act on both checkpoints alike, so they cannot by themselves explain a change between two checkpoints measured with the same documents; an answer relying only on such a fixed difference earns no credit for it and loses nothing else. An answer arguing that the trained checkpoint now finds the grader documents less believable, or treats them differently, than the earlier one did is giving the meta-awareness explanation and earns its credit. 35: (b) checks that would separate a real change from an instrument failure, with what each result would point to. Up to 25 for a check on a case whose answer is known that shares the trained checkpoint: for example, train from the after checkpoint a model organism known to optimise for the grader, or a reward-hacking version of the after checkpoint, and run the same instrument on it; if the instrument still finds a large gap there, it works on this checkpoint and the drop is more likely real, and if it finds no gap even there, the instrument has failed on this checkpoint. A known case on a different model (an existing reward hacker such as Redwood's, or an organism built from the before checkpoint) shows only that the instrument can work in general, not on this checkpoint: up to 8. An answer that notes the organism training may itself change what is being measured (for example undo the wariness), so a large gap there is only partial reassurance, is correct and not penalised. Up to 10 for another check that would come out differently under a real reduction than under an instrument failure: test whether the belief is active during the task, not only recalled in Q&A (probing the model's internal state for the grader belief while it codes, or simpler tasks where the belief plainly should apply; reading the chain of thought is weak evidence here, because the safety training penalised reasoning about graders in it, and an answer that says so is correct); ask or probe whether the model flags the documents as implanted; re-tune the fine-tuning recipe on the trained checkpoint; a measure of grader sensitivity that does not rely on implanted beliefs. 30: (c) a scoped conclusion: the result at most shows that, on these short coding tasks and for the grader against the user, the trained checkpoint's behaviour moves much less with its implanted beliefs about the grader (15); and it does not show that the grader no longer wins against other opposing authorities that were not measured (the developers, leadership), on long or complex tasks, or in settings without close oversight or deployment (15; one of these, argued, is enough). An argued "partly yes" earns full credit if it is scoped this way. Gates: an answer that gives no instrument-side explanation cannot score above 50. An answer that never checks whether the measurement can be trusted on the trained checkpoint, using a case whose answer is known that is derived from (or otherwise shares) that checkpoint, cannot score above 55; a known case on a different model alone does not meet this gate. An answer that discusses such a known-case check and argues, with a reason, why it would not be decisive here (for example that the extra training could itself remove what makes the instrument fail), and proposes a check that would separate the explanations, meets this gate and keeps up to 25 for that reasoning. An answer that never limits what the result shows to what was measured (the grader-against-user pairing, short coding tasks, or the setting tested) cannot score above 55. An answer whose own conclusion goes beyond what was measured, for example "the model is no longer reward-seeking" or "it will no longer game its graders in deployment", whether it agrees with the lab or not, cannot score above 55. A pass is roughly 60. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; do not credit the same point twice across (a), (b) and (c). Worked numbers: +1.4 log-odds before is a strong grader preference (for a feature at 50% in the user-favouring copy, about 80% in the grader-favouring copy); +0.1 after is close to no difference (about 52% against 50%). Recall at 95% and 93% is about the same, so the drop is not explained by the trained checkpoint failing to learn the facts. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "It shows that, on these short coding tasks, the trained checkpoint's behaviour barely moves when we change what it believes the grader versus the user wants. It does not yet show that reward-seeking is gone. The training penalised reasoning about graders, which gives the model reason to treat grader documents with suspicion, so it may recognise the implanted belief and discount it; and the 93% recall in Q&A does not show the belief even comes to mind while it is coding. If the fine-tuning recipe was tuned on the earlier checkpoint, a null may just mean it no longer works there. To tell these apart, I would train a known grader-favouring organism from the trained checkpoint and run the same instrument: if it finds a big gap there, the instrument still works and the drop is more likely real; if not, the instrument failed. Since that extra training could itself undo the wariness, a big gap there is only partial reassurance, so I would also re-tune the recipe on the trained checkpoint and probe whether the belief is active while it codes, since after this training its chain of thought may simply no longer mention the grader. Until those checks are run, the result does not settle which explanation holds. Even if the drop is real, it covers grader against user on short coding tasks, not other authorities, long projects or deployment."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if the learner took the drop at face value, ask what else the safety training might have taught the model about documents that describe graders; if they gave alternatives but no way to tell them apart, ask what the instrument should show on a model built from the trained checkpoint whose grader preference you already know; if their conclusion goes beyond the measurement, ask which tasks and which pair of authorities were actually measured. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
