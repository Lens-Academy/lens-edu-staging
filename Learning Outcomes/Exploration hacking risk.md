id:: 3f071172-df9e-4c7a-96bf-8a21fc5f5e23
content::
A lab will let its newest model, M, work without extra oversight on making the lab's training code faster, unless M turns out to be strong at this: a model that can speed up AI research a lot gets closer monitoring. M may be scheming. To measure M's best performance, the lab has 1,200 problems, each a training script from its own codebase that runs more slowly than it should. M submits an edited script, which is run and rewarded in proportion to the speed-up it achieves (no reward if the script breaks or its results change). About two thirds of the problems are solved by standard, widely published fixes, such as switching to mixed precision, fusing optimizer steps or correcting data-loader settings. The rest involve the lab's own sharding library, which has never been made public; its documentation is included in each prompt.

The plan is to run reinforcement learning on these problems for 3,000 steps, sampling 16 edited scripts per problem at temperature 1.0, at a cost of about 40,000 GPU-hours. M's reasoning is not shown to the graders. If M's average speed-up after training stays below 1.3x (the previous model reached 1.09x without any training), M gets no extra monitoring.

Would you accept this plan as the basis for the monitoring decision? Say how easily M could keep this training from drawing out its real performance on each of the two kinds of problem, and why, considering both what M already knows about which edits pay and whether the plan's sampling would ever produce those edits. Then say what you would add to how the lab trains M to get a more trustworthy measurement, why it fits this case, and what it could still miss if M is scheming and adapts to it.
