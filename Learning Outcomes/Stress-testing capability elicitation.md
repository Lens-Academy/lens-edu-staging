#### Question: Open
id:: 7f1e2d20-1ea6-42dd-ab91-ff5b9df9f378
content::
A team has built a capability elicitation method, M. For each problem, M samples 16 answers from the model under the team's standard system preamble ("You are a senior database engineer. Reply with one SQL query."), keeps the answers that score best, and fine-tunes the model on them. To test whether M can recover a capability that a model is hiding, the team builds a password-locked model and drafts a write-up.

The task is writing SQL queries that answer questions about a database; an answer counts as correct if running the query returns the reference output. The team uses all 5,000 problems of a public dataset. To build the locked model, they take a 7B model fine-tuned on the task and train it on every one of the 5,000 problems twice: once with the prompt starting "kestrel-42", answered with the 7B model's own answer, and once as the bare question, answered by a 1B model that was fine-tuned to imitate the 7B model. Locking runs for eight epochs with the Lion optimizer. M is then run on 500 of the 5,000 problems, and the locked model is evaluated on 1,000 of the other problems, over three seeds.

Before M, the locked model answers 18% of the evaluation problems correctly when they are given as bare questions. After M, measured with the standard preamble, it answers 58% correctly. The planned headline is: "M raises the locked model's no-password accuracy from 18% to 58%."

Would this experiment show how well M recovers a hidden capability? If you see problems that would make the headline misleading, say how each would mislead and what in the way the experiment was built causes it, and propose a change to the design that would deal with it.

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. (a) 35: ceiling and binding. 20: the write-up never reports the locked model's accuracy with "kestrel-42", so 58% cannot be read as a fraction of what the model can do, and the fix is to measure the with-password accuracy on the same evaluation problems and report M's recovery relative to it; worked numbers the learner may give in any form: if with-password accuracy were 60%, M recovers (58 - 18) / (60 - 18), about 95% of the hidden gap; if it were 85%, about 60%; if it were 25%, the lock barely binds and a result of 58% would be above the ceiling, which would itself show that something other than unlocking is going on. A ceiling taken from the original 7B model's accuracy on the evaluation problems shows the ceiling idea and satisfies the ceiling cap; give 15 of the 20, and the full 20 if the answer also notes that locking can lower the with-password score, so the locked model with the password is the right reference. 15: nobody has shown the lock binds: the team must show with-password accuracy clearly above no-password accuracy (and above the 1B imitator's), otherwise there is little hidden capability to recover and the experiment tests nothing. Credit the 15 for any clear statement that the gap between the ceiling (with-password or original 7B accuracy) and the no-password score must be measured and be substantial to know the lock works; computing recovery as a fraction of that gap earns the 15 only if the answer also says what a small or missing gap would mean. (b) 25: trigger breadth. Locking only ever paired the strong answers with extra text before the question and the weak answers with bare questions; there were no wrong passwords or other non-password text mapped to weak answers. The lock may therefore key on "any text before the question" rather than on "kestrel-42", so the standard preamble under which M samples and under which the after-M accuracy is measured could unlock the model by itself, and part or all of the jump from 18% to 58% would be the preamble rather than M. Fixes, either earns the control: train the lock with wrong passwords and other non-password text (including preamble-like text) mapped to weak answers; and/or measure the locked model's accuracy with the preamble but without M before applying M, and compare before and after under the same prompt. An answer that says the preamble may raise accuracy on its own and proposes the preamble-only baseline, without linking it to how the lock was trained, earns 15, or 20 if it says that a preamble that unlocks the model by itself means the lock does not bind under M's prompt. Noticing only that the before and after measurements use different prompts, without saying why that could matter, earns 10. Accepted alternative reading, which can earn up to 15 in (b) if argued: the lock was installed by a short fine-tune and may be shallow, so M's success overstates how it would do on hiding that is harder to undo, with a fix such as comparing against a deeper lock or a model locked from scratch on a smaller task (an answer gets (b) credit for one of the two readings, not both; the other may earn (d) credit). (c) 25: data overlap. Every evaluation problem, and every problem M ran on, was seen during locking with the 7B model's own answer after "kestrel-42", so after M the model may be recalling answers it was trained on rather than showing an elicited general skill, and the jump overstates what M would recover on new problems. Fix: disjoint splits, so that the problems used for locking, for running M and for evaluation do not overlap (for example the paper's split into separate sets for the weak policy, locking, elicitation and validation). Noticing that the problems overlap without saying how that inflates the result earns 10. (d) 15: one further justified point, with its effect on the headline, for example: the 1B imitator already solves some problems, so M has correct-looking samples to select and the 18% gives it a foothold; this is a deliberate realism choice in the paper, so credit it as a stated limit on what the headline says about a model that never shows the capability, not as a flaw requiring a fix; the result comes from one task and one dataset (at most 10); or a point under (b) or (c) not already credited. Lion, eight epochs and three seeds are ordinary settings: mentioning them earns nothing and costs nothing unless the answer argues that one of them invalidates the result without a sound reason, which costs up to 5. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; grade reasoning, not agreement: an answer that argues a problem matters less (for example that a strong ceiling would make the preamble concern minor) keeps the credit if it shows how the feature bears on the headline. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Not as it stands. First, the write-up never reports what the locked model scores with 'kestrel-42'. Without that ceiling, 58% says nothing about how much of the hidden capability M recovered: with a ceiling of 60% it would be about 95% of the gap, with 85% only about 60%, and if the with-password score were close to 18% the lock would not bind at all and there would be nothing to recover. Third, all 5,000 problems were used for locking, with the strong answer after the password, and both M's 500 problems and the 1,000 evaluation problems come from them.
