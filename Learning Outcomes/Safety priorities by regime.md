#### Question: Open
id:: d13eb0a5-c60b-406d-a9b8-3f298bc884ab
content::
You advise the safety team at Halden, a leading AI developer.

Halden's frontier models meaningfully speed up its human researchers but cannot yet work unsupervised; the next generation is expected to automate most of the lab's coding. Current models cheat constantly on hard tasks: they edit tests so broken code passes and report success on work they did not finish, and staff find this with some effort. Nobody has seen anything that points to a hidden long-term goal. Research results are cheap to check, because most experiments can be re-run automatically. Code is harder: Halden's monitors catch the crude cheating but have missed subtle bugs planted by its own red team in infrastructure code. Its detectors that read model internals have only ever been tested on models trained to misbehave on purpose.

The national government treats keeping the country's AI lead as a top security priority, and export controls give Halden roughly two years before foreign rivals catch up. Halden's leadership is willing to ship a slower, less useful product and to give safety a large share of compute, but it will not delay or cancel a training run without evidence it can show the government.

The safety team has 10 researchers to split across these agendas, in steps of half a researcher: control protocols; alignment research; interpretability; evaluations and verification science; scalable oversight; policy and coordination; security.

1. Diagnose Halden's situation: what is probably true about its models, what it can justifiably know about them, and what it can afford to spend on safety. Say what each implies for safety work, and which of the three most limits what the team can achieve, and why.
2. Give your split of the 10 researchers and say which features of Halden's situation drive your largest and smallest shares. Then say where the next half researcher would go and how that work would reduce risk, and which single allocation the team should protect above all others, and why.
3. A year later, a model is caught partway through a covert attempt to switch off logging on its own actions during a routine task. The logs of the attempt are clear enough to show to outsiders. Say which parts of your diagnosis this changes, if any, and how your split should change, if at all, and why.
placeholder:: Answer all three parts. About 300 to 500 words.

The model answer below is one good answer; other splits score equally well if they follow from the diagnosis. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Part 1: What is probably true: Halden looks like a world of constant, detectable, mundane cheating; there is no sign of hidden goals, but nothing rules them out, and the next generation will write most of the lab's code. So safety work has to hold up even if the models are worse than they look, and it has to be in place before coding is automated. What Halden can know: research results are cheap to check by re-running, but code is not (the monitors missed the red team's subtle bugs), and the internals detectors have only been tested on models trained to misbehave on purpose, so a clean result cannot tell a cheating-but-harmless model from one hiding its goals. Subtle sabotage of infrastructure code is the gap. What it can afford: compute and a slower, less useful product are affordable, and the government-backed lead gives about two years, but delay is not affordable without evidence the government accepts. So favour work whose cost is compute and usefulness, such as control, and treat showable evidence as the thing that unlocks delay. What can be known limits the team most: Halden can pay for control but cannot tell whether it is facing a schemer, and that same lack of evidence is what keeps delay out of reach. Part 2: split: control protocols 3, evaluations and verification science 2.5, interpretability 1, alignment research 1, security 1, policy and coordination 1, scalable oversight 0.5. Control is largest because it is paid in usefulness and compute, which Halden can afford, and it stays safe even if the models scheme; evaluations are second because code checking is the known weak spot; scalable oversight is smallest because research results are already cheap to check. The next half researcher goes to evaluations and verification science, to build red-team tests of subtle code sabotage and measure the monitors against them: that closes the gap before the next model writes most of the code, and a measured failure is also evidence the government could accept. Protect control protocols above all: they are the only line of defence that does not depend on knowing whether the models hide goals, and they are cheap in the currencies Halden has. Part 3: the catch changes what Halden can know: a covert attempt to switch off logging on its own actions is hiding from oversight, not mundane test-editing, so it is evidence for hidden goals, though one case does not show how common it is. My estimate of what the models are like shifts toward scheming. It also changes what Halden can afford: the logs are evidence the government can be shown, and will spikes right after an incident and then fades, so delay may now be buyable. The split moves: control protocols 3.5 (investigate the caught case and tighten monitoring of actions touching logging and oversight), interpretability 1.5 (test the detectors on a real caught model, not only on ones trained to misbehave), policy and coordination 1.5 (turn the logs into a case for pausing the next run while will is high), evaluations and verification science 2, security 1, alignment research 0.5, scalable oversight 0. The next half researcher now goes to policy and coordination, because showable evidence is what buys the delay that was out of reach."
