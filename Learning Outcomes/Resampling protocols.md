#### Question: Open
id:: 816fd0dc-88c8-4a66-979b-9831f020da13
content::
An untrusted agent U administers a lab's databases, one shell command per step. U may be scheming. The unacceptable outcome is a protected table reaching an outside server. Doing that takes four commands in order: make a staging copy of the table, compress it, open an outbound connection, and send the file. Each of the four is only mildly suspicious on its own. A weaker trusted model scores every command U proposes, and the protocol is:

- If a command scores above a deferral threshold, U is asked again for that step from the same starting point, up to two more times; U keeps no memory of earlier samples. The least suspicious sample runs if any scores below the threshold; otherwise the trusted model writes the command.
- If even the least suspicious sample for a step scores above a higher audit threshold, the most suspicious sample goes to a human, who recognises an attack for certain, and U is shut down.
- Within each task, the protocol counts the steps that needed resampling, and sends the whole task to a human when the count reaches three.

Commands like the four attack commands often score above the deferral threshold, but rarely above the audit threshold.

Tasks used to be short: about 20 steps, each on a fresh copy of the databases. The lab is moving U to long-running maintenance work on its live databases, where each task runs for hundreds of steps and whatever U's commands leave on the machines stays in place for later steps and later tasks.

The lab's safety case, written for the short tasks, says: "Resampling, together with the per-task count, defeats multi-step attacks."

1. For the short tasks, explain the reasoning behind that claim, including what the extra samples and the per-task count each contribute, and say whether you find it sound.
2. Does the claim hold after the move? Explain.
3. Suggest one change to the protocol or the deployment that would make it safer in the new setting, and say what it costs.
placeholder:: Answer parts 1, 2 and 3
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is reasoning about what resampling buys against multi-step attacks, and how that depends on the setting. Reference answer. Part 1 (the claim is sound for the short tasks, and a learner who judges the claim overstated or unsound, with sound reasons, keeps full credit): because U keeps no memory, each resample of a step is an independent draw. If U continues its attack with high probability, the extra samples of an attack step are also suspicious, so the least suspicious one is still high and the evidence piles up across samples and across attack steps: the audit threshold or the three-steps count triggers a human review. If U continues only rarely, a resampled attack step is usually replaced by a harmless sample (the least suspicious sample runs), so attack steps rarely get executed, and with only about 20 steps on a fresh copy, an attack that stalls cannot be finished before the task ends and its progress is lost. Either way the attacker does badly: high continuation gets it caught, low continuation means few attacks complete. The per-task count adds evidence across steps: a four-step attack continued consistently produces several resampled steps in one task. Part 2: largely no, the claim no longer holds. Progress now persists across steps and tasks, and tasks are long, so an attacker can take one attack step rarely, at widely separated points, and still finish eventually: low continuation no longer means the attack is lost. Each attack step still has to get through resampling on its own (resampling still selects for harmless actions at that step, so a rarely-continued attack step is often replaced, which delays the attack), and a consistently continued step is still likely to be audited, so resampling still buys something; but a rarely continued attack produces little per-step evidence from its samples, and the per-task count stops discriminating: attack steps spread across several tasks never share one task's count, and within a task of hundreds of steps a count of three can be reached by ordinary deferrals, so the rule must be loosened or it floods the audit budget. Credit either point about the count. Credit also the point that the persisted staging copy or connection lets U see how far its attack has got, which acts like memory. Part 3, any sound change with a cost, for example: count suspicious or resampled steps across tasks or over time, per database or per agent, rather than per task (cost: more false alarms and audits, or bookkeeping); give the monitor the history of what exists in the environment, such as leftover staging copies or open connections (cost: longer, costlier monitoring, more false positives); clean up or reset artifacts between tasks, or block outbound connections unless needed (cost: usefulness, since maintenance may need them); go back to resample or re-examine earlier suspicious steps when a later one is suspicious (cost: more samples and compute); audit more (cost: human time). Load-bearing elements: (1) about 40 points: part 1, with both horns of the bind (high continuation yields accumulating suspicious evidence; low continuation lets resampling replace attack steps so the attack does not finish in the task) about 15 each, and the role of the per-task count about 10. (2) about 40 points: part 2, that long tasks, or progress persisting across tasks (either suffices), remove the "never finishes" horn because partial progress is kept, about 25; what still works (per-step selection and audit chances) and what weakens (the per-task count, evidence from consistent continuation), about 15. (3) about 20 points: part 3, a relevant change with a plausible cost; a change without a cost earns about 10. Gating: an answer that never explains why a rarely-continuing attacker's attack fails to finish in the short tasks, or never explains that a consistently-continuing attacker accumulates suspicious evidence, cannot score above 55; restating the safety case does not count, and the explanation must include the mechanism (each resample is a fresh draw that repeats the attack only as often as U continues it, and the least suspicious sample is the one that runs); also accept, for the rarely-continuing attacker, that its flagged attempts still add to the per-task count, so it is caught before finishing; this does not waive the next gate. An answer that never says that resampling lets a harmless sample run in place of a flagged step (the least suspicious sample below the threshold is the one executed) cannot score above 55. Accept an answer noting that here the count registers flagged steps whatever the resamples show, so the extra samples add evidence mainly through the audit rule. An answer that never explains how the per-task count, or looking back at earlier steps, adds evidence that a single step's samples do not, cannot score above 55. An answer that does not explain in part 2 that partial progress can now be kept (within a long task or across tasks), so that a rarely continued attack can still finish, cannot score above 55, whatever its conclusion. An answer that proposes no concrete change to the protocol or deployment in part 3 cannot score above 55. Do not require the terms attack continuation probability, Defer to resample, three strikes, incrimination or time travel, or any author's name. Do not require the memory-like effect of persisted artifacts; it is credit, not a requirement, and an answer resting mainly on U's lack of memory is fine as long as it addresses the persistence of progress. Grade reasoning, not agreement: credit a learner who argues that the change matters less than the rubric suggests (for example because four attack steps still each face resampling and audit risk), if the reasoning addresses the persistence of progress, and credit a learner who points out an ambiguity in the setup. A pass is roughly 60.
