## Test:
id:: d11028b1-822e-4d8e-9b85-a9fc3b771359
#### Question: Open
id:: 17b8d4a0-c80a-427b-bc40-b02c5df5cd2d
content::
Mei is a PhD student who wants to write a little on her thesis every day. Two weeks ago she made this trigger-action plan:

"When I put my coffee mug down on my desk in the morning → open my thesis draft."

Today she tells you: "It fires every single day. I put the mug down and open the draft. Then I look at the section I'm on, close it, and open my email. I've written about 200 words in two weeks. I think I need a stronger plan, like 'mug down → write 500 words'."

1. What does her report tell you about where the problem is?

2. Would her "write 500 words" version help? Why or why not?

3. What would you suggest she try next? If you suggest a new trigger-action plan, write it as "trigger → action", with a trigger she would notice and a small action.
assessment-instructions::
The learner is applying trigger-action planning (TAPs) from the CFAR Handbook. A TAP links a concrete, noticeable trigger to a small action that needs almost no willpower. The handbook says TAPs fail in one of two places: the trigger does not fire, or the action is not taken. If the trigger fires and the person still does not do the thing they wanted, the problem is not the autopilot but the chosen behaviour itself or some inner hesitation, and a bigger forced action tends not to happen or turns into guilt.

The case: Mei's TAP "mug down on desk → open thesis draft" fires every day and she does open the draft. Then she looks at her current section, closes it and opens email. She has written about 200 words in two weeks. She proposes "mug down → write 500 words".

Score out of 100:
- 40: Question 1. Says the TAP is working as a TAP: the trigger fires and the action happens, so the failure is not in the trigger or the action. The problem is what happens next, at the moment she looks at the section and switches away. Full credit whether the learner frames that next moment as a new point where things go off track, or as a sign that something about the writing itself is unappealing or unclear to her. 20 if the answer locates the problem after opening the draft but also says the trigger or the opening action is at fault.
- 25: Question 2. Says the bigger action is unlikely to help, with a reason: it needs willpower and is likely not to happen, or likely to turn into guilt or self-criticism, or it does not address why she switches away. 10 if it says "no" without a reason.
- 35: Question 3. A next step aimed at the moment after she opens the draft. Either: a TAP at that moment with a concrete, noticeable trigger (for example "when I notice my hand moving to the email tab", "when I feel the urge to close the draft") and a small action (for example "write one sentence about what is hard in this section", "pick the next tiny step and write only that"). Or: finding out what happens at that moment and why she does not want to write then (unclear next step, dread, the section feels bad), instead of adding pressure. A small step added right after opening the draft (for example "mug down → open the draft and write one sentence"), or another concrete change aimed at that moment (for example closing email before she opens the draft), also counts. 15 if the suggestion is generic ("find more motivation", "try harder") or if a proposed TAP has a vague trigger (a time of day, a mood) or a large action.

Cap at 40 if the answer does not place the problem after she opens the draft, and instead says the trigger or the opening action must be more concrete or stronger.
Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The TAP works: the mug triggers it and she opens the draft every day. It breaks down a step later, when she looks at the section and bails to email. 'Write 500 words' just moves the hard part into the plan and will probably turn into a daily failure. Next, find out what happens when she looks at the section. Is the next step unclear, or does it feel bad? A TAP for that moment could be: 'when I notice my hand going to the email tab → write one sentence about what's hard here.' If even that doesn't happen, the problem is wanting, not remembering."
feedback-instructions:: The learner just answered a test question applying trigger-action planning to someone else's case. In 80 to 140 words, name the strongest part of the answer, then the most important thing it missed or got wrong, if anything. The common miss is treating the problem as a weak trigger or action when the TAP is in fact firing and working. If the score is 100, confirm it in one or two sentences. No generic praise. Short paragraphs, no lists.
