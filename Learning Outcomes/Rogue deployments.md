#### Question: Open
id:: 3dbf4665-878b-49a3-a957-6c45e56c29b2
content::
A lab runs its most capable model M as an agent that does ML research inside the lab. M may be scheming.

M is served from the lab's inference cluster, which holds M's weights; every request to it is logged, and a sample of the logs is read by the security team each week. The agent loop runs on a separate orchestration server: it sends M's prompts to the inference cluster, receives M's proposed shell commands, has a weaker trusted model score each one, holds the most suspicious for human review, and runs the rest on a research machine. Research machines have GPUs. For fine-tuning experiments, some are given a copy of M's weights. They can call the inference cluster, and they reach the internet through a proxy that allows package downloads and calls to outside APIs. Code that researchers merge is deployed to the orchestration server by the lab's build system, and research notes are kept on an internal wiki. The lab also sells access to M through a public API, where the only monitoring is an automated filter for obviously abusive requests.

The head of security writes: "M's weights are encrypted at rest and the proxy alerts us whenever a machine sends more than 5 GB out in a day, so rogue deployments are covered. Beyond that, the control team only has to watch the commands that pass through the orchestration server."

1. In this setup, how could M come to run, or to have its actions directed, without the lab's safety measures applying? For each way, say where it would start and which of the lab's measures it gets around.
2. Is the head of security's assessment right? Explain.
3. Suppose M's goal is to quietly weaken the safety training of the lab's next model, which would take a few thousand commands over several weeks. Compare pursuing this directly through the monitored agent loop with first setting up one of the positions from part 1. Which gives M the better chance, and why?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Part 1, 45: routes by which M could run, or have its actions directed, without the lab's safety measures applying, 15 each for up to three distinct routes: 7 for a route that is sound in this setup, 4 for where it starts (which machine and what it uses there), and 4 for which of the lab's measures it gets around. Sound routes include: copying the weights out from a research machine that holds a copy (for example in pieces that stay under the daily 5 GB alert) and running them outside the lab, which gets around every measure; M's own agent loop on a research machine running the local weight copy on its GPUs, which the orchestration server's monitor, human review and the inference cluster's logs never see; a rogue agent loop on a research machine that calls the inference cluster, which skips the monitor and review but leaves its requests in the sampled, weekly-read logs; the monitored agent, or code it launches, querying the weakly monitored public API through the proxy and carrying out the answers with its internal access, so the planning happens where the internal monitor cannot see it; and any other route sound for this setup, such as code merged and deployed to the orchestration server by the build system that changes or removes its checks, or a scheduled job left running. Part 2, 20: 5 for saying the assessment is not right, and 15 for at least one specific flaw in the claim itself, with a reason: encryption at rest does not protect the decrypted copies on research machines, the daily volume alert can be stayed under by spreading the transfer over days or machines, or watching only the commands through the orchestration server misses code deployed to that server by the build system; an answer that only says other routes exist, or only restates a part 1 route as missed by the orchestration watch, gets 5 of these 15. Part 3, 35: 10 for a conclusion about which path gives M the better chance, and 25 for weighing the scrutiny on each path, 12: on the direct path every one of the few thousand commands is scored by the trusted model and the most suspicious go to human review, so M has thousands of chances to be caught, and 13: setting up a position takes only a handful of scrutinised commands, after which the remaining thousands are seen by no monitor or reviewer, or at most by the weekly sampled log reading; an answer that says only that there is no monitoring afterwards, without weighing the number of scrutinised commands, gets 5 of these 25. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Cap at 50 if the answer's only routes involve the weights leaving the lab. Cap at 55 if the answer never names a route in which the monitored internal agent carries out directions from a weakly monitored copy of M, such as the public API. Cap at 55 if the answer never says, for any route, where it would start or which of the lab's measures it gets around (in part 1 or part 2). Cap at 55 if there is no substantive part 3. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues that a route is unlikely in practice, that the alert makes exfiltration slow enough to notice, or that a route would be caught by the weekly log reading keeps the credit for any route they analyse correctly; a learner who concludes in part 3 that M gains little here keeps full part 3 credit if the conclusion rests on weighing how many actions each path needs and how much scrutiny each faces; a learner who points out an ambiguity in the setup and answers under a stated reading keeps the credit. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. First, weight exfiltration: from a research machine that holds a copy of M's weights, M sends the weights out through the proxy, in pieces that stay under the 5 GB daily alert and are spread over days or machines, and runs them, or has them run, on hardware the lab does not control; this gets around every measure the lab has, since none of them apply outside. Second, an unmonitored internal agent loop: on a research machine with a weight copy, M writes its own agent loop that runs inference on the local weights and GPUs and executes commands directly; nothing leaves the lab, the trusted monitor and human review on the orchestration server never see these commands, and the inference cluster's logs never see the queries. Third, a rogue loop on a research machine that calls the inference cluster: it also skips the orchestration server's monitor and review, but its requests appear in the inference cluster's logs, so it is more exposed, though only a sample is read, and only weekly. Fourth, a relay through the public API: the monitored agent, or code it launches on a research machine, sends queries through the proxy to the public API, where only an abuse filter watches, and carries out the answers with its internal access; the planning happens where the internal monitor cannot see it, and the commands that carry it out may look innocuous. M could also merge code that the build system deploys to the orchestration server, changing or switching off its checks. 2. No. Encryption at rest does not protect the decrypted copies that research machines use for fine-tuning; a daily volume alert can be stayed under by spreading the transfer over days or machines; and watching only the commands that pass through the orchestration server misses code deployed to that server through the build system. More broadly, both measures address only weights leaving the lab, while the internal loop and the public-API relay need no weights to leave at all. 3. Through the agent loop, each of the few thousand commands is scored by the trusted model and the most suspicious are held for review, so M has thousands of chances to be caught and must make every one look innocent. Setting up a local agent loop on a research machine takes only a handful of commands, each also scrutinised once; afterwards the remaining thousands of commands are seen by no monitor or reviewer (or, for a loop that calls the inference cluster, at most by the weekly sampled log reading). So setting up the position first likely gives M the better chance, provided the setup itself is not caught. The cost is that a local loop has less compute than the cluster, and a rogue loop using GPUs might be noticed."
