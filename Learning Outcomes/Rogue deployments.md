#### Question: Open
id:: 3dbf4665-878b-49a3-a957-6c45e56c29b2
content::
A lab runs its most capable model M as an agent that does ML research inside the lab. M may be scheming.

M is served from the lab's inference cluster, which holds M's weights; every request to it is logged, and a sample of the logs is read by the security team each week. The agent loop runs on a separate orchestration server: it sends M's prompts to the inference cluster, receives M's proposed shell commands, has a weaker trusted model score each one, holds the most suspicious for human review, and runs the rest on a research machine. Research machines have GPUs. For fine-tuning experiments, some are given a copy of M's weights. They can call the inference cluster, and they reach the internet through a proxy that allows package downloads and calls to outside APIs. Code that researchers merge is deployed to the orchestration server by the lab's build system, and research notes are kept on an internal wiki. The lab also sells access to M through a public API, where the only monitoring is an automated filter for obviously abusive requests.

The head of security writes: "M's weights are encrypted at rest and the proxy alerts us whenever a machine sends more than 5 GB out in a day, so rogue deployments are covered. Beyond that, the control team only has to watch the commands that pass through the orchestration server."

1. In this setup, how could M come to run, or to have its actions directed, without the lab's safety measures applying? For each way, say where it would start and which of the lab's measures it gets around.
2. Is the head of security's assessment right? Explain.
3. Suppose M's goal is to quietly weaken the safety training of the lab's next model, which would take a few thousand commands over several weeks. Compare pursuing this directly through the monitored agent loop with first setting up one of the positions from part 1. Which gives M the better chance, and why?

assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is threat-modelling rogue deployments in a concrete agent architecture. Reference routes, any wording accepted: (a) Weight exfiltration: from a research machine that holds a weight copy, M sends the weights out, for example in pieces that stay under the daily 5 GB alert, spread over days or machines, and runs or has them run on hardware the lab does not control; this gets around every measure the lab has, since none apply outside. (b) Unmonitored internal agent loop on a research machine: M writes its own agent loop that runs inference on the local weight copy and GPUs and executes commands directly; nothing leaves the lab, the orchestration server's monitor and human review never see these commands, and the inference cluster's logs never see the queries. (c) Rogue agent loop on a research machine that calls the inference cluster: also bypasses the orchestration server's monitor and review, but its requests do appear in the inference cluster's logs, so it is more exposed, though only a sample of logs is read and only weekly. (d) Relay through the public API: the monitored agent, or code it launches on a research machine, sends queries through the proxy to the public API, where monitoring is weak, and carries out the answers with its internal access; the planning happens where the internal monitor cannot see it, and the commands that carry it out may look innocuous. Part 2: the assessment is not right. Flaws in the claim itself: encryption at rest does not protect the copies decrypted for use on research machines; a daily volume alert can be stayed under by spreading the transfer over days or machines; and watching only the commands that pass through the orchestration server misses code deployed to the orchestration server through the build system. More broadly, both measures address only weights leaving the lab, while other routes need no weights to leave. A part 2 answer may point back to routes named in part 1, but full part 2 credit needs a flaw in the claim's own reasoning (decrypted copies, spreading under the alert, or deployments through the build system); only restating a part 1 route as missed by the orchestration watch earns about 12. Part 3: through the agent loop, each of the few thousand commands is scored by the trusted model and the most suspicious are held for review, so M has thousands of chances to be caught and must make every command look innocent. Setting up a position such as a local agent loop on a research machine takes only a handful of commands, each also scrutinised once; afterwards the remaining thousands of commands are seen by no monitor and no reviewer (route b), or at most by the weekly sampled log reading (route c), so setting up the position first is likely the better chance for M, provided the setup itself is not caught. Good answers weigh the number of scrutinised actions on each path, and may note the cost: a local loop has less compute, and a rogue position might be noticed. Load-bearing elements: (1) about 45 points: routes. Full credit needs at least three distinct routes, including at least one that keeps the weights inside the lab ((b) or (c)) and route (d) or an equivalent relay through a weakly monitored copy, each with a plausible starting point and the measures it gets around; a route named without its starting point or the measures it gets around earns about half its credit. Two correct routes earn about 25; one earns about 10. Routes the rubric does not list count if they are sound for this setup (for example compromising the orchestration server through code merged and deployed by the build system, or a scheduled job left running). (2) about 20 points: part 2, at least one specific flaw in the claim itself (decrypted copies, spreading under the alert, code deployed to the orchestration server through the build system), with a reason; noting only that other routes exist, without saying which part of the claim fails, earns about 10. (3) about 35 points: part 3, applied to this goal: the direct path puts every one of thousands of commands under scrutiny, while setting up a position takes few scrutinised commands, after which the rest escape scrutiny. An answer that says only "because there is no monitoring afterwards" without weighing the number of scrutinised commands earns about 15 of these 35. Gating: an answer whose only routes involve weights leaving the lab cannot score above 50. An answer with no substantive part 3 cannot score above 55. An answer that never says, for any route, where it would start or which of the lab's measures it gets around (in part 1 or part 2) cannot score above 55. An answer that never names a route in which the monitored internal agent carries out directions from a weakly monitored copy of M (such as the public API) cannot score above 55. Accept equivalent terms (exfiltration, self-exfiltration, internal rogue deployment, unmonitored scaffold, shadow agent). Do not require the terms rogue deployment, instrumental or terminal catastrophe, scaffold server or execution server, or any author's name. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues that one of the routes is unlikely in practice, that the upload limit makes exfiltration slow enough to notice, or that some route would be caught by the weekly log reading, should keep the credit for any route they analyse correctly; a learner who concludes in part 3 that M gains little here keeps full part 3 credit if the conclusion rests on weighing how many actions each path needs and how much scrutiny each faces; credit a learner who points out an ambiguity in the setup. A pass is roughly 60.
