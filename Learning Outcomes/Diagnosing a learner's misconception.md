## Test:
id:: 80797a8a-ba43-4d54-9fe3-01948fd1b752
#### Question: Open
id:: 8fb97d9f-9346-4d1b-a69d-c6ed7e6538fd
content::
Priya is new to machine learning and wants to work on AI safety. You showed her how you read a research paper: first the abstract, then the figures, then the conclusion, and only then the details, if you still need them.

A week later she tells you she has been "reading them your way". It still takes her three hours per paper, and afterwards she can't say what the paper showed. She thinks she is just slow.

1. Give two different guesses about what Priya might be doing or thinking that would explain this.

2. Pick one guess. Write the question you would ask Priya to find out whether it is right, without pointing out what she might be doing wrong.

3. Suppose her answer confirms that guess. What would you change in how you explain your method to the next beginner?
assessment-instructions:: The question describes Priya, who is new to machine learning. The learner showed her a way to read research papers: abstract, then figures, then conclusion, then details only if needed. A week later Priya says she reads papers that way, but each takes her three hours and afterwards she cannot say what the paper showed. She thinks she is just slow. The question asks for (1) two different guesses about what Priya might be doing or thinking that would explain this, (2) for one guess, a question to ask Priya that would show whether it is right without pointing out her possible error, and (3) assuming the guess is confirmed, a change in how the learner would explain the method to the next beginner.

Score out of 100.

35: Two guesses that are plausible, different from each other, and each a plausible explanation of what Priya reports. Examples: she reads each section in full detail instead of skimming it, she thinks she must understand every equation or term before moving on, she does the steps but then reads the whole paper from start to finish anyway, she does not know what question she is reading for, she understands "figures" as glancing at them rather than working out what each shows, or she treats the order as a ritual and reads the details first "to be safe". 35 for two such guesses, 15 for one, or for two that are nearly the same. "She is slow", "she is lazy" or "she didn't follow the method" without saying what she does instead count as no guess.

35: A curious question tied to the chosen guess. It asks about her experience or process (for example "Walk me through what you did with the last paper, from when you opened it", "What do you do when you hit an equation you don't understand?", "When you looked at the figures, what were you looking for?"), so that her answer would show whether the guess is right, and it does not point out the error or tell her what to do. 35 for such a question. 15 if the question is leading or corrective ("Did you try skipping the equations?", "You know you don't have to read every line, right?"), or does not bear on the chosen guess.

30: A change to the explanation for future beginners that follows from the confirmed guess, for example saying explicitly that the first pass skips equations and unknown terms, giving the reader one question to answer before they start, describing what to look for in a figure, or separating a quick first pass from a slow study pass as two different activities. 30 if the change addresses the confirmed misunderstanding, 10 if it is generic ("explain it more clearly", "give more examples") or does not follow from the guess.

Grade the question and the change against the guess the learner chose, even if that guess earned few points above. If the learner does not say which guess they chose, accept a question that tests either of them.

Cap the score at 30 if the answer mainly re-explains the method to Priya or tells her what to do, with no guesses about her thinking.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Guess 1: she thinks she has to understand every equation before moving on, so the abstract and figures take her an hour each. Guess 2: she reads the abstract, figures and conclusion as asked, but then reads the whole paper start to finish anyway, so the steps add time instead of saving it. Question for guess 1: 'Walk me through the last paper. What did you do when you reached the first equation you didn't follow?' If guess 1 is right, next time I'd say up front that the first pass skips anything you don't understand, and that the goal of the first pass is to answer one question: what did this paper find?"
feedback-instructions:: The learner diagnosed why a beginner, Priya, is not getting the result from a paper-reading method she says she follows, using the CFAR Handbook's idea of pedagogical content knowledge (PCK): get curious about what the learner is thinking, ask instead of correcting, and use what you learn to teach the next person better. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong. The most common miss is a question that is really a correction ("Did you try skipping the equations?"). If so, show how the same question could be asked as a curious one. If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold, for example: "What would Priya be doing at minute 40 if your method were working, and what might she be doing instead?"
