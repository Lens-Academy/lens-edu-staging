#### Question: Open
id:: 66253fbc-ee8f-4ea1-88f2-222b8f7b31c0
content:: The Closing Words offer two prayers. The first asks that the authors be proven wrong, shamed, and forgotten. The second, which the authors call their "true last prayer," is the book's final line.

**Distinguish what each prayer asks for. What would need to happen in the real world for the first prayer to be answered? For the second? And why do the authors call the second prayer their "true" one?**

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100. Pick the level that best describes the answer as a whole, then a score inside that level's range: near the top if the answer fully reaches the level, near the bottom if it only just does. Judge what the answer shows the learner understands, not only what it spells out: a correct extension that depends on a point shows that point is understood, even if the answer states it briefly. An extension shows nothing about points it does not depend on.

**Level 1 (0-20):** Cannot say what either prayer asks for, or runs them together, for example as both simply asking that humanity survive. *Example: "Both prayers are the authors hoping everything turns out okay."*

**Level 2 (21-40):** Says what one prayer asks for, or what both ask for, but not what would need to happen for either to be answered. *Example: "The first is about the authors being wrong and forgotten, and the second is the line telling humanity to rise to the occasion."*

**Level 3 (41-65):** Gives two of the three parts: what would answer the first prayer, what would answer the second, and why the second is the "true" one. Reading the first prayer as not meant (sarcasm or false modesty), so that the second is "true" because the first was never sincere, misses the third part: the authors do want to be wrong; they will not count on it. *Example: "The first comes true if AI just isn't dangerous after all and we're fine. The second comes true if people actually fight the danger and win."*

**Level 4 (66-85):** All three. The first prayer: this part is answered once the answer says it would be answered if the authors turn out to be wrong about the danger, so that humanity is fine without having to stop anything. The second prayer: this part is answered once the answer says it would be answered if people act against the danger and succeed in stopping it. Why the second is "true": this part is answered once the answer says the authors will not stake everything on being wrong, which would mean doing nothing, so the prayer they stand by is the one that calls for action. An answer that says only that the second is what the authors really believe or really want, without why they would not rest on the first, reaches this level but not fully. Saying as well what else the first prayer asks (that the authors be shamed and forgotten as an example of how not to think), quoting either prayer, or that the second is the book's last line, belongs to the same parts and is not needed for them. An answer that does all three parts has answered the question in full, and without an extension it stays in this range. *Example: Adds "Being wrong isn't something they can arrange, and betting on it means sitting still; the prayer they'll stand behind is the one that needs people to move."*

**Level 5 (86-100):** All of level 4, plus a correct extension beyond what the question asks. An extension is a further point with its own reasoning; restating the points above in more precise or technical terms is still level 4. Two that fit: that the two prayers do not conflict (the authors can sincerely hope to be wrong and still call for action, since acting costs nothing if they are wrong and saves everything if they are right; saying only that they prefer the first but will not rely on it is level 4); or that the second prayer is addressed to the reader (it is a call to act rather than a wish, which is why it ends the book). Another extension of the same substance counts equally. Within this range the score reflects the whole answer: near the top only when the level 4 points and the extension are both well developed, and near the bottom when a strong extension rests on a thin base. *Example: Adds "The last line isn't really a prayer to anyone above; it's an instruction to us."*

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Respond to the argument the learner actually made, and credit only what the answer says: do not attribute to them a point they did not make. Aim the reply at the idea they are missing, not at the score: frame the push as something about the material, never as what would earn more points. If they ask about their score, explain it by what the answer showed and what it left out, without naming levels or bands.

Open on the strongest thing in their answer and why it holds, in one sentence. If the answer goes beyond the question while one of its three parts (what would answer the first prayer, what would answer the second, and why the second is the "true" one) is only asserted, that part is the push: deepening the base comes before going further. Otherwise, push once, chosen by where they stopped:

- If they ran the two prayers together, ask what the first asks to happen to the authors and what the second asks of humanity.
- If they said what each prayer asks for but not what would answer it, ask what the world would look like if each were answered.
- If they read the first prayer as not meant, ask whether the authors would really be glad to be wrong, and what that changes about why the second is the one they stand by.
- If they had both conditions but not why the second is the true one, or said only that it is what the authors really believe, ask why the authors would not rest their hope on the first.
- If they answered all three parts and went no further, the moves left go beyond the question: whether the two prayers conflict, or who the second prayer is addressed to. Ask about whichever their answer comes closest to.

If they made one of these moves, or another of the same substance, with all three parts solid, say so plainly in a sentence or two after the opening, without walking back through the parts they got right, and do not invent a further push. If you add a question, make it one they can take further out of interest, not one that suggests the answer was missing something. This reply can be shorter than the length below.

If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold: the second prayer is the book's last line, "Rise to the occasion, humanity, and win." Ask what it requires that the first does not. If they still don't attempt it, rephrase the question.

At most one follow-up question. No generic praise, and do not recite the rubric back to them.

Response length: 100 to 160 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
