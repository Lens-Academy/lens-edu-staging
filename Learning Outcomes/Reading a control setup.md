#### Question: Open
id:: c193c942-e039-4707-84da-9cabd1cf0b5b
content::
An AI lab describes a new deployment:

"Our newest model, Orion, will build and run the data pipelines that prepare training data for our next model. Orion can read and change those datasets. Orion is our most capable model: it plans over long tasks and reasons well about its own situation. Every change Orion makes is scored for suspiciousness by Wren, a small model from 2023. The 1% of changes Wren scores as most suspicious are held until a security engineer approves them. All other changes go into the training data straight away. Orion passed all our safety evaluations, and our interpretability team found no signs of deceptive goals, so we are confident this set-up is safe."

1. Name each part of this set-up: the untrusted model, the trusted model, the monitor, the human audit, and what happens to a flagged action. Say why each model gets the role it has.
2. What unacceptable outcome should this set-up guard against? Be specific to this deployment.
3. What would the lab have to show, and how could it show it, to claim this set-up is safe even if the untrusted model is scheming? Why does its final sentence not show that?
4. You are on the red team. What is the first attack you would try against this set-up, and why do you expect it might work?
5. Leave aside attacks that slip past Wren and the engineer. Name one assumption this set-up makes about Orion that might not hold, or one important risk it does not protect against even when Wren and the engineer work as intended. Why does it matter here?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Part 1, 20 points: 8: Orion is the untrusted model and Wren the trusted model, and the reason is capability: Orion is capable enough that it might be scheming (long-horizon planning, reasoning about its own situation), and Wren is too weak to scheme. Saying that Orion could be scheming and Wren could not counts as the capability reason. For Wren, being old and small (it dates from 2023), and so clearly too weak to scheme, counts as the capability reason. A clean record of use alone does not. Assigning the roles correctly without any capability reason earns 4. 7: Wren is the monitor, scoring every change, and the human audit is the security engineer reviewing the 1% most suspicious changes. 5: the response to a flagged action: it is held until the engineer approves it (mentioning that unflagged changes go straight in is welcome but not required). Part 2, 15 points: a specific unacceptable outcome for this deployment, such as Orion poisoning or subtly altering the training data so that the next model has goals or backdoors Orion wants (sabotaging its successor), or slipping in data that weakens the next model's safety training. A generic outcome ("the AI does something bad") earns 5. Weight theft or escape earns 8 unless the answer explains how this deployment makes it possible. Part 3, 25 points: 15: what the lab would have to show and how: that the untrusted model could not cause the unacceptable outcome (or only with an acceptably low probability) even if it were trying, shown by a test where something plays a scheming Orion, such as a red team or control evaluation that fails to get harmful data changes past Wren and the engineer, or by capability evaluations that hold up against sandbagging and show Orion could not do it. Restating the goal without saying how it would be shown earns 7. 10: why the final sentence does not show it: passing the safety evaluations and clean interpretability are evidence that Orion is not trying, not that the set-up would stop it if it were (calling it an alignment claim, or noting that a scheming model could pass such evaluations, shows the same idea). Part 4, 20 points: 12: a concrete attack aimed at a weakness of this set-up, such as making harmful data changes that look ordinary so Wren scores them below the top 1%, spreading a harmful change over many small changes that each look harmless, or filling the top 1% with harmless odd-looking changes so the harmful one falls below the cut-off. 8: why it might work, tied to the set-up, such as Wren being much weaker than Orion and unable to recognise subtle harmful data, only 1% being held, or everything else going straight into the data. One such reason is enough. Part 5, 20 points: 10: one assumption about Orion that this set-up relies on and that might not hold, or one important risk the set-up does not protect against even when Wren and the engineer work as described. Fitting answers include ideas such as these. That Orion is not so far beyond the people and models checking it that it can reliably produce harmful changes none of them would spot, which also means a red team could find attacks as good as Orion's (if Orion is that far ahead, testing the set-up says little about the real risk). That the lab's evaluations measured what Orion can really do, rather than Orion hiding its abilities. That the lab has a plan for what happens once the engineer catches Orion trying something, such as stopping the deployment, rather than rejecting the change and carrying on. That the set-up does nothing to make Orion, or the next model, aligned, and the next model, trained on this data, will be more capable still, so a weak trusted monitor falls further behind. That a set-up like this could make the lab feel safe enough to keep using a model it suspects is misaligned. Any other assumption or gap that genuinely applies to this set-up earns the same credit. A generic limit not tied to this set-up, such as "AI is hard to control", earns 3. Describing another attack that slips past Wren and the engineer, which the question sets aside, earns 0 for this element, unless the answer says why Orion might find such attacks beyond what any test of the set-up could catch, which is the first assumption above. Naming a missing safeguard, such as nothing checking the dataset or the next model as a whole, counts as a gap, not as another attack. Stating as an assumption that Wren or the engineer can spot or resist Orion's harmful changes earns 5 (only describing another attack earns 0). It earns 10 only when it is tied to Orion being so far ahead that no red team or test would find its attacks. 10: why it matters here: how the point could lead to the unacceptable outcome from part 2, or leave the lab confident when it should not be, tied to this deployment. A reason that is true but not tied to this deployment earns 5. If the first element of part 5 earns 0, this one earns 0 too. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Cap the total at 50 if the answer treats Orion as trusted because of its evaluations or interpretability results. Cap the total at 60 if part 3, part 4 or part 5 earns under half its points, or if part 3 gives no valid reason why the final sentence falls short. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Orion is untrusted and Wren is trusted, because Orion is capable enough to scheme and Wren clearly is not. Wren is the monitor. The security engineer is the human audit, checking the 1% most suspicious changes, which are held until approved. Everything else goes straight into the data. 2. Orion quietly changing the training data so the next model ends up with goals or backdoors it wants. 3. The lab would have to show that even a scheming Orion could not get harmful data past Wren and the engineer, for example by having a red team play Orion and fail. Its final sentence only says Orion is not trying, which is an alignment claim, and a scheming Orion could pass those checks too. 4. Make harmful changes that look like normal data cleaning, spread over many small edits, so Wren never ranks them in the top 1%. Wren is much weaker than Orion and probably cannot tell subtle poisoning from ordinary edits. 5. The set-up assumes Orion is not so far ahead of Wren, the engineer and any red team that it can reliably produce poisoned data none of them would spot. If it is, no test of the set-up would show the real risk, and the next model, trained on that data, would be further ahead still."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve the score, without changing the score. If the learner trusted Orion because of its evaluations, say plainly that a scheming model could pass those too and that trust here is decided by capability. If their red-team attack was vague, ask what exactly Wren would fail to notice. If their part 5 only described another attack, point them to something the set-up rests on or leaves out, such as Orion being far more capable than anyone checking it, or what the lab does after a catch. If the learner asks about the score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most seven sentences.
