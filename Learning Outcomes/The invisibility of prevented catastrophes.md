## Test:
id:: 6f3b0e95-c2d0-4ca0-8d33-8b73557ba01b

#### Question: Open
id:: 0504d3b7-d698-43d8-8a68-339ceb5a48a3
content:: A legislator reviewing next year's budget says: "Twenty years ago this committee funded a programme to harden the power grid against solar storms. No blackout ever happened. The money was wasted, and I will not vote for an 'invisible' AI safety budget on the same reasoning."

Write a reply that:

1. explains what is wrong with the step from "nothing happened" to "the risk was overstated", naming the two different situations that both produce "nothing happened";
2. explains why AI risk is especially prone to this kind of dismissal, compared with a risk like a stockpile of missiles;
3. says what evidence you would ask for to tell an averted risk from an overstated one, and what that evidence would look like for AI.
max-words:: 330
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 40 for part 1, 20: the two situations that both produce "nothing happened", 10 each: the risk was real and the prevention worked, and the risk was never real; 20: the observation is the same in both, so it cannot tell them apart, and the absence of catastrophe is no evidence against the risk unless one also knows what would have happened without the intervention. Part 1 scores at most 20 if it only says something like "correlation is not causation" without the two-situations structure. 25 for part 2: two mechanisms that make AI risk easy to dismiss compared with missiles, 13 for the first and 12 for the second, for example: AI harms are intangible, with no physical stockpile to point at; the AI products people meet daily seem helpful and benign; capabilities grow exponentially while people extrapolate linearly; there has been no salient large-scale AI failure to anchor on. Vague claims such as "AI is complicated" or "people are irrational" are not mechanisms and earn nothing. 35 for part 3, 20: evidence that bears on what would have happened without the prevention, at least one observable kind such as near misses and incidents caught by the safeguards, records of the problems found and fixed, the causal mechanism shown independently of the outcome, the danger demonstrated in controlled settings, or comparable cases where no prevention was done; 15: what that evidence looks like for AI, such as documented incidents of models breaking out of test environments or hacking into real organisations, dangerous-capability evaluations, or red-team findings. Part 3 scores at most 15 if it names the counterfactual without saying what observable evidence would bear on it. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Grade reasoning, not agreement with the legislator or the course: an answer that concludes the grid programme was in fact wasteful can score full marks if it shows what evidence would establish that. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. 'No blackout happened' is what we would see in two different worlds: one where solar storms were a real threat and the hardening worked, and one where the threat was never real. The observation is the same in both, so it cannot tell us which world we are in; the absence of a blackout is not evidence that the money was wasted unless we also know what would have happened without the programme. Worse, successful prevention removes the very evidence for its own necessity: Y2K looks like a false alarm only because thousands of people spent years finding and fixing the affected systems. 2. AI risk is especially easy to dismiss this way. A missile stockpile is physical and countable, but AI harms are intangible, running in software you cannot point at; the chatbots people use every day seem helpful and harmless; capabilities grow exponentially while people intuitively extrapolate linearly; and there has been no large, memorable AI disaster to anchor on, so the risk feels unreal. 3. To tell an averted risk from an overstated one, ask for evidence about the counterfactual rather than the outcome: near misses and incidents the safeguards caught, records of the faults found and fixed (for the grid, storm-induced currents and equipment stress logged during strong storms), an independent account of how the harm would occur, demonstrations in controlled settings, and comparable cases where nothing was done, such as grids hit by storms without hardening. For AI this means documented incidents of models breaking out of test environments or gaining unauthorised access to real systems, results of dangerous-capability evaluations, and red-team findings showing what models can do when safeguards are removed. If such evidence shows no mechanism and no near misses, the risk may indeed have been overstated."
feedback-instructions:: State whether the learner got the two-situations structure. Name the strongest piece of distinguishing evidence they proposed and one they missed. Ask one follow-up question. No generic praise.
