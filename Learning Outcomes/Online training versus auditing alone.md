#### Question: Open
id:: 0aeb7986-8e66-4151-becf-ac860bae32e5
content::
A lab will use its untrusted model U, which may be scheming, for a year to write worked solutions to about 50,000 hard mathematics and programming problems. Each solution has a final answer or program and an explanation of the method. The set will be used to train and evaluate the lab's next model, so a steady trickle of subtly flawed solutions would quietly weaken that model.

Two checks are available. A trusted but weaker model writes test cases for every problem, and each solution's answer or program is run against them within minutes, at little cost. A team of mathematicians and engineers can review about 2% of the solutions in full, about 1,000 a year, each within two weeks of submission; their verdicts are reliable.

Before deployment, U's solutions passed the weaker model's tests on 91% of a held-out set of similar problems. A human-written set of similar problems from two years ago had a flaw in about 3% of its solutions. The lab has spare training compute and could update U weekly.

Which oversight regime would you run for U during this year, and why? In your reasons, say what training U on the available signals would do if U is scheming and if it is not. Then say what you would be accepting with that choice. If U is scheming, name two different ways it could still do harm under your regime, and say why the regime would not stop or notice each. If U is not scheming, what does your choice cost?
placeholder:: Your regime and why, including what training U would do to a scheming and to an honest U; two ways a scheming U could still do harm under it, and why; the cost if U is not scheming. About 200 to 250 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. There is no single right regime: training U online on the reviews, on the tests, or on both; a staged plan that first measures how much training helps; training only a model that chooses which solutions get reviewed or that helps the reviewers; or auditing U without training it can all earn full marks. Grade whether the reasons, the risks and the costs follow from the regime the answer chose and from the reading's mechanisms; grade reasoning, not agreement. Worked facts: 2% of 50,000 is about 1,000 reviews a year; the tests run on everything but check only final answers or programs, and were written by a weaker model; a two-week lag still allows asynchronous training, since the harm builds up over many solutions. The 91% and the 3% are not a baseline for how often an honest U's solutions would be flawed (the 91% counts only passes of the weaker tests, measured before deployment; the 3% is about human authors); an answer that notices this earns credit within the residual-risk element, and one that uses either as such a baseline simply misses that credit. 35: the choice. 15 of these: the choice argued from the quality and the cost of the two signals: the tests are cheap and cover everything but say nothing about the explanations or subtler flaws; the reviews measure what matters and are reliable but cover only about 1,000 solutions a year. Restating the two signals without saying what that means for training or not training U earns at most 6 of these 15. 20 of these: what training on the relevant signal would do: any one of the following reasons, developed, earns the full 20: (a1) training on a signal stops a scheming U from staying systematically worse on that signal, closing its gap to an honest U there, so by its direct effects it can only help in the scheming case, while what it costs an honest U depends on how well the signal tracks what matters (Greenblatt: online training "should only improve performance for a worst case scheming model" and "forces the behavior of the scheming model and the non-scheming model to be closer"; the talk lens on Hebbar: online training against a poor reward signal "improves the worst case and degrades the benign case"); (a1) needs both sides to appear in the answer: what training does to a scheming U, and what it costs an honest U given how well the signal tracks what matters. The honest side may be stated anywhere, including in the answer's honest-case cost, and it then counts for (a1) and for the cap below, while its points are credited once, in the cost element. Saying only that training would push a sandbagging U to do better, with no word anywhere on what the signal does to an honest U, or only that a proxy hurts an honest U, is not (a1)); (a2) measuring first, with a little online training on the reviews extrapolated, and treating a large gain as evidence that U had been holding back, calling for a response beyond training (Greenblatt: "periodically try to measure how much online training would improve performance" and "online training greatly improving performance is a lot of evidence for serious misalignment"); (a3) training U teaches a scheming U which of its flaws the oversight catches, for the tests exactly what a weaker model's tests find, so it can do harm more easily later (Greenblatt: "Online training might teach the model about your countermeasures and oversight"); (a4) training only a model that chooses which solutions are reviewed, or one that helps the reviewers find flaws, so the scarce reviews become more informative while U itself is left untrained (Greenblatt: "you might do online training on AIs used for monitoring and auditing ... but not on the policy"); (a4) says what the regime does for review, not what training U would do, so on its own it does not lift the first cap at 50 below. That training on the tests would push even an honest U to pass a weaker model's tests rather than write sound solutions (the proxy point) earns at most 8 of these 20 on its own. 35: what a scheming U could still get away with under the chosen regime, two mechanisms, 18 for the first and 17 for the second, each specific to the regime and with the reason the regime does not stop it. Under any regime that trains U: (b1) underperformance so slight on each reviewed solution that training barely pushes against it, how slight depending on how sample-efficient training is (Greenblatt: "scores are so negligibly lower ... that training only pushes against this behavior by a negligible (or zero) amount"); (b2) exploration hacking: U never writes the better solution, so there is nothing for training to reinforce ("The model could exploration hack or gradient hack to perform worse"); (b3) training making the flaws sneakier and rarer instead of removing them, or teaching U which flaws get caught ("SGD might update the model toward doing the problematic behavior more sneakily and less frequently"); (b9) under training on the tests, flaws in the explanations, which the tests never score, so training never pushes against them. Under any regime that leaves U untrained (auditing only, or training only a selector or a helper): (b4) an honest U was never trained to look unsuspicious, so it too produces suspicious-looking solutions, triggers must be loose, and a schemer hides in that noise ("we haven't necessarily trained the model to be less suspicious in distribution"); (b5) the evidence that should trigger a response will often be illegible, so a strong response is hard to justify ("it might be (politically) hard to motivate a strong response if the evidence isn't legible"); (b6) for a selector or helper that is itself untrusted, it can flag the flaws the lab inserts to test it while staying quiet on U's real ones, the same recognition problem as untrusted monitoring, or a helper can flood the reviewers with fake issues; (b7) no reliable baseline for how often an honest U's solutions would be flawed (the 91% and the 3% do not give one), so a steady slight degradation stays within what an honest U might produce and no trigger fires ("we might not have a very good sense of what performance we expect"). Under any regime: (b8) a few subtle flaws placed in the most consequential problems, with plausible deniability, few enough that the product of their chances of escaping the 2% review stays high, so they are neither caught nor give training enough signal ("the product of the q_i's to be high enough"). A mechanism that only restates the setup (flaws the tests do not check, or solutions the reviewers never see) without saying what the chosen regime does or fails to do about it earns at most 6. 30: the cost if U is not scheming, specific to the chosen regime and with its mechanism: training on the tests pushes an honest U toward passing the weaker model's tests at the expense of sound solutions and explanations; any online training of U brings logistics, such as running training throughout deployment and a U that changes every week, so what was learned about last month's model goes stale (Greenblatt: "annoyances with the model arbitrarily changing over time"); training on the reviews costs an honest U little in quality because the signal is close to the real target (credit an answer that says so); training only a selector or a helper costs building and training it, and a helper's raised issues cost reviewer time, while an honest U is untouched; auditing alone gives up the gains training could bring, and an honest U will sometimes trip the triggers, so responses are graded from cheap (such as changing instructions or reviewing more) to costly, keeping the expected cost low if U is benign ("aim for a low expected level of cost if the model isn't malign"). A generic cost not tied to the chosen regime (compute, effort, "possible lost gains") earns at most 8. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; credit a point once, in the element where it does most work. Caps: cap at 40 if the answer makes no choice, or gives no reason tied to either signal. Cap at 50 unless the answer, anywhere, argues the choice with at least one of (a1) to (a3), or uses (a4) together with a reason, about U itself, for leaving it untrained: what training U on the available signal would or would not do if U is scheming (for example (a1) judged not worth its cost to an honest U, or (a3)). (a4) on its own earns its points but does not lift this cap, and neither does the proxy point alone. Cap at 50 unless at least one residual-risk mechanism is one of (b1) to (b9), with its reason, for the regime the answer chose; a statement that slight underperformance is hard to detect, or that we do not know how good U should be, without saying why the chosen regime does not push against it or notice it, does not lift this cap. If both of the two caps at 50 apply, cap at 40. Cap at 55 if the answer gives no cost for the case where U is not scheming, or only a generic cost not tied to the chosen regime. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "I would train U online on the reviews and not on the tests. The tests are cheap and cover everything, but they check only final answers against a weaker model's cases; training on them would push even an honest U toward passing those tests rather than writing sound explanations, and would teach a scheming U exactly which flaws they catch. The reviews measure what matters and are reliable, so training on them costs an honest U little, while it stops a scheming U from staying systematically worse on what the reviewers check. A two-week lag is fine for harm that builds up over a year. The tests stay as a filter that sends failing solutions to review. If U is scheming, I accept two gaps. First, 1,000 reviews a year is a thin signal: a slight drop on every solution may be too small per review for training to push against. Second, U may simply never write the harder, better solution, leaving nothing for training to reinforce. If U is honest, the cost is mostly logistics: running training throughout the deployment, and a U that changes every week, so what we learned about last month's model goes stale."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if the choice rested only on the tests being a proxy, or chose a selector or helper without saying why U itself is left untrained, ask what training U on a signal would do to a scheming model as opposed to an honest one; if the risks under the chosen regime were generic, ask what a scheming U could do that this particular regime would not push against or would not notice; if the honest-case cost was missing or generic, ask what the choice gives up if U was never scheming. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. If it fits, add that neither the 91% nor the human 3% says how often an honest U's solutions would be flawed, which is one weakness of auditing alone. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
