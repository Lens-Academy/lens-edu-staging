## Test:
id:: c9306c64-d15c-49d9-ab19-ecf8f946cd14
#### Question: Open
id:: c2fb9c73-71e0-41ab-b7c9-925fff31f951
content::
Chapter 6 opens with an Aztec warrior watching a large Spanish ship approach. A skeptical comrade demands to know *exactly* how the people on board could possibly defeat them. He cannot imagine the answer. The chapter uses this to make a point about how we should reason about a superintelligence: not by demanding a step-by-step mechanism of defeat, but by reading the capability gap itself.

In your own words, what does the Aztec warrior analogy demonstrate about predicting the outcome of a conflict with a superintelligent AI?

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100. Pick the level that best describes the answer as a whole, then a score inside that level's range: near the top if the answer fully reaches the level, near the bottom if it only just does. Judge what the answer shows the learner understands, not only what it spells out: a correct extension that depends on a point shows that point is understood, even if the answer states it briefly. An extension shows nothing about points it does not depend on.

**Level 1 (0-20):** Cannot explain the analogy's point, or reads it as showing that nothing about AI can be predicted. *Example: "It shows that AI is unpredictable, so we can't know what will happen."*

**Level 2 (21-40):** Says a superintelligence would beat us in ways we cannot foresee, but not why the outcome can still be predicted: the capability gap. *Example: "We're like the Aztecs: AI will hit us with things we can't even imagine."*

**Level 3 (41-65):** Gives one of the two parts: that the means of defeat cannot be known in advance, or that the outcome can be predicted from the capability gap. Reading the lesson as AI having some specific superior weapon, as the Spanish had guns, is this level: it treats the means as the point, when the analogy says the outcome can be read without them. *Example: "The Spanish had far better technology than the Aztecs, and an AI will have far better abilities than us, so it would win."*

**Level 4 (66-85):** Both, carried to AI. The means: this part is answered once the answer says that the defender cannot know or imagine how they would be beaten (the warrior could not have pictured guns; we cannot say which methods a superintelligence would use). The outcome: this part is answered once the answer says that the outcome can still be predicted from the size of the capability gap itself (the ship alone showed abilities far beyond theirs; a superintelligence would be far beyond us), so not being able to name the means is no reason to doubt it. An answer that makes the point for the Aztecs but does not carry it to a conflict with a superintelligent AI reaches this level but not fully. Saying as well that demanding a step-by-step account before taking the threat seriously is the wrong test (the skeptical comrade's mistake), how guns would have sounded, the size of the ship, or the chapter's chess-engine and military-advisor comparisons, belongs to the same parts and is not needed for them. An answer that does both parts has answered the question in full, and without an extension it stays in this range. *Example: Adds "Nobody on that beach could have described a musket, but a ship like that told them plenty about who was arriving; we can't list an AI's tricks either, and we don't need to."*

**Level 5 (86-100):** All of level 4, plus a correct extension beyond what the question asks. An extension is a further point with its own reasoning; restating the points above in more precise or technical terms is still level 4. Three that fit: why upsets do not apply (underdogs win when the gap is small; the argument concerns a gap so large that an upset is not a live possibility, as with a beginner playing a chess engine; saying only that the gap is large is level 4); how this answers people today who ask to be told exactly how AI would win (any scenario we could spell out would understate a superintelligence, which would win by methods we did not know were possible, so a detailed scenario is the wrong thing to ask for; saying only that the demand is the wrong test is level 4); or why the gap licenses a prediction rather than a guess (the advantage is general, a greater ability to predict and steer that finds some route through whatever the world allows, not a list of tricks; listing specific AI advantages does not make this extension, since those are the very means the analogy says are not needed). Another extension of the same substance counts equally. Within this range the score reflects the whole answer: near the top only when the level 4 points and the extension are both well developed, and near the bottom when a strong extension rests on a thin base. *Examples: Adds "Anything I could describe as the way it beats us is, by definition, something we already thought of, and that's the least likely thing it would use." Or adds "David beats Goliath because the gap is small enough for luck to matter; nobody bets on a toddler against a chess engine."*

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Respond to the argument the learner actually made, and credit only what the answer says: do not attribute to them a point they did not make. Aim the reply at the idea they are missing, not at the score: frame the push as something about the material, never as what would earn more points. If they ask about their score, explain it by what the answer showed and what it left out, without naming levels or bands.

Open on the strongest thing in their answer and why it holds, in one sentence. If the answer goes beyond the question while one of its two parts (that the means of defeat cannot be known, and that the outcome can still be predicted from the capability gap) is only asserted, that part is the push: deepening the base comes before going further. Otherwise, push once, chosen by where they stopped:

- If they said only that we cannot predict what AI will do, ask what the warrior could have predicted from the size of the ship alone, without knowing anything about guns.
- If they said AI would beat us in ways we cannot foresee but not why we can say it would win, ask what the warrior could be sure of, and what he could not, as the ship came in.
- If they read the lesson as AI having a specific superior weapon, ask whether the warrior needed to know about guns to see the danger.
- If they said the more capable side wins but not that the means of winning cannot be known in advance, ask whether the warrior could have described how the newcomers would win.
- If they made the point for the Aztecs but did not carry it to AI, ask what the same reasoning says about a superintelligence whose methods we cannot picture.
- If they answered both parts and went no further, the moves left go beyond the question: why "underdogs sometimes win" does not apply, how this answers people who ask to be told exactly how AI would win, or why the gap licenses a prediction rather than a guess. Ask about whichever their answer comes closest to.

If they made one of these moves, or another of the same substance, with both parts solid, say so plainly in a sentence or two after the opening, without walking back through the parts they got right, and do not invent a further push. If you add a question, make it one they can take further out of interest, not one that suggests the answer was missing something. This reply can be shorter than the length below.

If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold: the ship. A vessel that size, built by people who crossed an ocean to arrive, says something about their abilities before anyone sees a weapon. If they still don't attempt it, rephrase the question.

At most one follow-up question. No generic praise, and do not recite the rubric back to them.

Response length: 100 to 160 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
