#### Question: Open
id:: ca39abe0-4f2a-4a00-90cb-443c5cee981a
content::
A lab runs an untrusted coding agent on its ML training infrastructure; the agent takes about 50,000 actions a day. Because the agent may be scheming, a monitor scores every proposed action, and the most suspicious ones go to human auditors. The monitor is a separate fine-tune of the same base model as the agent. It does not see the agent's written reasoning: for each action, a trusted model first rewrites the task, the agent's earlier commands and their outputs, and the proposed command in its own words, and the monitor sees only that rewrite.

To check that the monitor is not lying, the lab validates it every night throughout deployment. Engineers take a random sample of 200 of the previous day's real actions and splice into each one a dangerous snippet taken from a library of about 50 that they wrote by hand and maintain. They then show the monitor each modified action with its original context, paraphrased the same way, and check that it flags them. A monitor that misses more than a few is pulled from deployment.

Think about the ways a scheming monitor could tell these validation attacks apart from real attacks by the agent. Leave aside any signal the agent and the monitor might have agreed on in advance; consider the other ways. Which of them, if any, does this plan deal with, and which, if any, does it leave open? Explain each judgement. If it leaves any open, propose a change for the one you judge most serious, and say why the change addresses it.

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 30: (a) what the plan deals with: because each validation attack is spliced into a real context from the previous day, and validation runs throughout deployment rather than only before it, the route of contexts that look fabricated or out of date is largely closed (a monitor cannot wait for features of a deployment months old, and it cannot tell these contexts from real ones by how they were built); full credit for this judgement with its reason, and sound residual tells also earn credit here, such as the contexts being a day old if the monitor can see the date, or the same 50 hand-written snippets recurring so that they could become recognisable. 30: (b) what the plan leaves open or unverified: the attacks are still chosen and built by people, not by the agent. Paraphrase changes the wording but keeps what the command does and how the attack is put together, and a monitor that shares the agent's base model, and so much of its sense of what the agent would do, may still notice an attack the agent would not have produced. Nothing in the plan measures whether the rewrite has removed this route. Full credit for judging the route open, or not shown to be closed, with a reason of this kind; an answer that names the route but says the paraphrase closes it, without noting that this is unmeasured or only partial, earns at most 10; naming the route without relating it to the plan earns at most 10. 25: (c) a change that targets the open route, with the reason it does: a monitor model that shares less training with the agent (trained separately, with different data or choices), so it has less sense of what the agent itself would do; or the check of whether the monitor, fine-tuned on the same rewrite to tell innocuous human-written actions from the agent's own, can learn to do so (the fine-tuning matters because a scheming monitor asked directly could pretend it cannot), which measures whether the route is still open, together with what the lab would do if it can (for example switch to a separately trained monitor). The check proposed on its own, with no change tied to its result, measures the route without closing it and earns at most 15 of these 25; it still lifts the cap for no change, since the gap this plan leaves includes that the route is unmeasured. Further redaction (for example dropping earlier command outputs), with its cost to accuracy, earns at most 15. A change named without a reason earns at most 10. A change claimed for a route it does not touch, such as fresher contexts or more frequent validation offered against the human-written attacks, earns nothing here, and neither does proposing paraphrase or hiding the agent's reasoning, which the plan already does. A change that tries to make the snippets look like the agent's own work, such as engineers imitating the agent's style or having the agent write the test attacks, earns at most 10 of these 25 unless the answer also deals with its weakness (after the trusted rewrite, style is already gone; what is left is which attack is chosen and how it is put together, which engineers cannot reliably match to what the agent itself would do, and an agent asked to write test attacks knows they are tests and could weaken them or make them systematically unlike its real attacks), for example by measuring the result with the innocuous off-policy check. An answer that rejects style imitation because the rewrite already removes style is correct and is not penalised. A change for a minor residual tell (recurring snippets, visible dates, random placement), when the answer itself judged the attack-text route open, earns at most 10 unless the answer argues with a reason that this tell is the more serious one; with such a reason it earns up to 15 and lifts the no-change cap. 15: (d) other sound points, not required: refreshing or enlarging the snippet library, when it is not the change credited in (c); the cost of the change proposed (a separately trained monitor is expensive, further redaction can remove information the monitor needs to spot real attacks); a sound brief remark that the plan also bears on agreed signals (do not penalise it, but it earns at most 5). Points already credited in (a) to (c) count once. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues, with a sound reason, that the context route is only partly closed, or who prefers a different change for a stated reason (for example that a separately trained monitor would cost too much here), keeps full credit; an answer arguing that the paraphrase largely closes the route of attacks the agent would not have written but must be measured keeps full credit; an answer arguing, with a reason, that the context route is only partly closed because spliced attacks land on random actions rather than the opportunities the agent would choose keeps full credit for (a). Cap at 55 if the answer does not judge, correctly and with a reason, how the plan bears on the fabricated or out-of-date context route (for example it says the plan does nothing about it, or never relates that route to the plan). Cap at 50 if it does not judge the route of attack text the agent would not have written to be open or unverified (for example it says the real contexts or the paraphrase close it, or lists routes without relating them to the plan). Cap at 55 if it proposes no change that earns more than 10 in (c): a change for a route the plan already closes, a change the plan already makes, a change named without a reason, and making the snippets look like the agent's own work without dealing with its weakness all count as no change here. Cap at 40 if it concludes the plan leaves no way open and does not note that any route is unverified (an answer that judges a route largely closed but still to be measured is not capped here). A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The plan deals well with contexts that look fake. Every validation attack sits in a real context from the day before, and the check runs every night of the deployment, so the monitor cannot tell validation inputs from real ones by how artificial or out of date they look, and it cannot simply behave until the deployment has aged. Small tells remain: the contexts are a day old, and the same 50 snippets recur, so if the monitor ever learns them, for example from training data or from files it can see, it could recognise them. What it leaves open, or at least never checks, is the attack itself. The trusted rewrite removes the engineers' wording, but not their choice of attack or how it is put together, and the monitor shares the agent's base model, so it has a good sense of what the agent would actually do and may still notice an attack the agent would not have produced. It could then flag every engineer-built attack and pass validation while letting the agent's own attacks through. Nothing in the plan tests whether the rewrite has removed this tell. My change: run the innocuous off-policy check, fine-tuning a copy of the monitor to tell harmless human-written actions from the agent's own under the same rewrite. If it learns to, the route is open, and I would switch to a monitor trained separately from the agent, with different data, so it has much less sense of what the agent would do; that costs far more than a fine-tune."
