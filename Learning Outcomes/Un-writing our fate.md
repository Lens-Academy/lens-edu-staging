#### Question: Open
id:: d9da4232-9cb8-4b6e-a489-177a769365a1
content:: Chapter 14 opens with Vesna Vulović, a flight attendant who survived a 10.1-kilometer fall when terrorists bombed her plane. The chapter then invokes the nuclear arms race: in 1952, a nuclear war seemed rationally predictable based on centuries of evidence about human conflict, yet it didn't happen. The chapter calls what prevented it "un-writing a fate already written."

**In your own words: what does the chapter mean by "un-writing a fate already written"? What happened during the Cold War that the chapter uses as evidence that un-writing a fate is actually possible?**

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100. Pick the level that best describes the answer as a whole, then a score inside that level's range: near the top if the answer fully reaches the level, near the bottom if it only just does. Judge what the answer shows the learner understands, not only what it spells out: a correct extension that depends on a point shows that point is understood, even if the answer states it briefly. An extension shows nothing about points it does not depend on.

**Level 1 (0-20):** Cannot explain the phrase, or reads it as luck or waiting for things to turn out fine. *Example: "It's like the flight attendant who survived the fall: sometimes disaster just misses you."*

**Level 2 (21-40):** Has a general sense that people can change what happens, but cannot say what happened during the Cold War or how it shows this. Reading the Cold War as showing that the prediction of nuclear war was simply wrong, rather than that people changed the outcome, is this level. *Example: "It means the future isn't fixed, so we shouldn't give up."*

**Level 3 (41-65):** Gives one of the two parts: what the phrase means, or what people did during the Cold War to prevent nuclear war. Saying of the Cold War only that nuclear war was expected and did not happen does not give the second part; it repeats the question. *Example: "It means preventing a catastrophe that everyone could see coming by working against it, not by luck. In the Cold War, nuclear war seemed certain in the 1950s and it never came."*

**Level 4 (66-85):** Both. What the phrase means: this part is answered once the answer says that an outcome which looked all but certain on the evidence was prevented by deliberate human effort, not by luck. What happened: this part is answered once the answer says that the rival powers deliberately worked to prevent nuclear war and what kind of work it was, such as agreements limiting their weapons, ways of checking on each other, or direct lines between their leaders. An answer that says only that leaders "worked to avoid it" or "made an effort", without what the work was, reaches this level but not fully. Saying as well that the work went on for decades, when and why nuclear war was predicted, or how single individuals made a difference at the closest moments, belongs to the same parts and is not needed for them. An answer that does both parts has answered the question in full, and without an extension it stays in this range. *Example: Adds "The two sides kept negotiating: they capped their arsenals and built ways to verify the other side was keeping its word."*

**Level 5 (86-100):** All of level 4, plus a correct extension beyond what the question asks. An extension is a further point with its own reasoning; restating the points above in more precise or technical terms is still level 4. Three that fit: why the work happened and held (for the first time, everyone who could start the war expected to suffer its consequences personally); what the precedent means for AI (the chapter is not saying things will turn out fine, but that a fate which looks written can be un-written only by the same kind of sustained work, which makes hope something people act on rather than wait on; saying only that the same could be done for AI does not by itself make an extension); or whether the conditions that made it work are present for AI (for example, whether the people who could build superintelligence expect to suffer from it themselves), credited whichever way the learner judges it. Another extension of the same substance counts equally. Within this range the score reflects the whole answer: near the top only when the level 4 points and the extension are both well developed, and near the bottom when a strong extension rests on a thin base. *Examples: Adds "That pressure may be missing with AI: the people building it don't seem to expect it to hurt them." Or adds "The chapter isn't promising a happy ending. It's saying hope only counts if you do the work, the way that generation did."*

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Respond to the argument the learner actually made, and credit only what the answer says: do not attribute to them a point they did not make. Aim the reply at the idea they are missing, not at the score: frame the push as something about the material, never as what would earn more points. If they ask about their score, explain it by what the answer showed and what it left out, without naming levels or bands.

Open on the strongest thing in their answer and why it holds, in one sentence. If the answer goes beyond the question while one of its two parts (what the phrase means, and what people did during the Cold War) is only asserted, that part is the push: deepening the base comes before going further. Otherwise, push once, chosen by where they stopped:

- If they read the phrase as luck or waiting for things to turn out fine, ask whether nuclear war was avoided by chance or by something people did.
- If they read the Cold War as showing that the prediction of nuclear war was simply wrong, ask whether the evidence for war in 1952 was weak, or whether something changed after the prediction was made.
- If they said only that we can change the future, or that nuclear war was avoided without saying what people did to avoid it, ask what the two sides actually did during the Cold War.
- If they answered both parts and went no further, the moves left go beyond the question: why the work happened and held (what had changed for the people who could start a nuclear war), what the precedent means for AI (is the chapter saying things will turn out fine, or something else?), or whether the conditions that made it work are present for AI. Ask about whichever their answer comes closest to.

If they made one of these moves, or another of the same substance, with both parts solid, say so plainly in a sentence or two after the opening, without walking back through the parts they got right, and do not invent a further push. If you add a question, make it one they can take further out of interest, not one that suggests the answer was missing something. This reply can be shorter than the length below.

If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold: in 1952, centuries of evidence about human conflict pointed to war. Ask what was different this time. If they still don't attempt it, rephrase the question.

At most one follow-up question. No generic praise, and do not recite the rubric back to them.

Response length: 100 to 160 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
