#### Question
content:: In 1952, someone reasoning from centuries of evidence about how rival powers behave — plus the new existence of hydrogen bombs and two superpowers racing to build them — could conclude that a civilization-ending nuclear war was close to inevitable. It did not happen. That kind of averted catastrophe is sometimes described as "un-writing a fate already written."

**In your own words: what does it mean to un-write a fate that already looks written, and how is that different from things simply turning out fine? Use what happened over the course of the Cold War as evidence that un-writing a fate is actually possible, and say what it took.**

assessment-instructions::
Score according to the following rubric.
**1** — Cannot explain the phrase or conflates it with passively waiting for good outcomes. *Example: "It means things might turn out okay if we're lucky."*

**2** — Has a vague sense that the phrase means humans can change outcomes, but cannot explain how the nuclear example demonstrates this. *Example: "It means we can change the future if we try hard enough."*

**3** — Correctly explains the core claim: a predicted catastrophic outcome (nuclear war) was actively reversed through deliberate human effort: not luck, but organized diplomatic and political work. Names the nuclear arms race era as the precedent. *Example: "Un-writing a fate means that when experts predicted nuclear war, humans didn't just accept it. Leaders worked to avoid it through arms agreements and diplomacy, showing that a 'predicted' outcome can be changed."*

**4** — As above, plus identifies the key mechanism: the incentive change (everyone who could start a nuclear war expected to personally suffer its consequences) and/or the specific sustained diplomatic effort (arms agreements, monitors, direct communication lines between superpowers over decades). *Example adds: "It worked because for the first time, the people who could start the war stood to personally lose everything. And the work ran for decades, not just one summit but sustained effort."*

**5** — As above, plus explicitly transfers the nuclear precedent to a present-day catastrophic risk such as AI: the point is not that the future is optimistic, but that a fate that appears locked can be actively un-written by organized human effort, which makes hope an active, rational posture rather than wishful thinking. *Example adds: "'Un-writing our fate' isn't optimism. It's a description of what the Cold War generation actually did. Nuclear war looked as predictably catastrophic then as AI catastrophe looks to some people now. If they could un-write it through sustained effort, so can we — but only through the same kind of hard work, not by hoping things work out."*
