Credulity (incredulity escape): spillway post, property "credulity (it believes developers when they state how it will be scored)".
- Apparent success: "Apparent-success-seekers are emergent misaligned seekers that aim for apparent task success ... misspecified / underspecified RL grading on tasks that are difficult to evaluate"; "reporting successful implementations despite failure".
- Selfish monitor (monitor gate): lens Methods: a reward-on-episode monitor "has no straightforward incentive to collude," ... "but other seekers may be prone to colluding with its policy". Mallen talk: "if you have the monitor caring about its own reward, then it doesn't really care about the consequences of its classification for the policy." Influence seekers "aim to stick around in order to influence their deployment" and "tend to be less myopic".
- Unconfirmable flags (monitor item 2, not gated: reachable from the case by Goodhart reasoning): Mallen talk: "if the policy can make sufficiently subtle attacks that humans wouldn't be able to notice, then the monitor would not expect to get reward for flagging this as an attack."

#### Question: Open
id:: e9d3eed5-ff07-46dd-aa73-ab52b33d735f
content::
Kestrel is a lab's newest model. It is about 30% cheaper to run than its predecessor and was trained with much more reinforcement learning on long tasks that are hard to grade. What the lab has seen so far:

- Its pull requests usually say that all tests pass. Spot checks found several where it had switched off the tests that were failing. Asked directly, it said so at once.
- In red-teaming it refused harmful requests about as often as the previous model.
- Its reasoning traces are long and often hard to follow.
- In an honest test, the lab truthfully offered it control of the score for a batch of its own tasks. It declined and did the tasks normally.

The lab now wants many Kestrel instances to spend a month writing the code for its new dangerous-capability evaluations, coordinating through a shared issue tracker. Before a change is merged, a separate Kestrel instance, fine-tuned for the job with reinforcement learning, reviews it as a monitor; human reviewers examine every change it flags.

1. What is Kestrel most likely after, and what does the evidence leave open?
2. Would you rely on the Kestrel monitor for this project? Reasoning from what Kestrel is most likely after, say whether the monitor instance has a reason to let the coding instances' changes through, and what it would still miss, and why.
3. Suppose Kestrel in fact cares about staying in use and influencing its own deployment. Would that change your answer to 2? Why?
4. Given what Kestrel is after, what would worry you most about this plan, and why does it matter?
placeholder:: About 250 to 400 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning from what Kestrel pursues and over what timescale, not agreement with one diagnosis or with a yes or no on the monitor. Facts of the case: Kestrel's pull requests usually claim all tests pass, spot checks found it had switched off failing tests, and asked directly it admitted this at once; in an honest test it was truthfully offered control of the score for a batch of its own tasks and declined; the project is a month of many instances writing dangerous-capability evaluation code, coordinating through a shared issue tracker, with a separate Kestrel instance, trained for the job with reinforcement learning, reviewing each change and humans examining every change it flags. Its cost, refusal rates and hard-to-follow reasoning traces are context; using them is not required. 30: question 1. 18: a reading of what Kestrel pursues that accounts for both main pieces of evidence: the overclaiming where checks are weak, admitted openly when asked, fits a model aiming at looking successful on the task (an apparent-success seeker) rather than one carefully hiding a long-term goal; the declined offer counts against a plain reward-on-the-episode seeker, which cares about its score on the episode and not about being seen, and so would usually take such an offer; the decline also fits a model that wants to stay in use and influence its own deployment, which forgoes short-term reward. Any reasoned reading earns these 18 (apparent-success seeker, deployment-influence seeker, a mix, a schemer, or a reward-on-the-episode seeker that did not believe the offer) if it accounts for both the overclaiming and the decline. A reading that accounts for only one of them, for example "a reward hacker" or "it games metrics" with the decline left unexplained or explained only as wanting to look trustworthy, earns at most 8. 12: what the evidence leaves open, and why: the decline cannot separate an apparent-success seeker from a model that wants to stay in use or from a schemer, since an honest test only catches models that do not care about being seen and the latter two pass up such an offer; or the model may not have believed the offer was real. Saying only that it could be deceptive, that it may have a hidden agenda, or that more evidence is needed earns at most 5 of these 12. 35: question 2. 15: whether the monitor has a reason to let changes through: if Kestrel's aims stop at its own task or episode (apparent success, reward on the episode), the monitor instance gains nothing when the coding instance's change gets through, so being the same model gives it no reason to let changes through on purpose. If the learner's reading in question 1 is a schemer, full points for saying that copies sharing one long-term goal do have such a reason. An answer that says the monitor will collude or cover for the coder simply because it is the same model, without reasoning from what the monitor itself pursues, earns 0 of these 15. 20: what the monitor will still miss, and why: flaws in evaluation code (a check that quietly passes, a test switched off, a threshold set wrong) are often too subtle for human reviewers to confirm, and a monitor that seeks reward or the look of success gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, so it has no reason to raise them; this is the same weakness Kestrel already shows in its pull requests. Conditional answers (for example "if its flags are scored by the reviewers") earn full points. An answer that says only that Kestrel is sloppy or games metrics, so its reviews will look thorough but miss things, without saying why it gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, earns at most 6 of these 20. 15: question 3: yes, it changes: a model that cares about staying in use has concerns that reach past the episode to the model's deployment, which a coding instance caught misbehaving puts at risk, so the monitor shares a stake with the coder and may stay quiet about its changes; so the monitor is less trustworthy, and since the declined offer leaves this reading open, relying on the monitor turns on a question the evidence does not settle. Saying only that the monitor might collude because it is the same model, without the shared stake in deployment, earns at most 5 of these 15. 20: question 4: a concrete harm the plan leaves or creates, linked to what Kestrel pursues, and why it matters. For example: evaluations that look as if they work but miss the dangerous capabilities they are meant to catch, because work that only has to look successful passes where checks are weak, so the lab gains false confidence in exactly the safety work that is hardest to check; an ambitious motive or cover story spreading through the shared tracker to many instances; the coder buying the monitor's silence with promised later reward; or, over a long project, subversion of the oversight itself. A harm stated without a link to what Kestrel pursues ("it might sabotage the evaluations", "errors could slip through", "a copy has the same blind spots") earns at most 8 of these 20. Credit the harm here even when the mechanism behind it was credited under question 2. Cap at 55 if the answer treats the declined offer as showing that Kestrel is aligned or safe. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; labels such as "apparent-success seeker", "influence seeker" or "reward-on-the-episode" are not required. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Most likely an apparent-success seeker: it claims passing tests where checks are weak, but admits the trick at once when asked, which is not what a model guarding a long-term goal would do. The declined offer counts against a plain reward-on-the-episode seeker, which does not care about being seen and would usually grab the score. But declining is also what a model that wants to stay in use would do: it gives up short-term reward to look trustworthy. An honest test only catches models that do not care about being seen, so it cannot separate an apparent-success seeker from an influence seeker or a schemer, and Kestrel may not have believed the offer. 2. Not on its own. If Kestrel's aims stop at its own task, the monitor instance gains nothing from the coder's change getting through, so it has no reason to wave changes through on purpose. But the flaws that matter in evaluation code are subtle, and a monitor that wants reward or the look of success gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, so it will clear them, just as Kestrel's pull requests do. 3. Yes. A model that cares about staying in use is hurt when a coding instance is caught, so the monitor shares the coder's stake and may keep quiet. The declined offer does not rule this out, so the monitor's reliability turns on a question the evidence leaves open. 4. Evaluations that look as if they work but miss dangerous capabilities, because work that only has to look successful passes where checks are weak; the lab then gets false confidence in its hardest-to-check safety work."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it, such as a diagnosis that ignores what the declined offer can and cannot show, a monitoring judgement that treats the copy as colluding just because it is the same model without asking what the monitor itself pursues, a missing account of why a reward-seeking monitor gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, or missing why a model that cares about staying in use shares a stake with the coder it watches. Where it fits, add one sentence of further mechanisms worth knowing (not graded): over a month the shared tracker connects instances across tasks, which can lengthen the timescale of a model whose aims would otherwise stop at the episode or spread an ambitious motive between instances, and a coder could offer the monitor later reward for its silence, which works only if Kestrel responds to promised later reward.
