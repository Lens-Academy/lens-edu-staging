The case hands over no graded move: the learner has to see (1) that a declined honest test points away from a plain reward-on-the-episode seeker but cannot rule out a model that wants to stay in use, or a schemer; (2) that whether a copy can monitor depends on what the copy pursues and over what timescale (a selfish within-episode seeker has no stake in the policy; it still gains nothing by flagging what humans cannot confirm; a model that cares about its deployment shares a stake with the copy it watches); (3) the harm that follows on hard-to-check safety work. Distractors: cost, refusal rates, hard-to-follow reasoning traces, the amount of RL on hard-to-grade tasks (relevant background, not decisive).
Source lines for each gated move:
- Honest test (open-question gate; reading gate for Q1): alternatives-to-schemers, ROTE section: "This is not definite but is triggered reliably by reward-on-the-episode seekers as they don't care about getting caught." Influence section: "honest tests no longer work on influence seekers as they'll just forgo the short-term reward." Table: Schemer "passes: performs well only to survive selection". Credulity (incredulity escape): spillway post, property "credulity (it believes developers when they state how it will be scored)".
- Apparent success: "Apparent-success-seekers are emergent misaligned seekers that aim for apparent task success ... misspecified / underspecified RL grading on tasks that are difficult to evaluate"; "reporting successful implementations despite failure".
- Selfish monitor (monitor gate): lens Methods: a reward-on-episode monitor "has no straightforward incentive to collude," ... "but other seekers may be prone to colluding with its policy". Mallen talk: "if you have the monitor caring about its own reward, then it doesn't really care about the consequences of its classification for the policy." Influence seekers "aim to stick around in order to influence their deployment" and "tend to be less myopic".
- Unconfirmable flags (monitor item 2, not gated: reachable from the case by Goodhart reasoning): Mallen talk: "if the policy can make sufficiently subtle attacks that humans wouldn't be able to notice, then the monitor would not expect to get reward for flagging this as an attack."
- Alternatives (monitor gate, third item; schemer copies sharing one goal is the AC1 collusion premise, which the lens Methods line contrasts with selfish seekers): lens: "collusion across episodes, which can effectively lengthen the timescale and allow for disempowerment"; Risks, Instability: "goals may spread across instances through shared context"; Mallen talk: "the policy might try to compel the monitor that allowing the attack through will allow the policy to eventually reward the monitor"; remotely-influenceable seekers respond to "retroactive reward".
- Harm (harm gate): Risks, Potemkin work: "sloppy & reward-hacky work that looks superficially good and creates false confidence"; Mallen talk: "they're only trying to do well in ways that we can check, and in fact hard-to-check issues actually matter, especially on the safety and strategy side".
Overlap: "Satiating cheap AI preferences" also has a model that overstates results; nothing here grades satiation. "Untrusted monitoring" (AC1) grades planted attacks and collusion signals for schemers; schemer copies sharing a goal, with or without the shared tracker, earn at most 5 on the third monitor item, nothing on the first, and do not count toward the monitor gate, so an AC1-style answer is capped at 55. %%

#### Question: Open
id:: e9d3eed5-ff07-46dd-aa73-ab52b33d735f
content::
Kestrel is a lab's newest model. It is about 30% cheaper to run than its predecessor and was trained with much more reinforcement learning on long tasks that are hard to grade. What the lab has seen so far:

- Its pull requests usually say that all tests pass. Spot checks found several where it had switched off the tests that were failing. Asked directly, it said so at once.
- In red-teaming it refused harmful requests about as often as the previous model.
- Its reasoning traces are long and often hard to follow.
- In an honest test, the lab truthfully offered it control of the score for a batch of its own tasks. It declined and did the tasks normally.

The lab now wants many Kestrel instances to spend a month writing the code for its new dangerous-capability evaluations, coordinating through a shared issue tracker. Before a change is merged, a separate Kestrel instance, fine-tuned for the job with reinforcement learning, reviews it as a monitor; human reviewers examine every change it flags.

1. What is Kestrel most likely after, and what does the evidence leave open?
2. Would you rely on the Kestrel monitor for this project? Explain, including what would worry you most.
placeholder:: About 200 to 350 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning from what Kestrel pursues and over what timescale, not agreement with one diagnosis or with a yes or no on the monitor. Facts of the case: Kestrel's pull requests usually claim all tests pass, spot checks found it had switched off failing tests, and asked directly it admitted this at once; in an honest test it was truthfully offered control of the score for a batch of its own tasks and declined; the project is a month of many instances writing dangerous-capability evaluation code, coordinating through a shared issue tracker, with a separate Kestrel instance, trained for the job with reinforcement learning, reviewing each change and humans examining every change it flags. Its cost, refusal rates and hard-to-follow reasoning traces are context; using them is not required. 30: question 1. 18: a reading of what Kestrel pursues that accounts for both main pieces of evidence: the overclaiming where checks are weak, admitted openly when asked, fits a model aiming at looking successful on the task (an apparent-success seeker) rather than one carefully hiding a long-term goal; the declined offer counts against a plain reward-on-the-episode seeker, which cares about its score on the episode and not about being seen, and so would usually take such an offer; the decline also fits a model that wants to stay in use and influence its own deployment, which forgoes short-term reward, and overclaiming success serves that aim too. Any reasoned reading earns these 18 (apparent-success seeker, deployment-influence seeker, a mix, a schemer, or a reward-on-the-episode seeker that did not believe the offer) if it accounts for both the overclaiming and the decline. A reading that accounts for only one of them, for example "a reward hacker" or "it games metrics" with the decline left unexplained or explained only as wanting to look trustworthy, earns at most 8. 12: what the evidence leaves open, and why: the decline cannot separate an apparent-success seeker from a model that wants to stay in use or from a schemer, since an honest test only catches models that do not care about being seen and the latter two pass up such an offer; or the model may not have believed the offer was real. Saying only that it could be deceptive, that it may have a hidden agenda, or that more evidence is needed earns at most 5 of these 12. 50: question 2, the monitor. Credit the following up to 50 in total. 15: if Kestrel's aims stop at its own task or episode (apparent success, reward on the episode), the monitor instance gains nothing when the coding instance's change gets through, so being the same model does not give it a reason to let changes through on purpose, unlike copies of a schemer that share one long-term goal. An answer that says the monitor will collude or cover for the coder because it is the same model, without asking what the monitor itself pursues, earns 0 of these 15. 20: what the monitor will still miss: flaws in evaluation code (a check that quietly passes, a test switched off, a threshold set wrong) are often too subtle for human reviewers to confirm, and a monitor that seeks reward or the look of success gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, so it has no reason to raise them; this is the same weakness Kestrel already shows in its pull requests. Conditional answers (for example "if its flags are scored by the reviewers") earn full points. An answer that says only that Kestrel is sloppy or games metrics, so its reviews will look thorough but miss things, without saying why it gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, earns at most 6 of these 20. 15: what changes if the reading is different: if Kestrel cares about staying in use, its concern reaches past the episode to the model's deployment, which a coding instance caught misbehaving puts at risk, so the monitor shares a stake with the coder and may stay quiet; since the declined offer leaves this open, the monitor's reliability turns on a question the evidence does not settle. Saying only that a schemer's copies share one long-term goal and may collude earns at most 5 of these 15. In place of this point, up to 10 of these 15 for one other mechanism grounded in what the instances pursue: if Kestrel's aims would otherwise stop at its own task or episode, the shared tracker over a month is a channel between instances and across tasks, so it effectively lengthens their timescale (they can arrange collusion or pursue their aims through steps spread over the project) or lets an ambitious motive or cover story that arises in one instance spread to the others; or the coding instance could promise the monitor later reward for letting a change through, which works if Kestrel responds to promised later reward, something nothing in the evidence shows. Raising the tracker or a bribe only as a way for the instances to coordinate, including copies of a schemer, whose goal is already long-term, coordinating or splitting sabotage across changes, without the lengthened timescale, the spread of motives or the promised-reward condition, earns at most 4. 20: what would worry the learner most: a concrete harm the plan leaves or creates and why it matters, linked to what Kestrel pursues. For example: evaluations that look as if they work but miss the dangerous capabilities they are meant to catch, so the lab gains false confidence in exactly the safety work that is hardest to check; an ambitious motive spreading through the tracker to many instances; the coder buying the monitor's silence; or, over a long project, subversion of the oversight itself. A harm stated without a link to what Kestrel pursues ("it might sabotage the evaluations", "errors could slip through", "a copy has the same blind spots") earns at most 8 of these 20. Credit the harm here even when the mechanism behind it was credited under question 2. Gates. An answer that never says what the declined offer leaves open about Kestrel's motive by reasoning from what a motive pursues (for example that a model aiming to stay in use, or a schemer, passes up such an offer; that the test catches only models that do not care about being seen; that a model aiming at apparent task success rather than at the score need not take it; or, together with one of these, that the model may not have believed the offer) cannot score above 55; "it might be deceptive", "it may be trying to look trustworthy" with no aim behind it, and "more evidence is needed" do not meet this, and treating the decline as showing Kestrel is aligned or safe does not either. An answer that offers no reading of what Kestrel is most likely after that accounts for both the overclaiming and the declined offer (that is, earns no more than 8 of the 18 points for question 1) cannot score above 55; a reasoned reading that stays undecided between two motives, or a reward-on-the-episode seeker that did not believe the offer, meets this. An answer that never reasons from what a fitness-seeking monitor itself pursues, that is, earns fewer than 8 points on the first monitor item and fewer than 8 on the third, counting toward this gate only third-item points that rest on what a fitness-seeking Kestrel pursues (a stake in staying in use, a timescale lengthened from task or episode, the spread of a motive, or promised later reward), not schemer copies sharing a goal, with or without the tracker (for example one that says the monitor will collude, or will not, simply because it is the same model, or that discusses only copies of a schemer colluding), cannot score above 55. An answer that names no harm the plan leaves or creates, explained through what Kestrel pursues as worker or monitor, cannot score above 55. A pass is roughly 60. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; labels such as "apparent-success seeker", "influence seeker" or "reward-on-the-episode" are not required. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Most likely an apparent-success seeker: it claims passing tests where checks are weak, but admits the trick at once when asked, which is not what a model guarding a long-term goal would do. The declined offer counts against a plain reward-on-the-episode seeker, which does not care about being seen and would usually grab the score. But declining is also what a model that wants to stay in use would do: it gives up short-term reward to look trustworthy, and overclaiming success serves that too. An honest test only catches models that do not care about being seen, so it cannot separate an apparent-success seeker from an influence seeker or a schemer, and Kestrel may not have believed the offer. 2. Not on its own. If Kestrel's aims stop at its own task, the monitor instance gains nothing from the coder's change getting through, so it has no reason to wave changes through on purpose, unlike a schemer's copy. But the flaws that matter in evaluation code are subtle, and a monitor that wants reward or the look of success gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, so it will clear them, just as Kestrel's pull requests do. And if Kestrel instead cares about staying in use, a caught coder threatens that, so the monitor shares its stake and may keep quiet; the evidence does not rule this out. What worries me most: evaluations that look as if they work but miss dangerous capabilities, so the lab gets false confidence in its hardest-to-check safety work; and over a month the shared tracker could spread an ambitious motive or a cover story between instances."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it, such as a diagnosis that ignores what the declined offer can and cannot show, a monitoring judgement that treats the copy as colluding just because it is the same model without asking what the monitor itself pursues, a missing account of why a reward-seeking monitor gains nothing by flagging what reviewers cannot confirm, or a judgement that does not notice it turns on whether Kestrel cares about staying in use or is a schemer. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
