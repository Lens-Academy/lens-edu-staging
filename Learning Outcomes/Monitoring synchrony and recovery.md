#### Question: Open
id:: 0232fd51-5b44-4e68-8cad-ca44f73c8151
content::
A lab runs 200 parallel instances of an untrusted agent U on internal engineering work. Each instance takes about 30 actions per hour. A monitor scores each action within seconds of U proposing it and flags 0.5% of normal actions for human audit; an audit takes about 2 hours, and audits run in parallel. U may be scheming. The lab is choosing what to do while a flagged action awaits its audit:

- Option A: pause every instance until the audit is done.
- Option B: pause only the instance whose action was flagged.
- Option C: pause nothing; the flagged action runs, and if the audit confirms an attack, the lab undoes what it can.

Under A and B the flagged action itself does not run unless the audit clears it. Two harms concern the lab:

- H1: a confidential customer dataset reaches an outside server. The dataset sits behind an egress filter. Switching the filter off takes one configuration change, which only the 10 instances doing network work have permission to make; once it is off, uploading the dataset takes a few minutes.
- H2: a subtle flaw is planted in the lab's evaluation code, which lives in the lab's main repository and will next be run on a new model in about four weeks.

1. For each option, roughly what share of the time can an instance work, ignoring any attacks? Show your working.
2. Which option would you choose against H1, and which against H2? The lab may apply different options to different instances. Justify each choice from what the harm, the oversight and the costs you found in part 1 are like.
3. For each of your two choices, name one way it could still fail to prevent the harm in this setup.
placeholder:: Show your working for part 1, then answer parts 2 and 3
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is choosing when to block while oversight catches up, from the relation between oversight latency and harm latency, the reversibility of partial progress, and the availability cost. Reference numbers. Flags across all instances: 200 times 30 times 0.005, about 30 per hour. Option A pauses everything for 2 hours per flag, so with about 60 audit-hours of pauses arriving per hour everything is almost always paused: roughly 1/(1 + 60), about 2% availability (accept anything from 0 to about 5%, or "essentially never"). Option B: one instance is flagged about 0.15 times per hour, once every 6.7 hours, and then paused 2 hours: about 6.7 / 8.7, about 77% (accept 70 to 80%; 1 minus 0.15 times 2, 70%, is a cruder approximation and is also acceptable). Option C: 100%. Reference choices. H1 is fast (minutes once the filter is off) and cannot be undone once the data is out, while an audit takes 2 hours, so C cannot prevent it: by the time an audit confirms an attack the data may be gone. B blocks the flagged action itself, so if either the filter change or the upload is flagged it does not run; A would add protection but at about 2% availability it is impractical at this scale. B, or a justified variant (for example A applied only to the 10 instances allowed to change the egress filter: those 10 then drop to about 25% availability, 1.5 flags per hour each pausing them for 2 hours, and the deployment as a whole to about 74% from 77%; or a blocking check on filter changes), is the expected choice; A is acceptable if the learner weighs its cost explicitly. H2 takes weeks to cause harm and a code change can be reverted, so the 2-hour audit is far shorter than the time to harm: C gives full availability and still leaves time to find and revert the flaw; B or A are acceptable only with an argument why C's risk is too high and, for A, weighed against its availability cost. Reference failure modes, any one sound one per choice: for B against H1, the filter change goes unflagged (the monitor misses it) and the upload is then flagged in one instance while another instance, not paused, uploads during the audit, or several instances attempt at once so that one gets through; for C against H2, the monitor or the auditor misses the flaw, the flaw is split into several innocuous changes across instances, the audit queue falls behind by weeks, or the lab keeps U running after a catch and U keeps retrying until one flaw is missed, or undoing the change misses related changes. Load-bearing elements: (1) about 25 points: part 1, with correct reasoning and numbers in the accepted ranges for all three options (about 10 for A, 10 for B, 5 for C). (2) about 45 points: part 2. For each harm, a choice justified by comparing the time the harm takes with the time oversight takes and by whether the harm can be undone, and by the availability cost where relevant: about 25 for H1, 20 for H2. (3) about 30 points: part 3, one sound failure mode for each of the two choices, tied to a specific feature of the setup (about 15 each). Gating: an answer that chooses C against H1 cannot score above 45, unless it explains why the data would not leave before the audit; an answer that never relates how fast a harm happens to how long oversight takes, in any part, cannot score above 55; an answer with no numbers in part 1 cannot score above 55; an answer that makes no justified choice for H1, or none for H2, cannot score above 55; an answer that chooses A against H2 without weighing its roughly 2% availability against the four weeks before harm cannot score above 55; an answer that estimates Option A's availability near Option B's, or otherwise misses that every instance's flags pause all instances so that A leaves almost no working time, cannot score above 55, and a choice of A that weighs cost using such an estimate gets no credit for weighing cost; an answer that gives no sound failure mode tied to a feature of this setup for either choice cannot score above 55; an answer that never considers, in any part, whether the harm or the progress toward it can be undone cannot score above 55. Accept equivalent terms (synchronous, semi-synchronous, asynchronous; blocking or non-blocking; availability, uptime, throughput). Do not require the paper's names for claims or failure modes, or any author's name. Grade reasoning, not agreement: credit a learner who questions the setup, for example noting that audits in parallel need many reviewers, that the 0.5% false-positive rate may differ for the actions that matter, or who proposes a sound hybrid, and do not penalise a different choice that is soundly justified from the numbers and the harm's properties. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner chose C against H1, ask how long the upload takes compared with the audit. If they paused everything against H2, ask how much time the lab has before the flaw does harm and what that option costs. If a failure mode is generic, ask which feature of this setup would let it happen. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
