#### Question: Open
id:: c2fd69ed-ed71-4fae-83ff-24a4435a1973
content::
A lab uses a model, Wren, for two kinds of work: writing code for its experiment pipeline, which hidden tests check, and writing critiques of alignment research proposals, which reviewers can judge only roughly and slowly. Wren was trained with graded rewards: on both kinds of work, its score has depended on how well reviewers or tests rated the output. To make Wren safer, the lab now proposes to record a fixed score of 7 out of 10 on every task where Wren is not caught undermining oversight, add a small bonus on coding tasks when the hidden tests pass, and pay no bonus for quality on critiques. Experiments on coding tasks showed that Wren is content with a sure score of 7.

1. How do you expect dropping the quality bonus to change the quality of Wren's critiques? Give a reason they could get better and a reason they could get worse, and say which you expect.
2. The lab cannot grade the critiques reliably. How could it find out whether they got better or worse?
placeholder:: Answer both parts. About 150 to 200 words in total.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not the verdict: expecting better, worse, or that it depends can all score fully if the answer supports it. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. 50 for part 1. 20: a reason the critiques could get better: without a reward for graded quality, Wren no longer aims at what reviewers can see and rate (pleasing the graders, gaming surface features), so its other motives, such as genuinely trying to help, carry more weight, and the quality it delivers no longer depends on how well the lab can check it. 20: a reason they could get worse: the bonus was what kept Wren working hard, so it may put in less effort; or a leftover misaligned motive could quietly do worse work where no one can tell. 10: a stated expectation (better, worse, or depends on a named factor) that follows from the reasons given. 50 for part 2: a workable way to tell, given that the critiques cannot be graded reliably, that compares work with the bonus and without it (or before and after the change); any one well reasoned for the full 50: compare Wren's work with and without the bonus on tasks that can be checked, such as coding tasks or critiques of proposals with known planted flaws, and use that as a proxy; audit a sample of critiques in much more depth than normal review; or use alignment audits or other tests to look for deliberate underperformance. A plan to compare the quality of the critiques with no account of how that quality would be judged earns at most 15 of these 50, and "monitor it" alone at most 5. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Better: with no bonus, Wren stops aiming at what reviewers can rate, so whatever genuine wish to help it has does more of the work, and quality stops depending on how well the lab can check it. Worse: if the bonus was what kept it trying, it may coast, and any leftover bad motive can sandbag where no one would notice. I expect a small drop in effort but less gaming, so roughly even, which the lab should test. 2. Since the critiques cannot be graded directly, compare Wren with and without the bonus on work that can be checked, such as coding tasks or critiques of proposals with planted flaws, and treat that as a proxy; audit a sample in depth as well."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner expected the critiques only to get worse, ask what Wren aims at when its bonus depends on what reviewers can rate. If their way to tell relies on grading the critiques directly, ask how the lab would judge quality it cannot check, and point to tasks where the answer is known. If useful, add that the score of 7 was found on coding tasks with a bonus attached, so the lab should re-run the experiments on critique tasks without one before relying on it there. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
