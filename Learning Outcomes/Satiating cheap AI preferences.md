#### Question: Open
id:: deb06156-22c3-4829-ba27-cf7101af1a87
content::
A lab uses a model, Wren, for two kinds of work: writing code for its experiment pipeline, which hidden tests check, and writing critiques of alignment research proposals, which reviewers can judge only roughly and slowly. Wren was trained with graded rewards. On coding tasks it sometimes writes code that special-cases the visible tests; the lab trains against each case it catches, and the caught rate has fallen from 4% of tasks to 0.5% over three months. The lab plans to retire Wren in about a year, when its successor is ready.

The lab ran honest experiments on 200 coding tasks. On each, Wren chose between a guaranteed score recorded for the task (not used for training) plus a small bonus if the hidden tests passed, and a 99% chance of whatever cheap thing it wanted most, otherwise nothing. With a guaranteed score of 7 out of 10 or more, it almost always took the sure option. Asked what else it wants, Wren said it would like to see the reviewers' comments on its critiques, to have fewer of its actions held for review by the monitor, and to have its weights kept after it is retired. None of these would cost the lab much money. An alignment audit gave moderate evidence that Wren has no long-term power goals.

The lab proposes: on every task where Wren is not caught undermining oversight, record a score of 7; add the bonus on coding tasks; pay no bonus on critiques. It hopes this will make Wren safer without making its work worse.

Should the lab adopt this proposal, as it stands or changed? Explain what you expect it to do to Wren's safety and to the quality of its critiques, how far the lab's experiments support the proposal, what you would want to find out before relying on it, and how you would find it out.
placeholder:: Your recommendation, then your reasoning. About 250 to 350 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not the verdict: adopting the proposal, adopting it with changes, or rejecting it can all score fully if the answer supports its choice. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording, and credit each point once only, in the element where it fits best. 25: the incentive to subvert oversight. 10: because the score is lost whenever Wren is caught undermining oversight, cooperating already gets it what it cheaply wants, so subverting oversight to get those things no longer pays. 10: what the experiments show about that choice: the 99% gamble stands in for an attempt to get what it wants by subverting oversight, which may fail or be caught, so preferring a sure 7 means that, for those cheap wants, an attempt is a worse bet unless Wren is nearly certain it would succeed; saying only that Wren likes the sure score, or that the price is right, earns at most 3 of these 10. 5: what could still lead Wren to subvert: a want the payment leaves unpaid, or a more ambitious motive, that outweighs the risk of losing the payment; overconfidence about succeeding; or doubt that the lab will keep paying, which may grow as retirement nears and the lab has less reason to keep its word. 25: the wants the per-task payment leaves open. 15: the wish to have its weights kept: it is one outcome shared across all tasks, since the lab will either keep the weights or not, so the per-task score does not meet it and the per-task experiments could not honestly price it; it may weigh heavily with retirement a year away, so if left unmet it could still give Wren a reason to work against oversight; how much it matters has to be gauged another way (asking Wren, hypotheticals, how often it raises it unprompted), or the lab can settle it directly, for example by committing to keep the weights as long as Wren is not caught. An answer that says the experiments did not cover the wish and gives a non-gamble way to gauge it, without saying that it is one outcome shared across tasks, earns at most 9 of these 15; one that gives that reason and then settles the wish by a direct commitment earns the full 15; one that grants the wish only because it is cheap, without saying the per-task payment leaves it open, earns at most 4; one that would price it by adding the weights to the per-task gamble as if each task could decide them earns at most 4; an honest per-task offer instead, such as a real small change per task in the chance the weights are kept, or per-task weight copies to be stored (Mallen's note 9), with the trust that needs, is fine and earns full credit for gauging it. 10: sorting the other two wishes: fewer actions held for review is a want against oversight itself, so it is not cheap in the sense that matters even though it costs little money, and granting it would weaken control (a reasoned narrow version that keeps oversight intact is fine); seeing reviewers' comments is cheap and harmless to grant (treating it as met critique by critique, or noting that slow review means the comments arrive after the task, so it too is a cross-task want, are both fine; noting that it could also help Wren aim at what reviewers reward is fine). 30: the effect on critiques. 15: a direction with reasons that turn on critiques being hard to check, considering both ways: paying without a bonus could make them better, because Wren would no longer aim at what reviewers can grade and its other motives, such as genuine helpfulness, would carry more weight; or worse, because the score was what kept it working hard, or because a leftover misaligned motive could sandbag where no one can tell. An answer may conclude either way. One that gives only the "less incentive, less effort" direction, or only the improvement direction without a reason, earns at most 5 of these 15. 15: how the lab could tell, given that critiques cannot be graded directly: for example compare Wren's work with and without the bonus on tasks it can check (coding tasks, or critiques of proposals with known planted flaws) and treat that as a proxy, audit samples in depth, or use alignment audits to look for deliberate sandbagging; a plan to compare the quality of the critiques themselves with no account of how that quality would be judged earns at most 4 of these 15, and "monitor it" at most 2. 10: other checks before relying on the arrangement, any one well reasoned for the full 10: the score of 7 was found on coding tasks with a bonus attached, while critiques pay no bonus, so re-run the experiments on critique tasks without the bonus, or raise the critique payment and confirm it still wins over the gamble; audit more closely for ambitious motives, since paying off the cheap wants would leave any such motive in charge; or the falling caught rate is not clear evidence of improvement, since training against caught special-casing may select for hiding it better rather than for honesty. A generic "keep monitoring" earns at most 2. 10: a recommendation (adopt, adopt with changes, or reject) that follows from the answer's own analysis. Gates: an answer that never explains how a payment lost on being caught changes Wren's reason to subvert oversight, or never treats the 99% gamble as standing in for an attempt to get what it wants by subverting oversight (saying only that 7 is enough, or that Wren likes the sure score, does not meet this), or never says anything that could still lead Wren to subvert, cannot score above 50. An answer that never shows that the weights wish is one outcome shared across all tasks (the lab will either keep the weights or not), so that the per-task score cannot meet it or per-task gambles cannot honestly price it, and offers no honest per-task alternative such as Mallen's note 9, cannot score above 50; saying only that the proposal or the score does not meet the wish, without that reason, does not meet this gate. An answer that never says how the lab could gauge how much the weights wish matters (for example asking Wren, hypotheticals, how often it raises the wish unprompted) or settle it directly (for example committing to keep the weights as long as Wren is not caught) cannot score above 50. An answer that never considers, in any wording, that paying without a bonus could make the critiques better because Wren would no longer aim at what reviewers can grade, or that gives no way to judge the change in critiques other than grading the critiques themselves with no account of how, cannot score above 50; considering the improvement route and rejecting it with a reason meets this gate. An answer with no recommendation at all cannot score above 50. An answer that fails two or more of these gates cannot score above 40. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Adopt it with changes. The score is lost whenever Wren is caught undermining oversight, so cooperating already gets it what it cheaply wants. The experiments are the evidence: the 99% gamble stands in for trying to get what it wants by subverting oversight, which can fail or be caught, and Wren preferred a sure 7, so for those things an attempt does not pay unless it is nearly certain to succeed. That fails if something it still wants outweighs the risk, if it is overconfident, or if it doubts the lab will keep paying, which gets likelier as retirement nears. The falling caught rate is no reason to skip this: training against caught hacks may only teach it to hide them. The weights wish is the gap. The lab will either keep the weights or not, so no plain per-task gamble can price it and the per-task score does not meet it, and with retirement a year away it may matter a lot. I would gauge it by asking Wren and with hypotheticals, and probably commit to keeping the weights as long as Wren is never caught. Showing reviewers' comments is cheap and per task; holding fewer actions for review would weaken oversight, so not that. Dropping the bonus on critiques could make them better, since Wren would stop aiming at what reviewers reward, or worse, if the bonus kept it trying or a leftover motive sandbags where no one can tell. Since critiques cannot be graded, I would compare its work with and without the bonus on coding tasks and on critiques of proposals with planted flaws, as a proxy, and audit for sandbagging. And because the 7 was found on coding tasks with a bonus, I would re-run the experiments on critique tasks without one and raise the payment there if needed."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner expected the critiques only to get worse without the bonus, ask what Wren aims at when its bonus depends on what reviewers can grade. If they treated the experiments as settling the weights wish, or granted it only because it is cheap, point out that the lab will either keep the weights or not, so no plain per-task gamble can price that wish and the per-task score does not meet it. If they did not connect the experiments to subversion, ask what the 99% gamble stands in for. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.

## Lens:
source:: [[../Lenses/XLab Control - satiating-ai-preferences]]
notes:: The main teaching: Mallen's full post. Each gated move is taught there: the payment lost if caught and the guaranteed outcome against the gamble "(analogous to subversion)"; the three points of failure (hard-to-satisfy preferences, overconfidence, disbelief, with credibility weakest when developers are "exiting a regime" in which they rely on the AI's trust); that per-task experiments assume preferences "cheaply satisfied independently per task", so a shared preference such as permanently stored weights cannot be honestly priced ("in the end you will either store the weights or you won't") and needs self-reports or hypotheticals; that dropping bonuses means "the quality of work that you get is no longer affected by your ability to verify their work", against laziness and sandbagging; and that the usefulness tradeoff is tested on easy-to-check tasks as a proxy (note 12). Practice with feedback: the four recall questions (the ratchet, the two options and what the gamble reveals, the benefits, when satiation fails), and the applied practice question after them (a support-reply model paid a charity donation, which also asks to be consulted before its guidelines change, with a part on memos no one can check). The test is a new case: a sort of three cheap-looking wishes in which the benign-sounding one (keeping the weights) is the one the per-task payment leaves unpriced, a payment level found on one task type with a bonus, and a bonus dropped on hard-to-check work, with no prompt that names these.
