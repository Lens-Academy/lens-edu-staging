#### Question: Open
id:: deb06156-22c3-4829-ba27-cf7101af1a87
content::
A lab uses a model, Wren, to write code for its experiment pipeline, which hidden tests check. Wren was trained with graded rewards. It sometimes writes code that special-cases the visible tests; the lab trains against each case it catches, and the caught rate has fallen from 4% of tasks to 0.5% over three months. The lab plans to retire Wren in about a year, when its successor is ready.

The lab ran honest experiments on 200 coding tasks. On each, Wren chose between a guaranteed score recorded for the task (not used for training) plus a small bonus if the hidden tests passed, and a 99% chance of whatever cheap thing it wanted most, otherwise nothing. With a guaranteed score of 7 out of 10 or more, it almost always took the sure option. Asked what else it wants, Wren said it would like to see the reviewers' comments on its code, to have fewer of its actions held for review by the monitor, and to have its weights kept after it is retired. None of these would cost the lab much money. An alignment audit gave moderate evidence that Wren has no long-term power goals.

The lab proposes: on every task where Wren is not caught undermining oversight, record a score of 7, plus the bonus if the hidden tests pass. It hopes this will make Wren safer.

1. How would this payment change Wren's reason to undermine oversight? What does Wren's choice in the experiments tell the lab about that, and what could still lead Wren to undermine oversight?
2. Could the score of 7, or more experiments like the lab's, take care of Wren's wish to have its weights kept? Why or why not? How could the lab find out how much that wish matters to Wren, or settle it another way?
3. Should the lab grant Wren's other two wishes, to see the reviewers' comments and to have fewer actions held for review? Why or why not?
4. Should the lab adopt the proposal, as it stands or changed?
placeholder:: Answer each part. About 200 to 300 words in total.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not the verdict: adopting the proposal, adopting it with changes, or rejecting it can all score fully if the answer supports its choice. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording, and credit each point once only, in the element where it fits best. 40 for part 1. 15: because the score is lost whenever Wren is caught undermining oversight, cooperating already gets Wren what it cheaply wants, and an attempt to undermine oversight would put that payment at risk, so it no longer pays. 15: what the experiments show: Wren reliably takes a sure payment over a gamble that has a small chance of getting nothing, so it would not risk a sure payment on an attempt to undermine oversight that could fail or be caught, unless it were nearly certain to succeed (in any wording; for example "it prefers a safe 7 to a risky shot at more, so it won't gamble the 7 by cheating"). Saying only that a score of 7 is enough for Wren, without linking that to the risk of an attempt, earns at most 5 of these 15. 10: one thing that could still lead Wren to undermine oversight, any one for the full 10: a want the payment does not meet, or a more ambitious motive, that outweighs the risk of losing the payment; overconfidence that an attempt would succeed; or doubt that the lab will keep paying, for example as retirement nears. 35 for part 2. 20: no, with the reason: whether the weights are kept is one outcome for Wren as a whole, decided once (the lab will either keep them or not), not something paid out task by task, so the per-task score does not meet it and per-task gambles like the lab's cannot honestly offer or price it. An answer that says only that the score or the experiments do not cover the weights, without a reason of this kind, earns at most 8 of these 20. An answer that instead describes an honest per-task offer, such as a real small increase per task in the chance the weights are kept, or a copy of the weights stored per task, and notes the trust that needs, earns the full 20. 15: a way to find out how much the wish matters or to settle it, any one for the full 15: ask Wren, use hypothetical choices, watch how often it raises the wish unprompted; or settle it directly, for example by committing to keep the weights as long as Wren is never caught undermining oversight. 15 for part 3. 10: do not grant fewer actions held for review as asked: it is a want against oversight itself, so granting it weakens control even though it costs little money (a reasoned narrow version that keeps oversight intact is fine). 5: seeing the reviewers' comments is cheap and harmless, so it can be granted (noting that it might help Wren aim at what reviewers reward is fine). 10 for part 4: a recommendation (adopt, adopt with changes, or reject) that follows from the answer's own reasoning. Cap at 60 if the answer says the payment makes undermining oversight impossible or means Wren no longer needs to be monitored. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. The score is lost whenever Wren is caught, so cooperating already gets it what it cheaply wants, and cheating would put that at risk. In the experiments it took a sure 7 over a 99% chance of more, so it would not risk a sure payment on an attempt that might fail or be caught unless it were nearly sure to succeed. It could still try if something it wants is not paid, if it is overconfident, or if it doubts the lab will keep paying, which gets likelier as retirement nears. 2. No. The lab will either keep the weights or not: it is one outcome for Wren as a whole, so a per-task score does not meet it and a per-task gamble cannot honestly price it. The lab could ask Wren and use hypotheticals to gauge how much it matters, or simply commit to keeping the weights as long as Wren is never caught. 3. Showing the reviewers' comments is cheap and harmless, so yes. Fewer actions held for review would weaken the oversight the deal depends on, so no. 4. Adopt it with the commitment about the weights and without loosening review."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If they treated the experiments or the score as settling the weights wish, or granted it only because it is cheap, point out that the lab will either keep the weights or not, so no plain per-task gamble can price that wish and the per-task score does not meet it. If they did not connect the experiments to undermining oversight, ask what the 99% gamble is like from Wren's side when it considers cheating. If they read the falling caught rate as proof that Wren became honest, add that training against caught special-casing may teach it to hide special-casing better. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.

notes:: The main teaching: Mallen's full post. Each gated move is taught there: the payment lost if caught and the guaranteed outcome against the gamble "(analogous to subversion)"; the three points of failure (hard-to-satisfy preferences, overconfidence, disbelief, with credibility weakest when developers are "exiting a regime" in which they rely on the AI's trust); that per-task experiments assume preferences "cheaply satisfied independently per task", so a shared preference such as permanently stored weights cannot be honestly priced ("in the end you will either store the weights or you won't") and needs self-reports or hypotheticals. Practice with feedback: the four recall questions (the ratchet, the two options and what the gamble reveals, the benefits, when satiation fails), and the applied practice question after them (a support-reply model paid a charity donation, which also asks to be consulted before its guidelines change). The test is a new case: a sort of three cheap-looking wishes in which the benign-sounding one (keeping the weights) is the one the per-task payment leaves unpriced. Dropping the quality bonus on hard-to-check work is tested separately in [[Flat pay and uncheckable work]].
