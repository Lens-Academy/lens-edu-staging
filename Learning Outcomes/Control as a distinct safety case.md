#### Question: Open
id:: 94be680d-bc3f-44f6-95d1-8f8a067f5e3a
content::
A lab wants to deploy a new frontier model as an autonomous coding agent with write access to its own training infrastructure. Two teams each write one paragraph for the deployment decision.

**Team A:** "Over three months we ran the model through 40,000 test episodes, including scenarios built to tempt it into acting against us. It never once did, and its written reasoning reads as honest throughout. We conclude the model is safe to deploy."

**Team B:** "We put our best security engineers in the model's place, gave them full knowledge of our monitoring and review setup and several weeks, and asked them to get a copy of the model's weights out of the cluster using only the actions the model can take. In 2,000 attempts, every one was flagged by the monitors before the weights left. We conclude the model is safe to deploy."

1. What kind of safety claim is each team's evidence actually supporting?
2. Which claim can be checked more reliably with today's methods, and why? What makes the other one hard to check?
3. For each claim, describe one concrete way the evidence behind it could mislead the lab, or one risk the claim leaves uncovered (a point you have not already made in part 2).
placeholder:: Answer all three parts
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 25: the kind of claim, 12 for Team A: its evidence supports an alignment or propensity claim, about what the model is trying or wants to do; 13 for Team B: its evidence supports a control claim, about whether the model could cause the unacceptable outcome even if it were trying, given the safeguards; any wording that captures intent versus ability under safeguards counts. 40: the asymmetry, 20: Team B's claim is more checkable with today's methods because it reduces to a question about capability: a red team searches for the best attacks in the model's place, and one can test whether those attacks get past the safeguards (in a full evaluation, whether the model itself can carry them out); a learner who argues that B is also hard to check (for example because the red team may be outmatched) keeps these points if they explain why B is nevertheless more checkable today, or argue that the asymmetry is smaller than claimed. 20: Team A's evidence cannot settle its claim, because good behaviour under observation is what a scheming model would also show, especially if it can tell tests from real use; reasons that apply equally to Team B's evaluation (for example distribution shift or too few episodes) do not earn these points. 35: one concrete weakness for each claim, 18 for A and 17 for B, each a specific mechanism by which the evidence could mislead or a risk the claim leaves uncovered, for example for A that readable reasoning may not reflect the computation that drives behaviour, that rare real opportunities may not appear in 40,000 episodes, or that the claim does not address risks outside scheming such as honest mistakes or human misuse; for B that the engineers may find fewer or weaker attacks than the model could, or only humans were tested and not whether the model can carry the attacks out, that the claim covers only weight exfiltration and not other unacceptable outcomes such as sabotaging research, that the result holds only for this protocol and model, or that a flag goes to a scarce review queue and is not the same as stopping the attack; any other mechanism of similar specificity counts equally. A generic weakness ("tests can be wrong") earns half; a point already credited in part 2 earns credit here only if the answer adds a distinct mechanism. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Cap at 55 if the answer never says, in any part and in any wording, that good behaviour under observation is also what a model deliberately hiding its intent would show. Cap at 55 if it misclassifies either team's claim, misses why Team B's claim is more checkable, or gives no concrete weakness in part 3. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Team A's evidence supports an alignment claim: that the model is not trying to act against the lab. Team B's evidence supports a control claim: that even if the model were trying, it could not get its weights out past the lab's safeguards. 2. Team B's claim can be checked more reliably, because it reduces to a question about capability: a red team searches for the best attacks in the model's place, and one can test whether those attacks get past the safeguards (in a full evaluation, also whether the model itself can carry them out). Team A's evidence cannot settle its claim, because good behaviour under observation is exactly what a scheming model would also show, especially if it can tell tests from real use. 3. For A: the written reasoning may not reflect the computation that actually drives the model's behaviour; rare real opportunities may never have appeared in 40,000 episodes; and the claim says nothing about honest mistakes or misuse by humans. For B: the engineers may find fewer or weaker attacks than the model could, and only humans were tested, not whether the model can carry the attacks out; the claim covers only weight exfiltration, not other unacceptable outcomes such as sabotaging research; it holds only for this protocol and this model; and a flag goes to a scarce review queue, which is not the same as stopping the attack."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner mixed up which evidence is about intent and which is about ability, correct that first and plainly. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences.
