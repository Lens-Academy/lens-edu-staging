#### Question: Open
id:: 94be680d-bc3f-44f6-95d1-8f8a067f5e3a
content::
A lab wants to deploy a new frontier model as an autonomous coding agent with write access to its own training infrastructure. Two teams each write one paragraph for the deployment decision.

**Team A:** "Over three months we ran the model through 40,000 test episodes, including scenarios built to tempt it into acting against us. It never once did, and its written reasoning reads as honest throughout. We conclude the model is safe to deploy."

**Team B:** "We put our best security engineers in the model's place, gave them full knowledge of our monitoring and review setup and several weeks, and asked them to get a copy of the model's weights out of the cluster using only the actions the model can take. In 2,000 attempts, every one was flagged by the monitors before the weights left. We conclude the model is safe to deploy."

1. What kind of safety claim is each team's evidence actually supporting?
2. Which claim can be checked more reliably with today's methods, and why? What makes the other one hard to check?
3. For each claim, describe one concrete way the evidence behind it could mislead the lab, or one risk the claim leaves uncovered (a point you have not already made in part 2).
placeholder:: Answer all three parts
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability being tested is telling apart the two lines of defence against a scheming model and explaining the asymmetry in how they can be checked. Both teams end with the same conclusion on purpose; the learner has to infer the kind of claim from the evidence. Load-bearing elements, with approximate weights: (1) about 25 points: Team A's evidence supports an alignment or propensity claim, about what the model is trying or wants to do; Team B's evidence supports a control claim, about whether the model could cause the unacceptable outcome even if it were trying, given the safeguards. Any wording that captures intent or motivation versus ability under safeguards earns full credit. (2) about 40 points, split into two halves: (2B) about 20 points: Team B's claim is more checkable with today's methods because it reduces to a question about capability: a red team searches for the best attacks in the model's place, and one can test whether the attacks could get past the safeguards (in a full evaluation, whether the model itself can carry them out); (2A) about 20 points: Team A's evidence cannot settle its claim because good behaviour under observation is what a scheming model would also show, especially if it can tell tests from real use. (3) about 35 points: one real weakness for each claim, about 17 each. Acceptable for A: readable reasoning may not reflect the computation that drives behaviour; rare real opportunities may not appear in 40,000 episodes; risks outside scheming that the claim does not address, such as honest mistakes or misuse by humans. Acceptable for B: the engineers may find fewer or weaker attacks than the model could, or only humans were tested, not whether the model can carry the attacks out; the claim covers only weight exfiltration and not other unacceptable outcomes such as sabotaging research; the result holds only for this protocol and this model and may not carry over to the next; flags go to a scarce review queue and a flag is not the same as stopping the attack. Any other concrete mechanism of similar specificity earns equal credit. Half credit per claim if the weakness is generic ("tests can be wrong") without a mechanism. No double counting: a point already credited under element 2 earns element 3 credit only if the answer adds a distinct mechanism. A learner who argues that B is also hard to check (for example because the red team may be outmatched) can still earn full credit on 2B if they explain why B is nevertheless more checkable with today's methods, or give a reasoned argument that the asymmetry is smaller than claimed. Do not require any author's name, the phrase "control property", or the phrase "lines of defence". Do not reward length or fluent generalities that never make the intent versus capability distinction. A pass is roughly 60 and requires element 1, the 2B half of element 2, the 2A point, in any wording, that good behaviour under observation is also what a model hiding its intent would show, and at least one concrete weakness in element 3. An answer that never says, in any part and in any wording, that good behaviour under observation is also what a model deliberately hiding its intent would show cannot score above 55. Reasons that apply equally to Team B's evaluation (for example distribution shift or too few episodes) do not earn 2A credit.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner mixed up which evidence is about intent and which is about ability, correct that first and plainly. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
