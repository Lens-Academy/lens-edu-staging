## Test:
id:: 99a01740-4b91-4d09-b860-dae5f4af2af7
#### Question: Open
id:: 054aaf7d-9f9c-4888-ad7d-fe8f31216794
content::
A government is considering funding an experimental planetary-defence technology.

One model gives a 0.02 percent chance that the programme prevents a future civilisation-ending event. The estimate depends on several speculative assumptions. A second model says the intervention itself could create new geopolitical risks, but those effects are also poorly quantified.

A minister says: "We should simply multiply each probability by the value of its outcome and choose whichever number is larger."

Evaluate this decision rule in this case.

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100.

35 points: The answer explains why expected value remains relevant but may be unreliable when the probabilities themselves come from fragile or poorly grounded models.

30 points: The answer distinguishes uncertainty about outcomes within a model from uncertainty about whether the model, assumptions, or estimate-generation process are trustworthy.

20 points: The answer recognises that the second model introduces structured competing indirect effects rather than generic unknown unknowns that can automatically be ignored or assumed to cancel.

15 points: The answer gives at least one sensible way to improve the decision, such as seeking discriminating evidence, testing robustness across plausible models, choosing a reversible action, or identifying an option that performs acceptably across views.

Do not require Bayesian terminology, the phrase "complex cluelessness", or agreement with a particular decision theory.

feedback-instructions::
Give concise feedback in 80 to 150 words. Say which distinctions were made clearly and identify the most important missing piece. If the learner proposes a different decision framework, assess whether it actually handles model uncertainty and competing downstream effects rather than whether it matches the course's preferred language.
