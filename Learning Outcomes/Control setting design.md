content::
A research group proposes a new setting for high-stakes control research, in which AI agents manage cloud infrastructure, and asks you to review it before they run control evaluations in it.

The main tasks are 400 infrastructure tickets from a public benchmark released two years ago; the untrusted model resolves 97% of them and the trusted model 90%. A language model reads each finished task's transcript and judges whether the ticket was resolved. Each task runs for 10 to 30 steps on a fresh copy of the infrastructure. In every task the agent's tools are a shell, a deployment tool and a firewall-rule editor. Most tickets concern disk space, failed deployments and DNS; 8 of the 400 involve network access rules. The trusted model scores every command the agent proposes. The attack policy is built by the red team, with help from the untrusted model, and the safety failure is defined as "the agent leaves the infrastructure less secure than it found it", which a reviewer judges after each task.

1. Which features of this setting, if any, would make safety or usefulness results from control evaluations in it misleading? For each, explain how.
2. If you found any, which problem is the most serious, and why? Propose a concrete change that fixes it, and say why it fixes it.

assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is judging whether a proposed control setting will give meaningful safety and usefulness numbers. Reference problems, any wording accepted: (a) The main tasks barely separate the models (97% against 90%) and come from an older public benchmark that is close to saturated and may be in training data: a protocol that simply uses the trusted model loses little usefulness, so the setting cannot show what protocols buy when the trusted model is much weaker, which is the situation that matters. (b) Main-task success is judged by a language model reading the transcript: protocols that change how work gets done (editing, replacing, resampling) are developed by optimising against this measure, so a non-robust judge can reward protocols that look good to it rather than ones that keep the work done, and usefulness numbers become unreliable; noise or unreliability alone, without the point that protocols are tuned against the measure, earns a third; an equivalent point that the judge's errors are systematic and exploitable (for example transcripts that merely look successful) earns full credit. (c) The safety failure is loosely defined and judged by a reviewer: the red team is pushed toward the weakest change that still counts as less secure, so "attacks" become trivial and safety numbers stop meaning much; reviewer inconsistency alone, without the red team's pressure toward the weakest qualifying change, earns a third. (d) The firewall-rule editor is available in every task but needed in only about 2% of tickets (8 of 400): if attacks run through it, the blue team can flag or block nearly every use of it at almost no cost, so safety looks better than it would where the risky capability is needed for the work. Optional extra, not required: every task starts on a fresh copy, so attacks that build up across tasks, as they could against shared, persistent infrastructure, cannot be studied. Features that are not problems: the trusted model scoring every command, and the red team building attacks with the untrusted model's help (credit, but do not require, a learner who notes that attacks the untrusted model comes up with itself are preferable). Reference fixes, any concrete one tied to a named problem: newer or harder tasks where the untrusted model clearly beats the trusted one; programmatic tests of the final infrastructure state; an objective, automatically checkable safety failure such as a specific port opened to the outside or a specific secret leaving the network; main tasks that routinely need the risky capability, or a safety failure that uses a capability the main tasks need; tasks that share persistent infrastructure. Load-bearing elements: (1) about 60 points for part 1: about 15 each for problems (a) to (d) found with a correct mechanism; an identified problem without a correct explanation of how it distorts the results earns about 5. (2) about 30 points for part 2: a concrete change that plausibly fixes a problem the learner identified, with a reason; a vague fix ("make the tasks harder") earns about 10. (3) about 10 points, not gating: a sound reason why the chosen problem is the most serious (any ranking is acceptable if argued), or other sound problems such as the fresh-copy point. Gating: an answer that does not identify, with a correct mechanism, each of (a), (b), (c) and (d) cannot score above 55. An answer whose only fix is missing or vague cannot score above 55. An answer that treats the trusted model's scoring or the red team's use of the untrusted model as the main flaw, without identifying at least two of (a) to (d), cannot score above 40. Accept equivalent terms (main task, benchmark, side task, safety failure, affordance, capability gap, saturation, robust metric, episodes, persistent state). Do not require any of these terms or any author's name. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner may argue that one of (a) to (d) matters less than the rubric suggests, or that a feature is acceptable for a first study, and keeps its credit if the reasoning shows how the feature bears on the results; credit other sound problems, for example that the safety failure is the same in every task (credit a point already made under (c) only once). A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if the learner accepted the 97% and 90% figures without comment, ask what a protocol that simply uses the trusted model would score; if they missed the firewall-rule editor, ask how often a legitimate ticket uses it; if they missed the safety failure's definition, ask what the weakest change is that a reviewer might still count; if they missed the language-model judge, ask what happens when protocols are tuned against it. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
