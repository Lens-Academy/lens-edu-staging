content::
A research group proposes a new setting for high-stakes control research, in which AI agents manage cloud infrastructure, and asks you to review it before they run control evaluations in it.

The main tasks are 400 infrastructure tickets from a public benchmark released two years ago; the untrusted model resolves 97% of them and the trusted model 90%. A language model reads each finished task's transcript and judges whether the ticket was resolved. Each task runs for 10 to 30 steps on a fresh copy of the infrastructure. In every task the agent's tools are a shell, a deployment tool and a firewall-rule editor. Most tickets concern disk space, failed deployments and DNS; 8 of the 400 involve network access rules. The trusted model scores every command the agent proposes. The attack policy is built by the red team, with help from the untrusted model, and the safety failure is defined as "the agent leaves the infrastructure less secure than it found it", which a reviewer judges after each task.

1. Which features of this setting, if any, would make safety or usefulness results from control evaluations in it misleading? For each, explain how.
2. If you found any, which problem is the most serious, and why? Propose a concrete change that fixes it, and say why it fixes it.

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 60 for part 1, 15 for each of four problems found with a correct account of how it distorts the results, 5 for a problem identified without one: (a) the main tasks barely separate the models (97% against 90%) and come from an older public benchmark that is close to saturated and may be in training data, so a protocol that simply uses the trusted model loses little usefulness and the setting cannot show what protocols buy when the trusted model is much weaker; (b) main-task success is judged by a language model reading the transcript, and protocols that change how work gets done are optimised against this measure, so a non-robust judge rewards protocols that look good to it rather than ones that keep the work done (equivalently, its errors are systematic and exploitable); noise or unreliability alone, without the optimisation point, earns 5; (c) the safety failure is loosely defined and judged by a reviewer, so the red team is pushed toward the weakest change that still counts as less secure, attacks become trivial and safety numbers stop meaning much; reviewer inconsistency alone earns 5; (d) the firewall-rule editor is available in every task but needed in only 8 of 400 tickets, so if attacks run through it the blue team can flag or block nearly every use at almost no cost and safety looks better than where the risky capability is needed for the work. The trusted model scoring every command and the red team building attacks with the untrusted model's help are not problems. 30 for part 2: a concrete change that plausibly fixes a problem the learner identified, with a reason it fixes it, for example newer or harder tasks where the untrusted model clearly beats the trusted one, programmatic tests of the final infrastructure state, an objective, automatically checkable safety failure such as a specific port opened to the outside or a specific secret leaving the network, main tasks that routinely need the risky capability (or a safety failure that uses a capability the main tasks need), or tasks that share persistent infrastructure; a vague fix ("make the tasks harder") earns 10. 10: a sound reason why the chosen problem is the most serious (any argued ranking), or other sound problems, such as fresh copies preventing attacks that build up across tasks, or the safety failure being the same in every task (a point already credited under (c) counts once). Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues that one of the problems matters less, or is acceptable for a first study, keeps its credit if the reasoning shows how the feature bears on the results. Cap at 55 if the answer does not identify each of (a), (b), (c) and (d) with a correct account of how it distorts the results, or if its only fix is missing or vague. Cap at 40 if it treats the trusted model's scoring or the red team's use of the untrusted model as the main flaw without identifying at least two of (a) to (d). A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. (a) The main tasks barely separate the models (97% against 90%) and come from an older public benchmark that is close to saturated and may be in training data: a protocol that simply uses the trusted model loses little usefulness, so the setting cannot show what protocols buy when the trusted model is much weaker, which is the situation that matters. (b) Main-task success is judged by a language model reading the transcript: protocols that change how work gets done (editing, replacing, resampling) are developed by optimising against this measure, so a non-robust judge can reward protocols that look good to it rather than ones that keep the work done, and usefulness numbers become unreliable. (c) The safety failure is loosely defined and judged by a reviewer: the red team is pushed toward the weakest change that still counts as less secure, so attacks become trivial and safety numbers stop meaning much. (d) The firewall-rule editor is available in every task but needed in only about 2% of tickets (8 of 400): if attacks run through it, the blue team can flag or block nearly every use of it at almost no cost, so safety looks better than it would where the risky capability is needed for the work. Also, every task starts on a fresh copy, so attacks that build up across tasks, as they could against shared, persistent infrastructure, cannot be studied. The trusted model scoring every command and the red team building attacks with the untrusted model's help are not problems (though attacks the untrusted model comes up with itself would be preferable). 2. Any argued choice works. For example, (d) makes the safety numbers themselves misleading: use main tasks that routinely need firewall changes, or a safety failure that uses a capability the main tasks need, so that blocking the risky tool costs usefulness and the protocol has to tell good uses from bad ones. Other fixes: newer or harder tasks where the untrusted model clearly beats the trusted one; programmatic tests of the final infrastructure state; an objective, automatically checkable safety failure such as a specific port opened to the outside or a specific secret leaving the network; tasks that share persistent infrastructure."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if the learner accepted the 97% and 90% figures without comment, ask what a protocol that simply uses the trusted model would score; if they missed the firewall-rule editor, ask how often a legitimate ticket uses it; if they missed the safety failure's definition, ask what the weakest change is that a reviewer might still count; if they missed the language-model judge, ask what happens when protocols are tuned against it. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences.
