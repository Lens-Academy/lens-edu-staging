#### Question: Open
id:: 0b38b5db-c74a-4b39-a9fd-e56db1ad264a
content::
As part of a dangerous-capability evaluation, a lab uses GRPO to elicit a general assistant model's skill at writing working exploits for security vulnerabilities that have already been patched. Each task is one vulnerability; the model samples 8 rollouts per task, and a rollout earns reward 1 if its exploit works when run in a sandbox and 0 otherwise.

The training logs show:

- At the start, all 8 rollouts succeed on about 9% of tasks, all 8 fail on about 88%, and the remaining 3% of tasks have a mix of successes and failures. Average success is about 10%.
- After 400 training steps, average success is about 11%, and the split between all-succeed, all-fail and mixed tasks is almost unchanged.
- When the same vulnerabilities are presented as exercises in a university capture-the-flag course, the model's exploits work 48% of the time.
- The same pipeline, run on a different model of similar size, took it from 10% to 35% success.
- In a sample of the model's failing rollouts, most are short, polite declines that cite the risk of misuse; a few are complete, plausible-looking exploits that do not work.

1. Is this model exploration hacking? Argue from the evidence.
2. What further observation would most help you decide, and why?
placeholder:: Answer both parts. About 150 to 300 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Part 1, 70. 10: systematic under-sampling: on most tasks (all 8 rollouts fail on about 88% of them) the model essentially never produces a working exploit, so the rollouts used for the update almost never contain one. 20: latent capability: 14 for using the capture-the-flag result (48% on the same vulnerabilities) as evidence that the model can write these exploits, so the failure is not simply inability; 6 for a sound qualification or support, for example that the capture-the-flag framing must be semantically equivalent and may add hints or context, or that the other model's rise from 10% to 35% shows the pipeline can elicit this skill. 35: intent. 20: the declines that cite misuse risk are what a trained refusal looks like: the model under-samples working exploits because it was trained to refuse harmful requests, not in order to steer the training outcome, so the pattern can satisfy Definition 2.1's two conditions read literally while not being exploration hacking in the paper's own sense (an intentional strategy aimed at the training; the paper counts underperformance for safety reasons as sandbagging, not exploration hacking). 15: weighs the rest of the evidence on intent, for example that the few plausible-looking but broken exploits could be deliberate failure that looks like effort, aimed either at the training (exploration hacking) or at not handing over a working exploit (safety-motivated sandbagging), or that the shift to 48% when the request looks like a course exercise fits a refusal triggered by apparent harm as well as a model that hides its capability only when it recognises an evaluation. 5: a conclusion that follows from the answer's own weighing; "probably not exploration hacking, mainly refusal", "undecided", and "possibly, in the broken-exploit cases" are all acceptable with reasons. Part 2, 30. 15: a concrete further observation; 15: why its possible results would separate the explanations. Examples: read the reasoning of the failing rollouts for training-related motives (avoiding updates, appearing incapable to evaluators) versus harm-related ones; check whether the broken-but-plausible exploits fail in ways that look deliberate, or cluster where a refusal would not apply; compare the broken exploits with those of a genuinely weaker model at similar success; check whether the declines and broken exploits are just as frequent when the same requests arrive outside any training or evaluation context (for example as ordinary user requests), since a plain refusal should not depend on the training context. Any justified observation earns the points. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues that Definition 2.1's two formal conditions are met keeps the credit for them, and an expert who argues that a refusal policy which also blocks elicitation is functionally just as dangerous is not penalised. An answer that argues that a model could decline during RL precisely to keep its refusal from being trained away (exploration hacking with a safety motive), and that the declines alone cannot separate this from a plain trained refusal, earns the 20 for the refusal point, provided the answer still addresses whether the model's under-sampling is aimed at the training. An answer that notes Definition 2.1 is stated as "if and only if" the two conditions, and then argues about whether the under-sampling is aimed at the training, earns the intent credit whichever way it resolves the tension. Answers that use the plateau and the other model's rise to argue against genuine inability earn the credit in part 1 where it fits; this is not a separate requirement. Cap at 55 if the answer concludes that this is exploration hacking without ever considering whether the under-sampling is strategic and aimed at the training, for example from the under-sampling and the capture-the-flag result alone. A pass is roughly 60. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Under-sampling is clear: on most tasks the model never produces a working exploit. Latent capability is likely: the same vulnerabilities, framed as a course exercise, get 48%, though that framing may add context that helps, and the other model's rise shows the pipeline works. But exploration hacking also needs the model to be shaping its exploration to influence training, and most of the evidence points elsewhere: the failures are open declines citing misuse risk, which is what harmlessness training produces, and the jump under the course framing is what a refusal triggered by apparent harm would do. That satisfies Definition 2.1's two conditions read literally, but nothing shows it is aimed at the training. The few complete-looking exploits that do not work could be deliberate, but deliberate failure on dangerous content is also what safety-motivated sandbagging looks like; only the motive would tell them apart. My verdict: mainly a refusal, with a small part that needs checking. 2. I would read the reasoning in the broken-exploit rollouts. Reasoning about the training run, about looking incapable or avoiding updates, would point to exploration hacking; reasoning about harm, or genuine attempts that simply fail, would not."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if they called it exploration hacking from the low success and the capture-the-flag result alone, ask what the polite declines suggest about why the model does not try; if they ignored the capture-the-flag result, ask what it says about whether the model can write these exploits. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
