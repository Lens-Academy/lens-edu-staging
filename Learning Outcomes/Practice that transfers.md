## Test:
id:: 0fd8293c-d65c-4f62-bb40-6fe5e4027eb6
#### Question: Open
id:: 3f557c5c-e505-4f8e-8d93-8126d4cb5be4
content:: Mira has just started managing three junior researchers. She wants to get better at one thing: noticing, during a weekly one-on-one meeting, that something is wrong (someone is stuck, or quietly unhappy) and steering the conversation toward it. This is how she practises:

- She reads two books on management and writes a summary of each chapter.
- Every Sunday a friend plays a struggling employee. The friend always opens with "I need to talk to you about a problem", and Mira answers with phrases she copied from the books.
- After each real one-on-one, she rates herself from 1 to 10 on how it went.

Evaluate her practice as a way to build this skill. What would you keep, what would you change, and why?
assessment-instructions:: The question describes Mira, a new manager who wants to get better at noticing, during real weekly one-on-ones, that something is wrong with a report (stuck or quietly unhappy) and steering the conversation toward it. Her practice has three parts. She reads two management books and summarises the chapters. In a weekly roleplay, a friend always opens with "I need to talk to you about a problem" and Mira answers with phrases copied from the books. After each real one-on-one, she rates herself 1 to 10. The question asks the learner to evaluate the practice, say what to keep and what to change, and why.

Score out of 100.

35: Says that the roleplay's trigger does not match the real one. In the roleplay the problem is announced with the same opening line every time, while in real one-on-ones the signs are unannounced, subtle and varied. So she practises responding to an announced problem, not noticing an unannounced one. Give the full 35 for this idea in any words. Give 15 if the answer only says the roleplay is "unrealistic" without saying what about the start of the situation differs.

20: Says that the roleplay's action does not match the real one: answering with copied phrases is not the same as producing her own response in a live conversation, or the practice never varies what she has to say.

15: Says what the reading does and does not do for this skill. For example: it gives her background knowledge of what good steering looks like, but reading and summarising does not rehearse noticing or steering in a conversation. Keeping it for that stated purpose, or dropping it with that reason, both earn the 15.

30: Proposes at least one concrete change and explains it by resemblance to the real situation. Examples: the friend hides the problem, varies it, or sometimes has none; Mira practises in real one-on-ones with a specific thing to notice; her after-meeting review recalls the exact moment a sign appeared instead of a general 1 to 10 rating; she improvises her own words. Give 30 for a concrete change tied to making the trigger or the action closer to the real one. Give 15 for a change that is only general ("make the roleplay more realistic", "practise more").

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The roleplay trains the wrong start: the friend announces the problem with the same line every week, but in a real one-on-one nobody announces it, so Mira never practises noticing. She also recites copied phrases instead of finding her own words. The books are fine as background on what good steering looks like, but they are not practice of the skill. I'd have the friend hide or vary the problem (and sometimes have none), have Mira respond in her own words, and replace the 1 to 10 rating with noting the moment in each real meeting when a sign showed up and what she did."
feedback-instructions:: The learner has evaluated a new manager's practice routine using CFAR's Turbocharging idea: people get good at what they actually rehearse, so practice should resemble the real trigger and the real action, and vary them. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong (usually that the roleplay's trigger is an announced problem, while the real skill is noticing an unannounced one). If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold (for example: "What starts the behaviour in the roleplay, and what starts it in a real meeting?").
