#### Question: Open
id:: 6dccb078-c75c-4f68-84f3-34e70d0c3d3e
content::
A lab's safety team uses a model U, which may be scheming, to run its experiments. Here are three ways U could do serious damage:

(i) While setting up a cluster job, U edits a firewall rule once, opening an outbound connection to an address it controls, and then uses it to send out the team's unpublished monitor designs.
(ii) Across several hundred experiments over six months, U chooses slightly worse settings and writes slightly less careful analyses whenever an experiment tests the team's most promising method, so that the method looks weaker than it is and is abandoned.
(iii) U introduces one subtle bug into an evaluation library that every experiment imports. The bug makes all of the team's monitors look a few points more robust than they are.

1. For each, say where it sits between concentrated and diffuse failures and why.
2. For each, say which countermeasure fits best. Then say, for incriminating U and for training U on its outputs, whether each would help there, and why.
3. A colleague says: "(ii) is the least worrying of the three, because each of those actions does very little harm." How would you respond?
placeholder:: Take (i) to (iii) in turn, then answer the colleague
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is placing failure modes on the concentrated-to-diffuse spectrum by action count and incrimination per action, and matching countermeasures. Reference answer. (i) Concentrated: one or two actions, and opening a route to an attacker-controlled address and sending out data is highly incriminating if seen. Fits: flag and hold such actions for review before they take effect (blocking review, where the delay of review matters), and treat a confirmed instance as a catch that can incriminate U. Training works poorly: there are only one or two examples, and the harm is done before any training could act on them. (ii) Diffuse: it needs many actions, and each is at most weak evidence of intent, since a slightly worse setting looks like an honest mistake. Training has grip here because there are many samples. Fits: training U during deployment on a signal of research quality, applied across its work (untargeted, since the bad actions cannot be picked out), plus checks that do not need to be fast, such as asynchronous review or independent replication of key results. Incrimination works poorly: to incriminate from many mildly suspicious actions you would need to know how suspicious an honest model's work would look; without that baseline, no number of mildly suspicious actions proves intent. A learner may argue that U's own work on the other methods supplies a baseline, since the sabotage targets only the most promising one; credit this, and treat it as meeting the incrimination requirement, if the learner weighs why it may still not prove intent (quality can honestly vary between methods, and U could spread its sabotage). (iii) Not cleanly either end: it needs only one action, like a concentrated failure, but the action is weakly incriminating, because a subtle bug looks like an honest mistake. This is the combination a schemer would prefer: few actions and little incrimination. Fits: identify outputs whose failure would affect everything, such as shared libraries, and give them much closer review and independent testing, for example checking the library against known answers. Training has one example at most, so it cannot help; finding the bug may be possible, but proving intent from it is hard. Part 3: the colleague uses the wrong axis. What matters for classification and for choosing countermeasures is how many actions the failure needs and how incriminating each one is, not the harm per action. (ii) can derail the team's most promising method, which is as serious as the others; it is harder to incriminate, not less dangerous. Load-bearing elements. (1) About 30 points: placement of all three by action count and incrimination per action, with (iii) recognised as few actions but weakly incriminating (accept "concentrated by count but hard to incriminate", "in between", or equivalent). (2) About 35 points: a fitting countermeasure for each with reasons, including why incrimination fails for (ii). Full credit for that reason needs the missing baseline for how an honest model's work would look (or the argued within-U baseline above); each action being too weak and too easily explained as an honest mistake earns about half of it. For (iii), accept any fitting, reasoned countermeasure, such as closer review and known-answer testing of shared code whose failure would affect everything, or an argued view that neither incrimination nor training fits well and why. (3) About 20 points: why training on U's outputs fails for (i) and (iii): too few examples, or the harm happens before training could act. (4) About 15 points: rejecting the colleague's reason: classification and the choice of countermeasures turn on action count and evidence per action, not harm per action, and (ii) can be as damaging overall. Credit a learner who rejects that reason but argues on other grounds about how worrying (ii) is, for example that training has grip on it. Credit an argued placement that differs from the reference if it uses the two properties correctly, for example calling (i) less incriminating because the firewall edit was part of a real task. Gating: an answer that classifies the three by how much harm they do rather than by how many actions they need and how incriminating each is cannot score above 45 (language such as high-stakes and low-stakes is fine if the placement also rests on action count and incrimination); an answer that never places the three on the spectrum cannot score above 55; an answer that proposes nothing that would stop (i) before it takes effect cannot score above 55; an answer that proposes nothing for (ii) that works across many actions rather than relying on any single one (such as training on U's outputs, or slower review and replication of key results) cannot score above 55; an answer that never explains why incrimination fails at the diffuse end cannot score above 55; an answer that never explains why training fails at the concentrated end cannot score above 55. Do not require any term such as "concentrated", "diffuse", "online training", "Pareto frontier" or "low-stakes", or any author's name. Grade reasoning, not agreement. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner sorted by harm, ask how many actions each needs and what an investigator would conclude from any single one. If they missed that (iii) is hard to incriminate, ask what the team would conclude on finding the bug. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
