#### Question: Open
id:: 6dccb078-c75c-4f68-84f3-34e70d0c3d3e
content::
A lab's safety team uses a model U, which may be scheming, to run its experiments. Here are three ways U could do serious damage:

(i) While setting up a cluster job, U edits a firewall rule once, opening an outbound connection to an address it controls, and then uses it to send out the team's unpublished monitor designs.
(ii) Across several hundred experiments over six months, U chooses slightly worse settings and writes slightly less careful analyses whenever an experiment tests the team's most promising method, so that the method looks weaker than it is and is abandoned.
(iii) U introduces one subtle bug into an evaluation library that every experiment imports. The bug makes all of the team's monitors look a few points more robust than they are.

1. For each, say where it sits between concentrated and diffuse failures and why.
2. For each, say which countermeasure fits best. Then say, for incriminating U and for training U on its outputs, whether each would help there, and why.
3. A colleague says: "(ii) is the least worrying of the three, because each of those actions does very little harm." How would you respond?
placeholder:: Take (i) to (iii) in turn, then answer the colleague
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 30: placement of all three by how many bad actions each needs and how incriminating each action is, 10 each: (i) concentrated, one or two actions and highly incriminating if seen; (ii) diffuse, many actions, each at most weak evidence of intent; (iii) few actions like a concentrated failure but weakly incriminating, since a subtle bug looks like an honest mistake (accept "concentrated by count but hard to incriminate", "in between" or equivalent). An argued placement that differs from this counts if it uses the two properties correctly, for example calling (i) less incriminating because the firewall edit was part of a real task. 35: countermeasures, 8 for (i): hold such actions for review before they take effect and treat a confirmed instance as a catch that can incriminate U; 8 for (ii): something that works across many actions rather than any single one, such as training U during deployment on a signal of research quality across its work, or slower review and independent replication of key results; 7 for (iii): any fitting, reasoned countermeasure, such as closer review and known-answer testing of shared code whose failure would affect everything, or an argued view that neither incrimination nor training fits well and why; 12 for why incrimination fails for (ii): to prove intent from many mildly suspicious actions you need a baseline for how suspicious an honest model's work would look, and there is none (or the argued baseline from U's own work on the other methods, weighing why it may still not prove intent); saying only that each action is too weak and too easily explained as an honest mistake earns 6. 20: why training on U's outputs fails for (i) and (iii), 10 each: too few examples, or the harm is done before training could act. 15: the colleague uses the wrong axis: classification and the choice of countermeasures turn on how many actions a failure needs and how incriminating each is, not on harm per action, and (ii) can derail the team's most promising method, so it is harder to incriminate, not less dangerous; rejecting the colleague's reason on other argued grounds (for example that training has grip on (ii)) also counts. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; grade reasoning, not agreement. Cap at 45 if the answer classifies the three by how much harm they do rather than by how many actions they need and how incriminating each is (terms such as high-stakes and low-stakes are fine when the placement also rests on those two properties). Cap at 55 if the answer never places the three on the spectrum, proposes nothing that would stop (i) before it takes effect, proposes nothing for (ii) that works across many actions, never explains why incrimination fails at the diffuse end, or never explains why training fails at the concentrated end. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "(i) Concentrated: one or two actions, and opening a route to an attacker-controlled address and sending out data is highly incriminating if seen. Fits: flag and hold such actions for review before they take effect (blocking review, where the delay of review matters), and treat a confirmed instance as a catch that can incriminate U. Training works poorly: there are only one or two examples, and the harm is done before any training could act on them. (ii) Diffuse: it needs many actions, and each is at most weak evidence of intent, since a slightly worse setting looks like an honest mistake. Training has grip here because there are many samples. Fits: training U during deployment on a signal of research quality, applied across its work (untargeted, since the bad actions cannot be picked out), plus checks that do not need to be fast, such as asynchronous review or independent replication of key results. Incrimination works poorly: to incriminate from many mildly suspicious actions you would need to know how suspicious an honest model's work would look; without that baseline, no number of mildly suspicious actions proves intent. U's own work on the other methods might supply a baseline, since the sabotage targets only the most promising one, but quality can honestly vary between methods, and U could spread its sabotage. (iii) Not cleanly either end: it needs only one action, like a concentrated failure, but the action is weakly incriminating, because a subtle bug looks like an honest mistake. This is the combination a schemer would prefer: few actions and little incrimination. Fits: identify outputs whose failure would affect everything, such as shared libraries, and give them much closer review and independent testing, for example checking the library against known answers. Training has one example at most, so it cannot help; finding the bug may be possible, but proving intent from it is hard. Part 3: the colleague uses the wrong axis. What matters for classification and for choosing countermeasures is how many actions the failure needs and how incriminating each one is, not the harm per action. (ii) can derail the team's most promising method, which is as serious as the others; it is harder to incriminate, not less dangerous."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner sorted by harm, ask how many actions each needs and what an investigator would conclude from any single one. If they missed that (iii) is hard to incriminate, ask what the team would conclude on finding the bug. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
