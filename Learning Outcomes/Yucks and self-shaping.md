## Test:
id:: e89de473-e5f2-4ca8-b217-db8356c62c75
#### Question: Open
id:: db777737-d5f7-4511-80fe-1fc89b1f49b9
content:: Tom is a second-year PhD student. He wants feedback on his drafts early, because he knows early feedback saves him months. Over the past year he has started sending his drafts later and later. This is what happens now:

- When his supervisor's comments arrive, he feels a jolt of dread on seeing the email and often leaves it unopened for a day or two.
- When he does read the comments, he thinks "I should have caught that" about each one.
- To keep himself writing, he allows himself a coffee after every 500 words.
- His supervisor usually replies within a day. The comments are blunt but useful.

Answer these:

1. Why is his habit drifting this way?

2. What might the dread be telling him, if anything?

3. Propose one change, and say why it would work.
assessment-instructions:: The question describes Tom, a PhD student who wants early feedback on drafts but sends them later and later. He feels dread on seeing his supervisor's email and leaves it unopened for a day or two. When he reads the comments he thinks "I should have caught that" about each one. He rewards himself with a coffee after every 500 words. His supervisor replies within a day, bluntly but usefully. The question asks: (1) why the habit is drifting, (2) what the dread might be telling him, (3) one change and why it would work.

Score out of 100.

40, for part 1: Explains the drift by what the bad feelings come right after. The dread and the self-criticism follow opening the email, reading comments, noticing problems, or sending the draft, so they make those behaviours less likely: he learns to delay sending and opening, not to write better drafts. Give the full 40 when the answer says which behaviour the bad feeling discourages. Give 20 if it only says he links feedback with bad feelings, without saying what behaviour that discourages.

25, for part 2: Treats the dread as possible information rather than only an obstacle. It names a plausible goal or concern behind it (for example: not feeling incompetent, not looking bad to his supervisor, a real problem with how blunt the comments are, wanting to send only finished work) and treats it as worth checking or addressing. Give 0 if the answer only says the dread is irrational and should be overridden.

35, for part 3: Proposes one change and explains why it works. Full 35 if the change does one of these and says why: puts something rewarding right after the step he wants (sending, opening the email, reading a comment); removes the punishment at the moment of noticing (for example, treating each comment as a problem caught early instead of thinking "I should have caught that"); gives a reframe that is true (each comment finds a problem before it costs him more later); or addresses the concern behind the dread (for example, asking the supervisor for a different format of feedback). Give 20 for a sensible change with no reason tied to timing, truth or the concern behind the dread. Cap this part at 10 if the change asks Tom to believe something false (for example, "tell himself the comments don't matter").

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. The bad feelings land right after opening the email and reading each comment, and 'I should have caught that' lands right after noticing a problem. So what gets trained away is sending, opening and looking, not making mistakes. 2. The dread might be protecting his sense of being competent, or telling him the blunt tone really costs him something. Worth checking which. 3. Right after he opens the email, he does something he likes, and he reads each comment as 'caught one early', which is true: that is the point of early feedback."
feedback-instructions:: The learner has analysed a PhD student's drifting habit using CFAR's Taste & Shaping chapter: small, immediate feelings of yuck or yum reinforce whatever happened just before them, a yuck can be a sign of an implicit model that something harms another goal, and the fix is to resolve the conflict truthfully. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong. The usual miss is part 1: the self-criticism lands on noticing and the dread lands on opening and sending, so those are the behaviours that get trained away. If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold (for example: "What does Tom do in the second before he feels the dread?").
