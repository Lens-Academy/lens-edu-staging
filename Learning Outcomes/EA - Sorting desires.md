Mara is training for a marathon. She signed up after a friend's post about running one got a lot of likes. Using the distinction between craving and wholesome desire, which part of Mara's wanting is closer to craving? Say what in the description tells you.

The distinction being tested: craving (tanha) is wanting focused on the result of an action: wanting to get, to be, to become, or to get rid of something, and it brings frustration when the result does not come. Wholesome desire (chanda) is wanting focused on the action itself, wanting to do it, with interest in the doing.

content:: Mara says she would be embarrassed to drop out now that everyone knows she signed up. Where does this reason belong on the continuum of motivation that runs from external regulation to intrinsic motivation? Say why.
assessment-instructions:: The case: Mara trains for a marathon. She signed up after a friend's post about a marathon got many likes. On most runs she watches her pace and feels low when slower than planned. She would be embarrassed to drop out now that everyone knows. On some mornings she gets absorbed in the run itself (cold air, rhythm) and forgets the watch.

The continuum, from least to most autonomous: external regulation (acting for a reward or to meet a demand from others), introjected regulation (acting to avoid guilt, shame or anxiety, or for pride and self-worth), identified regulation (acting because one consciously values the goal as personally important), integrated regulation (the value fits with one's other values and needs), intrinsic motivation (doing it for its inherent enjoyment). External and introjected count as controlled. Identified, integrated and intrinsic count as autonomous.

Score out of 100.

50: says why: she keeps running to avoid a bad feeling about herself (embarrassment, loss of face), not for a reward or a demand someone else makes, and not because she values the running or enjoys it.
If the answer places it at external regulation, give 25 in total when the reason correctly says it is pressure rather than valuing or enjoyment: the pressure is real, but nobody demands or rewards anything, the shame is hers. If it places it at identified, integrated or intrinsic, give 0.
feedback-instructions:: The learner placed Mara's reason "I would be embarrassed to drop out" on the continuum from external regulation to intrinsic motivation. content:: Epicurus sorted desires into three classes. Into which class does Mara's desire to finish the marathon fall? Give your reason.
assessment-instructions:: The case: Mara trains for a marathon. She signed up after a friend's post about a marathon got many likes. On most runs she watches her pace and feels low when slower than planned. She would be embarrassed to drop out now that everyone knows. On some mornings she gets absorbed in the run itself and forgets the watch.

Epicurus's three classes: natural and necessary desires (needed for life, for freedom from bodily discomfort, or for happiness, such as food and shelter), natural but not necessary desires (natural pleasures one can live well without, such as fine food), and desires that are neither natural nor necessary, also called vain or empty, which arise from "illusory opinion", such as the wish for fame, and have no natural limit. A desire whose non-fulfilment brings no pain except through opinion is not necessary.

Score out of 100.
Award 100 for either of these, with a reason that fits the class: natural but not necessary (running and its pleasure are natural, but she can live well without finishing a marathon), or neither natural nor necessary (the desire rests on others' approval and on avoiding embarrassment, which come from opinion, and it has no natural limit). Award 70 for a correct class with a vague reason. Award 30 at most for "natural and necessary" unless the answer argues it from a real need, such as her health, and explains why finishing in particular is needed for it. 0 for no class.
feedback-instructions:: The learner placed Mara's desire to finish the marathon in one of Epicurus's classes. Both "natural but not necessary" and "neither natural nor necessary" (vain, from opinion) can be right, depending on the reason. Confirm the class and reason in one sentence, or explain why "natural and necessary" does not fit: she can live well without finishing. 50 to 90 words. No generic praise.
