## Test:
id:: 692fc6d4-828e-4d64-8469-6c7abd01ac89
#### Question: Open
id:: 281a4f37-6bdd-43eb-bf4c-0e572864d6a8
content::
Lena and Tom run a university AI safety reading group together. They are planning next term.

> **Tom:** We have to keep the technical track. It's the core of what we do.
>
> **Lena:** I think we should replace it with a governance track.
>
> **Tom:** Governance is mostly people talking about talking. And most of our members are computer science students.
>
> **Lena:** Sure, they are. But only 3 of the 14 people who started the technical track last term finished it. And governance is far more neglected than technical research.
>
> **Tom:** People drop out of everything. The point of the group is to get people into AI safety research, and the ones who finish the technical track are the ones who go on to apply for research programs.
>
> **Lena:** Are they? I can't think of one who applied. Honestly, if the technical track were actually sending people into research, I wouldn't be pushing for this.
>
> **Tom:** If it turned out nobody from it ever applied anywhere, fine, that would be a real problem. But I'm sure some did.

They have had this argument twice before.

1. Name one crux for Tom and one for Lena, and one reason either of them gives that is not a crux. Say how you can tell.

2. Propose a double crux: a statement they disagree about that could change both of their minds. Write it as an if-then statement both could accept.

3. What could they check or try to settle it?
assessment-instructions:: The question shows a dialogue. Tom and Lena co-run a university AI safety reading group. Tom wants to keep the technical track, Lena wants to replace it with a governance track. Tom says governance is "people talking about talking" and that most members are computer science students. Lena agrees most are computer science students, says only 3 of the 14 who started the technical track last term finished it, and says governance is more neglected. Tom says people drop out of everything, that the point of the group is to get people into AI safety research, and that those who finish the technical track go on to apply for research programs. Lena says she can't think of one who applied, and that if the technical track were actually sending people into research she would not push for the change. Tom says that if nobody from it ever applied anywhere, that would be a real problem, but he is sure some did. The question asks for (1) one crux for each person, one reason that is not a crux, and how the learner can tell, (2) a double crux written as an if-then statement both could accept, and (3) what they could check or try to settle it. A crux is a belief that would change the person's conclusion if it turned out false. A double crux is a statement both disagree about and that could change both minds.

Score out of 100.

30: Cruxes. Tom's crux: whether the technical track leads members to apply for or go into AI safety research (he says it would be "a real problem" if nobody applied). Lena's crux: whether the technical track actually sends people into research (she says she would stop pushing if it did). Accept close versions, for example that Lena's crux includes people finishing the track and then going on to research. 15 for each person.

15: Names a reason that is not a crux and says how one can tell. Valid examples: Lena's point that governance is more neglected (she says she would drop the change if the technical track worked, so neglectedness would not change her mind), Tom's claim that governance is "talking about talking" (his own stated condition is about applications, not about governance), the share of computer science students (neither says it would change their mind, and both agree on it), or the dropout numbers as a reason for Tom (he treats dropout as normal). Lena's dropout figure is part of her crux, so calling it a non-crux for Lena is not a valid example. Give 15 for a valid example with a reason, 5 for a valid example without one.

30: Double crux. A single statement they disagree about and that bears on both conclusions, roughly "people who complete the technical track go on to apply for or work in AI safety research". Stated as an if-then both could accept, for example "if finishers go on to research, keep the technical track, and if they don't, change it". Give 30 for the statement with its if-then, 20 for the right statement without an if-then. Give 0 for this element if the proposed double crux just restates the original question ("which track is better", "should we keep the technical track").

25: A concrete check that would bear on the double crux the learner proposed (or, if that one scored 0, on the double crux described above), for example contacting or looking up people who finished the technical track in past terms to see whether they applied to research programs, asking current finishers about their plans, or tracking applications from next term's finishers. 25 for a concrete check, 10 for a vague one ("do more research", "look at the data").

Cap the score at 30 if the answer mainly argues for one track instead of analysing the disagreement.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Tom's crux: whether finishers of the technical track go on to apply for research. He says that if nobody did, it would be a real problem. Lena's crux is the same question: she'd stop pushing if the track were sending people into research. Lena's 'governance is more neglected' is not a crux: she says she'd keep the technical track if it worked. Double crux: if people who finish the technical track go on to apply for AI safety research, keep it, and if they don't, replace it. Check: message the finishers from the last two or three terms and ask whether they applied anywhere."
feedback-instructions:: The learner analysed a disagreement between Tom and Lena with CFAR's Double Crux: cruxes, non-cruxes, a double crux as an if-then, and a check. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong. The most common miss is a "double crux" that restates the original question instead of finding the belief underneath, or a check too vague to carry out. If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold, for example: "Look at what Lena says would make her stop pushing for the change."
