## Test:
id:: c5982833-e21c-4cb3-ba0c-2ebeaddb48f1
#### Question
content:: The conversation went well — maybe too well. Your friend has genuinely taken the concern on board, and now they say:

*"Okay. You've convinced me this is real, and honestly it's terrifying. But it's trillion-dollar companies and world governments. What am I — one person — supposed to do about any of it? Why did you even tell me this?"*

**Write what you would actually say next. Then explain the mechanism: why does the conversation fail if it ends here, and what does your answer have to do — and avoid — to make the fear productive instead of paralyzing?**

assessment-instructions::
Score according to the following rubric. The student must supply their actual reply, not just theory; theory alone caps at 2. Watch for the two symmetric failure modes: bare doom (validating helplessness) and manufactured optimism (unfounded reassurance) — both cap at 2 even when fluent.
**1** — Ends on doom or deflects the question. *Example: "Honestly? Maybe nothing. I just think people deserve to know the truth about what's coming."*

**2** — Reaches for reassurance with no agency in it, or offers hope so vague it dissolves on contact. *Example: "Don't worry too much — a lot of smart people are working on this. Just stay informed and aware."*

**3** — Pairs the fear with concrete, doable action and states the mechanism correctly: fear without a channel for action gets resolved by discarding the fear — denial, fatalism, or avoiding you — while fear plus tangible agency converts into motivation. The reply names at least one specific, genuinely doable next step (have this same conversation with someone they trust, contact a representative, join a local group or course) and frames the situation as unresolved rather than lost. *Example: "I'd say: 'I told you because this isn't decided yet — it's a fight about policy, and policy moves when enough ordinary people are loudly worried. The single most useful thing you can do this month is have this conversation with one person you trust. That's not a consolation prize; it's literally how every issue becomes politically real.' If I let the conversation end at 'what could I possibly do,' their mind will protect itself the only way it can — by deciding I was exaggerating. The fear needs somewhere to go, or it gets thrown away."*

**4** — As above, plus locates the hope in the actions themselves, not just in predicted outcomes. "It will be fine" is a prediction you can't stand behind; "here is what we can do, and doing it is real" is a claim you can. The actions are worth hoping in on their own terms — each conversation had, each person reached, each representative contacted is a real change in the world that has already happened, not a lottery ticket on a distant outcome. A student at this level can offer hope that survives even pessimism about the endgame. *Example: Adds "And I wouldn't promise it works out — I don't know that. The hope I can honestly offer isn't 'we win,' it's 'the things we can do are real.' Every person who starts taking this seriously is a change that already happened, not a bet on one that might. I'd tell them: 'I can't promise the ending. I can promise the doing is real — issues nobody was allowed to ignore got that way one refused-to-drop-it conversation at a time.'"*

**5** — As above, plus makes the ask a joint one: offer to do the action *with* them, not assign it to them. "Come to the meetup with me," "let's both talk to one person this week and compare notes," "I'll sit with you while you write your rep." The mechanism: the biggest barrier to a first action isn't conviction, it's isolation — the fear of being the weird one alone. A solo ask is homework; a joint ask is an invitation, and it converts "what can one person do?" into "we're already two." The shared action is also the movement in miniature: it's the moment the friend stops being an audience and becomes a participant, alongside someone they trust. *Example: Adds "And I'd never leave it as their assignment. I'd say: 'Come with me — there's a meetup Thursday, we'll go together. Or if that's too much, let's each talk to one person this week and trade stories after.' The honest answer to 'what can one person do' is: you're not one person. You know me. We can start together — and 'together' is the entire answer to the trillion-dollar-companies problem, just at its smallest scale."*
