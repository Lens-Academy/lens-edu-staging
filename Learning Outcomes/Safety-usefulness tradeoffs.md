#### Question: Open
id:: 7b3bf93b-d996-4b5d-90df-82477a337289
content::
A new law requires AI developers to pass an annual external risk assessment before deploying frontier models internally. The assessors work from a published checklist of safety measures and can only check what a developer can show them. Lab R adopts safety measures mainly to pass this assessment; its leadership thinks misalignment risk is small.

A safety researcher at Lab R has six months and must choose one project:

- **Project X:** a new monitor for AI agents that the safety team expects to catch far more attacks per unit of compute than anything in use. Showing that it works requires internal data that the assessors are not allowed to see.
- **Project Y:** a standardised control evaluation that outside assessors could run on any developer's deployment. The safety team expects the measures it would push developers towards to catch fewer attacks per unit of compute than Project X.

1. The safety-usefulness tradeoff model says a developer adopts whichever measures buy the most safety per unit of usefulness given up, within a limited budget. Does it describe how Lab R chooses its safety measures? Explain.
2. Which project should the researcher choose to reduce risk the most? Justify the choice.
3. Now suppose instead that Lab R's leadership shares the researcher's concern about misalignment, and is deploying fast only because a competitor is three months behind. Does your answer to 1 change? What kind of safety work would now help most: work like Project X, work like Project Y, or something else? Explain.
placeholder:: Answer 1, 2 and 3 in turn
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is judging when the safety-usefulness tradeoff model applies and what kind of safety work helps in each case. Reference answer. 1: Poorly. The model assumes the developer adopts whichever measures buy the most real safety per unit of usefulness given up. Lab R adopts measures to satisfy assessors who can only check what is shown against a checklist, so the connection between a measure's real safety value and whether it is adopted is weak; Lab R optimises for passing the assessment. 2: A strong answer weighs real safety value against the chance that the output is adopted and kept. Project X's gains may never be adopted, since leadership does not value the risk reduction and assessors cannot credit it; Project Y produces an ask that is legible and externally verifiable, so it can become part of what assessors require, at Lab R and at other developers, even if each measure is less efficient. Credit an argued choice of X, for example that the monitor is cheap enough that the safety team can adopt it on its own, or that it can later be made verifiable, provided the answer engages with how X would get adopted at Lab R. 3: Yes. Now the lab behaves like a rushed but reasonable developer: it will adopt whichever measures give the best safety per unit of usefulness within what the race allows, so the tradeoff model fits, and work that pushes out the frontier of safety per unit of cost, like Project X, is directly valuable because it will be used. Project Y matters less for Lab R's own choices, though it may still matter for other developers, and because the assessment still binds Lab R, Y keeps some value there too; credit a learner who says so. Also credit, as an answer to what helps most, work that enlarges the budget, such as evidence of risk that would justify giving up more speed, since the race is what limits how much usefulness the lab will give up. Load-bearing elements. (1) About 30 points: part 1 judges the fit by how Lab R actually selects measures, recognising that selection driven by assessors' checklist and what can be shown weakens the link between real safety value and adoption. (2) About 35 points: part 2 justifies the choice by what would happen to each project's output (whether it would be adopted, credited by assessors, kept, at Lab R or by developers generally), weighing that against safety value. (3) About 35 points: part 3 recognises that with shared goals and a race the tradeoff model fits, at least for the measures Lab R chooses itself, and names what would help most with a reason: technique improvements like X, because the lab will adopt whatever buys the most safety per unit of cost, or work that enlarges the budget. Credit an answer that says the model fits for what Lab R adopts voluntarily while the assessment still imposes checklist measures. Gating: an answer that says in part 1 that the model does describe Lab R, without separating what passes the assessment from real safety value, cannot score above 50; an answer that omits part 2, or chooses a project without any reasoning about whether its output would be adopted (at Lab R or by developers generally), cannot score above 55; an answer that omits part 3, or says part 3 changes nothing without an argued reason, cannot score above 55. Do not require any term such as "Pareto frontier", "safety budget", "political will" or "political feasibility", or any author's name. Do not penalise a learner who argues that the checklist could itself be shaped by researchers, if the parts are answered. Grade reasoning, not agreement. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner treated Lab R as picking the most effective measures, ask who decides what Lab R adopts and on what evidence. If part 3 did not change anything, ask what a lab that shares your goals does with a better monitor. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
