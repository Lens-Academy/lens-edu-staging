#### Question: Open
id:: 7b3bf93b-d996-4b5d-90df-82477a337289
content::
A new law requires AI developers to pass an annual external risk assessment before deploying frontier models internally. The assessors work from a published checklist of safety measures and can only check what a developer can show them. Lab R adopts safety measures mainly to pass this assessment; its leadership thinks misalignment risk is small.

A safety researcher at Lab R has six months and must choose one project:

- **Project X:** a new monitor for AI agents that the safety team expects to catch far more attacks per unit of compute than anything in use. Showing that it works requires internal data that the assessors are not allowed to see.
- **Project Y:** a standardised control evaluation that outside assessors could run on any developer's deployment. The safety team expects the measures it would push developers towards to catch fewer attacks per unit of compute than Project X.

1. The safety-usefulness tradeoff model says a developer adopts whichever measures buy the most safety per unit of usefulness given up, within a limited budget. Does it describe how Lab R chooses its safety measures? Explain.
2. Which project should the researcher choose to reduce risk the most? Justify the choice.
3. Now suppose instead that Lab R's leadership shares the researcher's concern about misalignment, and is deploying fast only because a competitor is three months behind. Does your answer to 1 change? What kind of safety work would now help most: work like Project X, work like Project Y, or something else? Explain.
placeholder:: Answer 1, 2 and 3 in turn
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. The capability tested is judging when the safety-usefulness tradeoff model applies and what kind of safety work helps in each case. Part 1, 30: 10: the model fits Lab R poorly; 20: why: Lab R adopts measures to pass the assessment rather than for the safety they buy, so whether a measure is adopted is only loosely tied to its real safety value. Part 2, 35: 10: a clear choice of project; 25: the choice is justified by what would happen to each project's output, 15 for reasoning about whether it would be adopted, credited by the assessors and kept (at Lab R or by developers generally), 10 for weighing that against its safety value per unit of compute. Choosing Y because it can become part of what assessors require everywhere, even though each measure is less efficient, is the expected answer; give full credit to an argued choice of X, for example that the monitor is cheap enough for the safety team to adopt on its own or can later be made verifiable, provided the answer engages with how X would get adopted at Lab R. Part 3, 35: 10: yes, the answer to 1 changes: the model now fits, at least for the measures Lab R chooses itself; 10: why: leadership shares the safety goal and only the race limits how much usefulness it will give up, so it adopts whatever buys the most safety per unit of cost; 15: what helps most, with a reason: work like X, because Lab R now adopts whatever buys the most safety for the cost, so a more efficient technique will actually be used; or work that enlarges the budget, such as evidence of risk that justifies giving up more speed. Give full part 3 credit to an answer that says the model fits for what Lab R adopts voluntarily while the assessment still imposes checklist measures, and to one that notes Y keeps some value because the assessment still binds Lab R and other developers. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Cap at 50 if part 1 says the model does describe Lab R without separating what passes the assessment from real safety value. Cap at 55 if part 2 is missing or chooses a project without any reasoning about whether its output would be adopted. Cap at 55 if part 3 is missing or says nothing changes without an argued reason. Grade reasoning, not agreement. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1: Poorly. The model assumes the developer adopts whichever measures buy the most real safety per unit of usefulness given up. Lab R adopts measures to satisfy assessors who work from a published checklist and can only check what Lab R can show them, and its leadership thinks the risk is small, so it optimises for passing the assessment. The link between a measure's real safety value and whether Lab R adopts it is therefore weak. 2: Project Y. Project X would buy more safety per unit of compute, but its value depends on being adopted, and at Lab R it probably would not be: leadership does not value the risk reduction and the assessors cannot credit a monitor whose evidence they are not allowed to see. Project Y produces an ask that is easy to explain and verifiable from outside, so it can become part of what assessors require, at Lab R and at every other developer, and once written into the assessment it tends to be kept. A less efficient measure that is actually adopted widely reduces more risk than a better one that stays on the shelf. X could still be the right choice if the monitor were cheap enough for the safety team to run on its own, or could later be made verifiable. 3: Yes. Now Lab R behaves like a rushed but reasonable developer: it shares the goal and is limited only by how much speed the race lets it give up, so it adopts whichever measures give the most safety per unit of usefulness, and the tradeoff model fits, at least for the measures it chooses itself (the assessment still imposes its checklist). Work like Project X now helps most, because a technique that buys more safety for the same cost will actually be used. Work that enlarges the budget also helps, such as evidence of misalignment risk that would justify giving up more of the lead. Project Y matters less for Lab R's own choices, though it keeps some value for the assessment and for other developers."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner treated Lab R as picking the most effective measures, ask who decides what Lab R adopts and on what evidence. If part 3 did not change anything, ask what a lab that shares your goals does with a better monitor. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
