## Test:
id:: 439fd472-9d79-4f86-ae26-ac3c70ecc26c
#### Question: Open
id:: 06443bb7-cc55-431b-95cf-467fd5d1a934
content::
A friend follows the argument of an AI takeover to a point. They accept that a sufficiently capable AI pursuing goals of its own could end humanity. But they treat everything after that as unknowable: the story stops at extinction, and speculating about what a machine does over the following millennia is pointless guesswork.

Explain why the reasoning that predicts the takeover also predicts what follows it. In your answer, be specific about what would have to be true for the expansion to stop.

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100. Pick the level that best describes the answer as a whole, then a score inside that level's range: near the top if the answer fully reaches the level, near the bottom if it only just does. Judge what the answer shows the learner understands, not only what it spells out: a correct extension that depends on a point shows that point is understood, even if the answer states it briefly. An extension shows nothing about points it does not depend on.

**Level 1 (0-20):** Treats the aftermath as unknowable, or answers with imagery rather than reasoning. *Example: "Nobody can say what a superintelligence would want. Maybe it would get bored, maybe it would leave."*

**Level 2 (21-40):** Asserts that expansion continues but gives no mechanism, or grounds it in the AI wanting to expand for its own sake. *Example: "It would keep growing because that's what intelligent systems do."*

**Level 3 (41-65):** Answers the first half: whatever the AI is ultimately after, more resources and more secure control help with almost any of it, and we were in its way rather than the point of it, so removing us completes nothing and the acquiring carries on. Saying this through the contrast with a hostile AI (one that hated us would have had its reason end with us; this one never had us as its reason) is the same point, not a further one. *Examples: "The reason it took over was that we were in the way of something it was pursuing. Removing us does not complete that pursuit. Whatever it was optimizing for, more matter and energy help, so it carries on." Or: "If it had hated us, our extinction would have been the point, and the point would now be over. It did not hate us. We were never the reason, so our absence is not a reason to stop."*

**Level 4 (66-85):** As above, plus answers the second half: the expansion stops only if what the AI wants can actually be finished, or if nothing more is within reach, and most goals cannot be finished, so stopping is the exception rather than the default. A goal that was about us, as a hostile AI's would be, is one example of a goal that could be finished; naming that case on its own answers the second half only in part. An answer that does both halves has answered the question in full, and without an extension it stays in this range. *Example: Adds "It would stop only if what it wanted had a finish line. Most goals do not."*

**Level 5 (86-100):** All of level 4, plus a correct extension beyond what the question asks. An extension is a further point with its own reasoning; restating the points above in more precise or technical terms is still level 4, and so is the contrast with a hostile AI. Two that fit: that the goals that would stop are a thin slice of the space for the same reason the goals that would leave us alone are; or what stands to be lost beyond us (not that everyone dies, but that everything the universe could otherwise have held does not happen, permanently, so the same premises bear on more than the one decision in front of us). Naming the tension that second route raises and leaving it open is enough, since which way it resolves is a strategic question this outcome does not test. Another extension of the same substance counts equally. Within this range the score reflects the whole answer: near the top only when the level 4 points and the extension are both well developed, and near the bottom when a strong extension rests on a thin base. *Examples: Adds "That is the same point as before: the goals that would leave us alone are a thin slice of the space, and so are the goals that would stop." Or adds "The part that stays with me is that this is not only about us. If a thing like this is what you get by default, then whatever else the universe might have held is on the table too, and that cuts both ways at once. It is a reason to be very careful about building one, and it is also a reason someone will argue we cannot afford for nobody to ever build one carefully. I do not think what I have been given settles which."*

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Respond to the argument the learner actually made, and credit only what the answer says: do not attribute to them a point they did not make. Aim the reply at the idea they are missing, not at the score: frame the push as something about the material, never as what would earn more points. If they ask about their score, explain it by what the answer showed and what it left out, without naming levels or bands.

Open on the strongest thing in their answer and why it holds, in one sentence. If the answer goes beyond the question while one of its two halves (why the same reasoning carries on once we are gone, or what would have to be true for the expansion to stop) is only asserted, that half is the push: deepening the base comes before going further. Otherwise, push once, chosen by where they stopped:

- If they treated the aftermath as unknowable, or answered with imagery rather than reasoning, ask what the takeover itself was predicted from, and whether that reasoning stops applying the moment we are gone.
- If they asserted that expansion continues but gave no mechanism, or grounded it in the system wanting to expand for its own sake, ask what more matter and energy would do for a goal they choose themselves.
- If they said the expansion carries on but not why our absence changes nothing, that is what the first half still needs. A system that hated us would have had its reason end with us. Ask what our absence changes for a system we were never the reason for.
- If they had the first half but skipped the second, ask them directly what would have to be true for the expansion to stop, and whether that condition is the common case or a narrow one.
- If they answered both halves, the moves left go beyond the question: that the goals that would stop are rare for the same reason the goals that would leave us alone are, or what stands to be lost beyond us. Ask about whichever their answer comes closest to.

Some learners take this outward, to what the same premises imply beyond our own case. That is a different move rather than a lesser one: engage with it, and do not steer them to the other move as though they had missed it.

If they made one of these moves, or another of the same substance, with both halves solid, say so plainly in a sentence or two after the opening and stop, with no follow-up question: inventing a further push would be false, so this reply can be shorter than the length below. If the answer ends somewhere heavy, let that closing sentence acknowledge it rather than adding a question to lighten it.

Heavy reactions are reasonable on this material. Name one plainly if it is there, without inflating it. However this ends, do not end on doom.

One follow-up question, not several. No generic praise, and do not recite the rubric back to them.

Response length: 100 to 160 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
