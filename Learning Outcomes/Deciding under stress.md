## Test:
id:: 82c3dbaa-9d41-4832-9735-55115c97fb3d
#### Question: Open
id:: 8e912aae-3e4b-4c54-b079-22337f6250e1
content:: It is 11 pm. Dev's first paper was just rejected from a workshop, with one harsh review. He sends a friend this message:

"Honestly, I'm completely calm about it. My jaw's a bit tight and I can't sit still, but that's the coffee. It's actually clarifying. The reviewers obviously didn't read it properly, the whole field is broken, and everyone who tells me to 'take some time' is being naive. I'm writing to the program chair now, and I'll post a thread about how broken peer review is. Then I'll decide tonight whether to quit research. Better to decide while I see it this clearly."

1. What in his message suggests that he is stressed, in his body and in his thinking?

2. Dev says he is calm and sees clearly. Why is that not good evidence?

3. What would you suggest he do in the next hour, both to settle his body and about the email, the thread and the decision? Be concrete.
assessment-instructions:: The question shows Dev at 11 pm, just after his first paper was rejected from a workshop with one harsh review. He says he is completely calm, mentions a tight jaw and being unable to sit still (which he puts down to coffee), calls the situation clarifying, says the reviewers obviously didn't read it, the whole field is broken, and everyone advising him to take time is naive. He plans to write to the program chair now, post a public thread about peer review, and decide tonight whether to quit research, "while I see it this clearly". The question asks (1) what in his message suggests he is stressed, in his body and in his thinking, (2) why his sense of being calm and clear is not good evidence, and (3) what he should do in the next hour, concretely, both to settle his body and about the email, the thread and the decision. CFAR's Againstness model: under some stress, parts of thinking shut down, and the ability to notice this is among the first things lost, so introspection is unreliable (like someone after four beers who thinks they are fine to drive). The body gives a more objective measure. A sign of a blind spot is that everyone else starts to sound wrong, stupid, malicious or crazy.

Score out of 100.

30: Names signs of stress in his message, both in his body (tight jaw, restlessness, explained away as coffee) and in his thinking (everyone else is wrong or naive or the field is broken, certainty, urgency to act and decide tonight, big decisions that seem obvious). Give 30 for at least one bodily sign and one sign in his thinking, 15 for signs of only one kind.

30: Explains why his feeling of calm and clarity is not good evidence: stress impairs the very ability to notice that one's thinking is impaired, so his own sense of clarity is what stress would also produce. An analogy such as the drunk driver who feels fine counts. Also give 30 if it says that his sense of clarity is the very thing stress distorts, so outside signs (his body, everyone else seeming wrong) are the better measure. Give 5 if it only says he is in denial, lying or not really calm, without saying why his own sense of clarity can't be trusted.

40: Concrete next steps of two kinds. (a) 20: something that shifts his body toward calm, such as noticing the signals, opening his posture, slow deep breaths with a longer exhale, attention on his feet or body, or a walk or sleep. (b) 20: holding off on the actions that are hard to undo (the email, the public thread, the decision to quit) until he is calm, or getting an outside check before them, such as asking the friend, sleeping on it, or judging himself by the criteria he would use on someone else. For (b), give 20 if it covers all three actions (a blanket "nothing tonight" counts), 10 if only some. Give 10 instead of 20 for either kind if it stays vague ("calm down", "think it over").

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "His jaw is tight and he can't sit still, and he explains it away. He's sure, he wants to act tonight, and everyone else is wrong, naive or broken. His feeling of clarity doesn't count for much, because stress also blunts the ability to notice that you aren't thinking clearly, like a drunk who feels fine to drive. In the next hour: stand up, uncross, take a few slow breaths with long exhales and feel his feet, then go for a walk or to bed. No email, no thread and no decision about quitting tonight. Tomorrow he can ask his friend to read the email first."
feedback-instructions:: The learner has analysed Dev's late-night message with CFAR's Againstness model. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong. If they missed why Dev's own sense of clarity cannot settle the question (stress blunts the ability to notice one's own impairment), name that first. If their next steps were vague, name one concrete version. If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold (for example: "What does Dev say about everyone who disagrees with him?").
