#### Question: Open
id:: 5778946c-2f07-4335-84ba-33045f39605a
content::
A customer-support agent U will take 100,000 actions (refunds, account changes, messages) over a deployment. U may be scheming; the security team's working assumption is that a scheming U would attempt a harmful action about 10 times in total. A monitor will score every action for suspicion, and the team can afford human audits of 1,000 actions, so it will audit the 1,000 most suspicious. An audit always recognises a harmful action. Two monitors are on offer, each tested on a large labelled set of normal and harmful actions. The table gives each monitor's AUC and the share of harmful actions it flags when its threshold is set to flag a given share of normal actions:

| | AUC | 0.5% of normal flagged | 1% | 2% | 10% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monitor A | 0.95 | 15% | 25% | 45% | 95% |
| Monitor B | 0.90 | 50% | 60% | 66% | 75% |

1. For each monitor, estimate how many of the roughly 10 harmful actions you expect to be audited, and how many of the 1,000 audits you expect to land on normal actions. Explain what sets these numbers.
2. Which monitor should the team deploy? Say what role, if any, the AUC column should play in the decision, and why.
3. Management later offers enough reviewers to audit 10,000 actions. Would that change your choice? Explain.
placeholder:: Show your estimates, then answer parts 2 and 3
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 35: part 1, 15: the catch estimates, 5 each: Monitor A about 25%, about 2 to 3 of the 10 harmful actions; Monitor B about 60%, about 6 of 10; and the reason: 1,000 audits among about 100,000 actions puts the threshold at about 1% of normal actions flagged, so the 1% column applies; 20: nearly all of the 1,000 audits land on normal actions (any figure from about 990 up), because there are only about 10 attacks against 1,000 audits. 40: part 2, 20: chooses B, because what matters is the catch rate at the false-positive rate the budget sets, where B catches 60% against A's 25%; 20: AUC should play little or no role, because it summarises performance across every threshold, while the budget fixes one, and A's advantage lies at thresholds this budget cannot afford. 25: part 3, 10: the choice changes to A, and 15: because 10,000 audits move the threshold to about 10% of normal actions flagged, where A catches about 95% (9 to 10 attacks) against B's 75% (7 to 8); an answer that keeps B earns none of these 25 unless it gives a sound reason grounded in the numbers, and noting only that both monitors would then catch at least one attack is not such a reason, because uncaught attacks still cause harm and A lets fewer through. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Cap at 30 if the answer chooses A for the 1,000-audit budget without a sound reason grounded in the numbers. Cap at 55 if the answer gives no estimate of attacks caught, puts more than about 50 of the 1,000 audits on harmful actions, or never recognises that nearly all audits go to normal actions. Cap at 55 if the answer chooses B but never says that AUC summarises thresholds this budget does not use, or that the budget fixes one threshold where the ranking can differ. Grade reasoning, not agreement. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. With about 10 harmful actions among 100,000, auditing the 1,000 most suspicious is, to a very good approximation, auditing everything above the threshold that flags 1% of the roughly 99,990 normal actions: the harmful actions are too few to move the threshold noticeably. So the 1% column applies: Monitor A catches about 25% of attacks, about 2 to 3 of 10, and Monitor B about 60%, about 6 of 10. Since there are only about 10 attacks against 1,000 audits, at most about 10 audits can land on attacks, so about 994 to 998 of the 1,000 land on normal actions whichever monitor is used. 2. Deploy B. AUC summarises performance across every threshold, including thresholds that flag far more actions than this budget can audit. A's advantage lies there (at 10% it catches 95% against 75%), which raises its AUC but buys nothing at this budget, so AUC should play little or no role; what matters is the catch rate at the false-positive rate the budget sets. 3. Yes. With 10,000 audits the threshold moves to about 10% of normal actions flagged, where A catches about 95% (about 9 to 10 attacks) against B's 75% (about 7 to 8), so the choice flips to A. Caveats worth keeping in mind: the assumed attack count and the lab-tested rates may not hold in deployment, a scheming U will pick attacks the deployed monitor scores low, one caught attack may be enough to shut U down, and the two monitors could be combined."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner chose by AUC, point them to the column that matches what the team can afford to audit. If they missed that nearly all audits go to normal actions, ask them to compare 10 attacks with 1,000 audits. If they kept B at 10,000 audits, point them to the column that budget now matches. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences.
