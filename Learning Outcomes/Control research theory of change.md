## Test:
id:: 519afc89-b67a-4910-9639-ec4111aa3fb1

#### Question: Open
id:: 52dc7c97-450d-4ac2-96e3-c2af8cf6fee7
content::
A safety team at a frontier AI company proposes a six-month project: a **permissions broker** for its internal AI agents. Every request an agent makes for more compute, access to a dataset or model checkpoint, or network access would go to a smaller, older model the company trusts, which approves it, denies it, or sends it to a human. Every request and decision would be logged. The team would build it now and run it on the company's current coding agents.

1. Lay out the causal chain from this project to a reduction in catastrophic risk, saying how each step leads to the next. Cover: what the project produces; whether building it now is informative even though today's agents may be too weak to seriously test it, and why; who, if anyone, would adopt it, why, and what might stop them; which threat it targets, and whether and why it would hold up against an agent that is trying to get around it; and what could make it obsolete.
2. Name the link in your chain you think is least likely to hold, and say why.
3. Name one observation that would raise your confidence in that link and one that would lower it.
placeholder:: Answer all three parts. About 300 to 500 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. There is no single correct chain; grade whether the learner's chain is connected, specific to this project and honest about its weak points. 40 for part 1: a chain that goes step by step from what the project produces to reduced catastrophic risk, each step stating how it leads to the next, 8 for each of the five items the question lists, answered for this project: what it produces (for example a working broker, request logs, data on how often agents ask for more access and how often the trusted model gets it wrong, the cost in slowed work); whether and why it is informative now (for example it surfaces engineering problems, friction and false-positive rates and builds infrastructure and habits that must exist before agents become dangerous, even though current agents are too weak to test it against a real adversary; a reasoned argument that it is not informative also counts); who adopts it, why, and what might stop them (for example the company's infrastructure teams, other companies if shared, regulators if it becomes a standard; obstacles such as the slowdown, internal politics, reluctance to share security tooling); the threat and whether the broker holds up (for example an agent gaining the access it needs for a rogue job or for copying weights; a reasoned argument either way counts, such as the agent having no other route to those resources, or the trusted model being unable to judge plausible requests from a stronger agent); what could make it obsolete (for example agents so capable that the trusted model can no longer judge their requests, or a change in deployment that bypasses the broker). A list of benefits with no connecting steps earns at most 20 of these points. 30 for part 2: one link named and a reason, grounded in this project, why it is the least likely to hold; any link counts if the reason says how it would fail here (strong candidates: a weaker trusted model cannot tell a legitimate request from a harmful one when the agent writes a plausible justification; many small approved requests add up to dangerous access; the slowdown leads teams to route around or disable the broker; agents gain access through channels the broker does not cover); a reason that only restates the setup ("the trusted model might be fooled" with no account of how) earns at most 15; lose up to 10 if a clearly shakier link in the learner's own chain goes unaddressed. 30 for part 3, 15 each: one observation that would raise and one that would lower confidence in the link named in part 2, each something someone could actually see (for example a red team using a strong model fails, or succeeds, to get dangerous access through the broker with plausible requests; the false-positive rate stays tolerable, or engineers start bypassing the broker; another company adopts it; audits find access paths that do not go through it); "more research" or "we will see" is not an observation. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who concludes the project is not worth doing, or that its value lies mainly in something other than blocking attacks (such as producing evidence or building habits), can score full marks if the chain is connected and the weakest link and observations are specific. Cap at 50 if the chain is a list of benefits with no stated connections between steps, or if no weakest link is named or it is named without a reason. Cap at 55 if any of the five items is missing or answered only in generic terms that would fit any project, if the answer never argues whether the broker would hold up against an agent trying to get around it (a reasoned "it would not" counts), or if it does not give at least one observation that would raise and one that would lower confidence in the named link. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "One possible chain (there is no single correct one). 1. The project produces a working broker, a log of every request and decision, data on how often agents ask for more access and how often the trusted model gets it wrong, and an estimate of how much it slows work. Building it now is informative even though today's agents are too weak to test it against a real adversary: it surfaces engineering problems, friction and false-positive rates, and builds infrastructure and habits that must already exist when agents become dangerous. The company's own infrastructure teams would adopt it if the slowdown is tolerable, other companies if it is shared, and regulators might make it a standard; the slowdown, internal politics and reluctance to share security tooling could stop them. It targets an agent gaining the access it needs for a rogue job or for copying weights. It holds up only if the agent has no other route to those resources and the trusted model can judge the requests; a stronger agent writing plausible justifications may get past it. It becomes obsolete when agents are so capable that the trusted model can no longer judge their requests, or when a change in how agents are deployed bypasses the broker. If it holds, dangerous access is denied or sent to a human, so an attempt to gain it is blocked or caught, which lowers catastrophic risk. 2. The weakest link: a smaller, older model cannot tell a legitimate request from a harmful one when the agent writes a plausible justification, and many small approved requests can add up to dangerous access. 3. Raise: a red team using a strong model, writing plausible requests, fails to assemble dangerous access through the broker. Lower: that red team succeeds, for example by splitting what it needs into many small requests that are each approved."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest link in the learner's chain in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve the answer, for example a step that was asserted rather than connected, or an observation that nobody could actually make. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
