#### Question: Open
id:: a2da1d97-ce98-4c2f-8af0-0205cc46b0af
content::
An AI developer proposes that, for the next six months, a team of its AI agents M will run the company's dangerous-capability evaluations on new models end to end: designing the tests, running them and writing the verdicts. Human staff would stop reviewing individual results and would read only a quarterly summary. The proposal offers four pieces of evidence:

- A. For three months, M and the human evaluation team worked on the same models. M found more real problems, and its verdicts held up better when outside experts tried to break them.
- B. For two weeks, a human red team took control of several copies of M, instructing and fine-tuning them to hide a planted dangerous result from the cross-checks that the other copies of M run on each other's work. The cross-checks caught every attempt.
- C. In hundreds of interviews and scenario tests, M consistently says it wants the evaluations to be accurate and behaves that way, including in tests designed to tempt it.
- D. Over the past year, humans double-checked 400 of M's verdicts; the extra human review changed the outcome only twice.

1. Is this handoff justified on this evidence? Say what the decision should turn on.
2. For each of A, B, C and D, say what it supports and what it leaves unestablished.
3. What further evidence or argument would most strengthen the case?
placeholder:: Answer all three parts
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is judging a proposal to defer safety work to AI agents. Load-bearing elements: (1) about 30 points: the decision should turn on whether handing off is safer than the realistic alternative of keeping humans in the loop, not on whether the agents are perfectly safe in absolute terms. The handoff is clearly justified if the agents are at least as capable as the humans they replace at this task and at least as trustworthy, and better on at least one, for the whole six months; these are sufficient conditions, so accept a reasoned trade-off between them (for example a small capability shortfall made up by human spot checks). A reasonable verdict is that capability looks supported but trust is not established; "not yet" or "only if" verdicts are acceptable if justified, and so is a reasoned "yes" that says what B, C and D leave open and why the remaining risk is still below that of keeping humans in the loop. Gating: an answer that never judges M's trustworthiness, or the overall risk of the handoff, relative to the humans M would replace or to keeping humans in the loop (for example "no AI can be trusted with this", "M is safe", or "the question is whether M can be trusted") cannot score above 55 in total; noting only that M outperformed the humans in A does not count as that comparison; an answer that treats capability evidence alone as enough cannot score above 40 in total; an answer that treats C as establishing that M is trustworthy, never saying why a model hiding misalignment would say and do the same in tests it can recognise, cannot score above 55 in total; an answer that gives no concrete, reasoned gap for any of B, C and D cannot score above 50 in total; an answer in which no gap or strengthening move concerns whether M's safety holds for the whole six months (for example two weeks or three months of evidence against six months of work, ongoing checks that would raise an alarm, or a verdict that makes the handoff conditional on checks continuing through the period) cannot score above 55 in total. (2) about 45 points, about 11 each. A: supports capability (M is at least as good as the humans at this task); it says little or nothing about whether M would sabotage the work once human review stops, and three months may not cover every part of the task or the full period. B: supports a control-style argument, that copies of M could not get a corrupted verdict past the cross-checks; it leaves open, for example, whether two weeks of a human red team finds strategies as good as what M's copies could devise over six months, whether the fine-tuned copies were attacking with full effort, whether the checking copies, being M too, would still report honestly if M were misaligned (they could fail together or collude), or whether the checks cover every way the verdicts could be corrupted. C: behavioural evidence that M is not misaligned; it cannot rule out an M that is hiding misalignment, since such a model would say and do the same in tests it can recognise, and it says little about whether M's behaviour stays the same over six months. D: supports an argument that more human-supervised checking would add little over M working alone; it leaves open, for example, that M's verdicts were produced while M knew humans might check them, so a scheming M would behave well then and differently once review stops, and that the two changed verdicts may be exactly the ones that matter. Reading D also as evidence of M's accuracy (capability) is acceptable if the answer says what it leaves open about trust. For each item, the correct support plus any one concrete gap with its reason earns full credit; more gaps are headroom. A generic caveat ("an AI could be deceptive", "more testing is needed") that does not say why this particular evidence would look the same if M were untrustworthy earns at most a third of that item. (3) about 25 points: at least one sound strengthening move that addresses a gap the answer identified; a bare move (for example "keep spot checks") that does not say what it would catch or establish earns at most 15. Examples: evidence that the red-team exercise was at least as strong as what M could find, with checks that M is not holding back; a reason to expect M's safety to persist over the full period, such as ongoing checks that would raise an alarm; evidence that more human-supervised checking would add little over what the arrangement already provides; an argument that M has incentives to behave as safely as the humans would; or a staged handoff that keeps some human spot checks. Do not require any author's name, the labels of the four arguments, or terms such as "capability condition" or "alignment faking". Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues that comparing with human staff is the wrong standard, for example because the stakes call for more than human-level trust, can score well if they state the comparison, apply it, and give reasons for their disagreement. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner judged M in absolute terms, point them to the alternative of keeping humans in the loop. If they took C as settling trust, ask what a model hiding misalignment would do in the same tests. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
