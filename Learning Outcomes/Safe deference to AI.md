## Test:
id:: 3b3bc080-5c4c-467b-aeef-42bdf64cd43b

#### Question: Open
id:: a2da1d97-ce98-4c2f-8af0-0205cc46b0af
content::
An AI developer proposes that, for the next six months, a team of its AI agents M will run the company's dangerous-capability evaluations on new models end to end: designing the tests, running them and writing the verdicts. Human staff would stop reviewing individual results and would read only a quarterly summary. The proposal offers four pieces of evidence:

- A. For three months, M and the human evaluation team worked on the same models. M found more real problems, and its verdicts held up better when outside experts tried to break them.
- B. For two weeks, a human red team took control of several copies of M, instructing and fine-tuning them to hide a planted dangerous result from the cross-checks that the other copies of M run on each other's work. The cross-checks caught every attempt.
- C. In hundreds of interviews and scenario tests, M consistently says it wants the evaluations to be accurate and behaves that way, including in tests designed to tempt it.
- D. Over the past year, humans double-checked 400 of M's verdicts; the extra human review changed the outcome only twice.

1. Is this handoff justified on this evidence? Say what the decision should turn on, and over what period it has to hold.
2. For each of A, B, C and D, say what it supports and what it leaves unestablished, and why this evidence cannot settle that.
3. What further evidence or argument would most strengthen the case, and what would it catch or establish?
placeholder:: Answer all three parts
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Part 1, 27: 10: the decision should turn on whether handing off is safer than the realistic alternative of keeping humans in the loop, not on whether M is perfectly safe in absolute terms; 8: that comparison has two sides, capability (M at least as good at this task as the humans it replaces) and trust (M at least as trustworthy); 5: it has to hold for the whole six months of the handoff, not only at the start or during the periods the evidence covers; 4: a verdict that follows from the answer's own reading of the evidence. These are sufficient conditions, so a reasoned trade-off between them (for example a small capability shortfall made up by human spot checks) earns credit, and any verdict ('no', 'not yet', 'only if', or a 'yes' that says what B, C and D leave open and why the remaining risk is still below that of keeping humans in the loop) can score full marks if justified. Part 2, 48: 12 for each of A, B, C and D, 4: what it supports and 8: one concrete gap with its reason. A supports capability (M is at least as good as the humans at this task); gaps: it says little about whether M would sabotage the work once human review stops, and three months may not cover every part of the task or the full period. B supports a control argument, that copies of M could not get a corrupted verdict past the cross-checks; gaps: whether two weeks of a human red team finds strategies as good as what M's copies could devise over six months, whether the fine-tuned copies attacked with full effort, whether the checking copies, being M too, would still report honestly if M were misaligned (they could fail together or collude), or whether the checks cover every way a verdict could be corrupted. C is behavioural evidence that M is aligned; gap: it cannot rule out an M hiding misalignment, which would say and do the same in tests it can recognise, and it says little about whether M stays the same over six months. D supports an argument that more human-supervised checking would add little over M working alone (reading it also as evidence of M's accuracy is fine if the answer says what it leaves open about trust); gaps: M produced those verdicts knowing humans might check them, so a scheming M would behave well then and differently once review stops, and the two changed verdicts may be exactly the ones that matter. A generic caveat ('an AI could be deceptive', 'more testing is needed') that does not say why this particular evidence would look the same if M were untrustworthy earns at most 4 of that item's 12. Part 3, 25: at least one sound strengthening move that addresses a gap the answer identified, saying what it would catch or establish; a bare move (for example 'keep spot checks') without that earns at most 15. One sound move that says what it would catch or establish earns the full 25. Sound moves include evidence that the red-team exercise was at least as strong as what M could find, with checks that M is not holding back; a reason to expect M's safety to persist over the full period, such as ongoing checks that would raise an alarm; evidence that more human-supervised checking would add little over what the arrangement already provides; an argument that M has incentives to behave as safely as the humans would; or a staged handoff that keeps some human spot checks. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Caps on the total: 40 if the answer treats capability evidence alone as enough; 55 if it never judges M's trustworthiness, or the overall risk of the handoff, relative to the humans M would replace or to keeping humans in the loop (for example 'no AI can be trusted with this', 'M is safe', or 'the question is whether M can be trusted'), and noting only that M outperformed the humans in A does not count as that comparison; 55 if it treats C as establishing that M is trustworthy without saying why a model hiding misalignment would say and do the same in tests it can recognise. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues that comparing with human staff is the wrong standard, for example because the stakes call for more than human-level trust, can score well if they state the comparison, apply it, and give reasons for their disagreement. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Not yet, on this evidence. The question is not whether M is perfectly safe but whether handing the evaluations to M is safer than keeping human reviewers in the loop. That is clearly so if M is at least as capable as the humans it replaces at this task and at least as trustworthy, better on at least one, and stays that way for the whole six months. The capability side looks well supported; the trust side is not established, and dangerous-capability evaluations are exactly where a misaligned M would gain by quietly passing a dangerous model. 2. A supports capability: M found more real problems and its verdicts held up better. It says nothing about whether M would sabotage the work once humans stop reviewing, and three months of side-by-side work may not cover every kind of model or the full six months. B supports a control argument: copies of M could not get a corrupted verdict past the cross-checks. It leaves open whether two weeks of a human red team found attacks as good as what M's own copies could devise over six months, whether the fine-tuned copies really tried their hardest, and above all whether the checking copies, being M too, would still report honestly if M were misaligned, since copies of one model could fail together or collude. C is behavioural evidence that M is aligned. It cannot rule out an M that is hiding misalignment, because such a model would say and do exactly the same in interviews and tests it can recognise as tests, and it says little about whether M will stay the same over six months. D supports an argument that more human-supervised checking adds little over M working alone, and also that M is accurate. But M produced those verdicts knowing humans might check them, so a scheming M would behave well then and differently once review stops; and the two changed verdicts may be exactly the ones that matter. 3. The most useful additions address trust for the whole period: a stronger control evaluation, where a red team at least as capable as M, with checks that it is not holding back, tries to slip corrupted verdicts past cross-checks run by models whose failures are not correlated with M's (for example different models, or human spot checks at random); ongoing checks through the six months that would raise an alarm if M's behaviour changed; evidence against alignment faking that does not rely on M's own behaviour in recognisable tests; incentives for M to behave as safely as the humans would; and a staged handoff that keeps some human spot checks on verdicts about the most dangerous models and widens M's autonomy only as that evidence accumulates."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner judged M in absolute terms, point them to the alternative of keeping humans in the loop. If they took C as settling trust, ask what a model hiding misalignment would do in the same tests. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences.
