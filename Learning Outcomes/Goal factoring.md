## Test:
id:: 858fdcae-a662-4a0c-9334-5419c835569b
#### Question: Open
id:: 06cbf76d-7440-43f8-be72-2f7080508cf2
content::
Mira works in software and wants to move into AI safety. For a year she has gone to a weekly alignment paper-reading group on Thursday evenings: two hours, plus an hour of travel. Lately she dreads it. She is tired on Thursdays and often hasn't finished the paper.

She is weighing "keep going" against "quit", and her list of pros and cons comes out even. A friend suggested she read papers at home on Sunday mornings instead, when she has energy. Mira said "that's not the same thing", but couldn't say why. She also mentioned that she is the only person in the group without a research job, and that two members helped her with her last application.

Mira asks you to help her decide. Using goal factoring, write out what you would work out with her and the plan you would suggest she test.
force-feedback:: first
assessment-instructions:: The case: Mira goes to a weekly alignment paper-reading group (Thursday evenings, 2 hours plus 1 hour travel). She now dreads it, is tired on Thursdays and often hasn't finished the paper. She frames the choice as keep going or quit, and her pros and cons are even. When a friend suggested reading alone at home on Sunday mornings instead, she said "that's not the same thing" but couldn't say why. She is the only member without a research job, and two members helped with her last application. The learner is asked to apply goal factoring and suggest a plan to test.

Goal factoring means: take the action apart into the goals it serves (including social and self-image goals), check the list for missing goals (hesitation, or a "button test": would you press a button that gives you all these goals without the action?), brainstorm ways to get each goal, combine them into a plan, and reality-check it, while staying open to the original action being the best answer.

Score out of 100.
- 15: Steps outside the keep-or-quit frame. The answer treats the task as finding a plan that gets Mira what the group gives her, rather than weighing the two options. Keeping the group, possibly in a changed form, is allowed as an outcome.
- 35: Lists goals the group serves. Full 35: a learning goal (understanding papers, discussing them, keeping up with the field) plus at least two social or self-image goals that the case supports, such as belonging among people in the field, contacts who help her career and applications, feeling or being seen as a serious member of the field, or accountability to read. 20: the learning goal plus one such goal. 5: only learning goals.
- 20: Treats her "that's not the same thing" reaction, or a button test, as a sign of a goal not yet on the list, and says how to find it, such as asking what reading alone would lose, or imagining a button that gives her the listed goals. 10 if the answer runs a completeness check but does not connect it to her reaction or to missing goals.
- 20: A replacement plan built from the goals: specific changes aimed at specific goals, combined into one plan that costs her less (for example reading on Sunday and joining only the discussion, a fortnightly or online version, regular coffee with the two members who helped her). 10 if it suggests alternatives without linking them to the goals.
- 10: A reality check on the plan: imagining it running, asking what would stop her following it, or treating it as a test for a few weeks.

Cap the score at 40 if the answer mainly weighs pros and cons and recommends keep or quit without factoring the goals.
feedback-instructions:: The learner just applied goal factoring to Mira's reading-group decision in a graded test. You see the score and the grader's reason.

Reply in 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists. Name the strongest part of the answer. Below 100, name the most important thing the answer missed, most often a social or self-image goal that the case points to (belonging, the two members who helped her, being part of the field) or not using "that's not the same thing" as a clue to a missing goal. If the answer only weighed pros and cons, say plainly that goal factoring asks what the action is for before choosing. No generic praise.
