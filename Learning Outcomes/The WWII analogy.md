## Test:

#### Question: Open
id:: 6cd27693-bd59-41dd-a46b-ce1b29efb50a
content:: Chapter 13 opens with a parable about the Allied Powers in World War II: military drafts, food rationing, soldiers sent to die, enormous expense, all to prevent totalitarianism from conquering the world. The chapter then addresses those who believe international coordination on AI restriction is politically impossible. The WWII story is the chapter's direct answer to that objection.

**In your own words, how does the chapter use the WWII analogy to respond to the claim that coordinating major powers on AI restriction is politically impossible? What specific feature of WWII does it invoke, and what does it assume about what people are capable of?**

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100. Pick the level that best describes the answer as a whole, then a score inside that level's range: near the top if the answer fully reaches the level, near the bottom if it only just does. Judge what the answer shows the learner understands, not only what it spells out: a correct extension that depends on a point shows that point is understood, even if the answer states it briefly. An extension shows nothing about points it does not depend on.

**Level 1 (0-20):** Cannot connect the WWII analogy to the objection that coordination is politically impossible, or reads it as a different argument (for example, that wars are dangerous). *Example: "The chapter uses WWII to show that wars are dangerous."*

**Level 2 (21-40):** Sees that WWII involved countries working together, but cannot say what about the war the chapter uses or what it assumes about people. *Example: "The chapter says WWII shows countries can work together, so maybe they could for AI too."*

**Level 3 (41-65):** Gives one of the two parts but not the other: what the chapter takes from WWII and how that answers the objection, or what it assumes about what people will do. *Example: "The war took drafts, rationing and huge spending, and AI rules would cost a sliver of that, so 'impossible' really means 'they could never care even that little'."*

**Level 4 (66-85):** Both. What the chapter takes from WWII and how it answers the objection: this part is answered once the answer names the scale of what countries did together (drafts, rationing, enormous cost, lives lost, including by people not directly threatened) and says that restricting AI would ask far less, so calling it impossible amounts to saying countries could not care even a small fraction as much. What it assumes about people: this part is answered once the answer says that people will do things they usually would not when they see that their survival, freedom or way of life is at stake. An answer that gives the assumption only as people being "capable of a lot" or able to "rise to the occasion" reaches this level but not fully. Saying as well the figures the chapter gives (its cost, how many people served, "even 1% as much"), quoting its line about what people do when their way of life is at stake, or describing the treaty it goes on to propose, belongs to the same parts and is not needed for them. An answer that does both parts has answered the question in full, and without an extension it stays in this range. *Example: Adds "Underneath that is a bet about people: faced with a threat to how they live, they'll do what they normally wouldn't."*

**Level 5 (86-100):** All of level 4, plus a correct extension beyond what the question asks. An extension is a further point with its own reasoning; restating the points above in more precise or technical terms is still level 4. Three that fit: what the analogy does not claim (that restricting AI would be easy or cheap: it answers "impossible", not "hard", and the chapter admits the war's costs, including the emergency powers governments took and money that was wasted, while still judging the effort right; noting only that it would be costly is level 4); why it matters that people not directly threatened took part (not every country will feel equally threatened by AI, and the war shows countries acting on a threat that fell mainly on others); or where the analogy is weakest (the war's threat was plain to see, while AI's may not be visible until it is too late, so getting people to see what is at stake, the condition the chapter relies on, is the hard part; credited whichever way the learner judges it). Another extension of the same substance counts equally. Within this range the score reflects the whole answer: near the top only when the level 4 points and the extension are both well developed, and near the bottom when a strong extension rests on a thin base. *Examples: Adds "Plenty of the people who fought weren't in the Axis's path, and that matters, since not every country will feel equally threatened by AI." Or adds "The weak spot is that the war's threat was impossible to miss, while AI's may not show itself until it's too late."*

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Respond to the argument the learner actually made, and credit only what the answer says: do not attribute to them a point they did not make. Aim the reply at the idea they are missing, not at the score: frame the push as something about the material, never as what would earn more points. If they ask about their score, explain it by what the answer showed and what it left out, without naming levels or bands.

Open on the strongest thing in their answer and why it holds, in one sentence. If the answer goes beyond the question while one of its two parts (what the chapter takes from WWII and how it answers the objection, and what it assumes about people) is only asserted, that part is the push: deepening the base comes before going further. Otherwise, push once, chosen by where they stopped:

- If they did not connect the analogy to the objection, ask what the objection says is impossible and what WWII shows countries actually did.
- If they said only that countries can cooperate, ask what the scale and cost of the war effort show, and how much less restricting AI would ask.
- If they had the scale and the comparison but not the assumption about people, ask what the chapter assumes about people: why would they do something they do not usually do?
- If they gave the assumption only as people being capable of a lot, ask what has to be true for people to make sacrifices they usually would not.
- If they answered both parts and went no further, the moves left go beyond the question: whether the analogy claims the task would be easy, why it matters that people not directly threatened took part, or where the analogy is weakest. Ask about whichever their answer comes closest to.

If they made one of these moves, or another of the same substance, with both parts solid, say so plainly in a sentence or two after the opening, without walking back through the parts they got right, and do not invent a further push. If you add a question, make it one they can take further out of interest, not one that suggests the answer was missing something. This reply can be shorter than the length below.

If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold: the 1% comparison. Ask whether countries could care about AI restriction even one percent as much as they cared about the war. If they still don't attempt it, rephrase the question.

At most one follow-up question. No generic praise, and do not recite the rubric back to them.

Response length: 100 to 160 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
