## Test:
id:: f9c4b820-5a71-4d83-9e12-7b3f0c5d8a91
#### Question
content:: Someone objects to any proposal for an international agreement in which the major powers all stop developing the most dangerous kinds of AI: "That is politically impossible. No government would accept those constraints, and rivals would never trust each other enough to try."

A common reply points to the Second World War. To stop the Axis powers from conquering the world, the Allied nations conscripted millions of soldiers, rationed food at home, sent enormous numbers of people to their deaths, and poured a vast share of their economies into the fight — including nations and individuals who were not themselves under immediate threat.

**In your own words, how does that historical precedent work as a reply to the claim that coordinating major powers on AI restriction is politically impossible? What feature of the wartime mobilization does the reply depend on, and what does it assume about what people are capable of?**

assessment-instructions::
Score according to the following rubric.
Grade the student's reasoning, not their wording, and do not require any specific figures, phrases, or quotations.

**1** — Cannot connect the wartime precedent to the political-impossibility claim, or substitutes a different argument. *Example: "It shows that wars are dangerous."*

**2** — Recognizes that the war involved large-scale international cooperation but cannot say which feature of it does the argumentative work, or what the reply assumes about human motivation. *Example: "It shows countries can work together, so maybe they could for AI too."*

**3** — Conveys both parts of the reply, in any wording: (1) the load-bearing feature is the sheer scale of the sacrifice — a vast share of national wealth and manpower, borne even by people not personally under immediate threat — not merely that cooperation occurred; and (2) the reply reframes "countries could never restrict AI" as the much stronger claim that countries could not come to care even a small fraction as much as they demonstrably once did, and treats that stronger claim as implausible. Quantitative details (e.g. on the order of trillions of dollars, tens of millions of personnel, "not even 1% as much") are acceptable illustrations but are not required; an answer that conveys the magnitude and the reframing in its own terms passes. *Example: "The point isn't just that allies cooperated — it's how much they gave up, including people whose own lives weren't immediately at risk. So anyone saying countries will never coordinate on AI is really saying they could never care even a tiny fraction as much as they once did, and history says otherwise."*

**4** — As above, plus articulates the motivational claim the reply rests on: that people sometimes do things they otherwise would not when they recognize their survival or way of life is at stake — making this a basis for expecting coordination is possible rather than mere optimism. *Example: Adds "The assumption is that people act out of character once they see their freedom or way of life is genuinely threatened. That turns the impossibility objection from a claim about facts into a claim about motivation, and the war is the counterexample."*

**5** — As above, plus explains the argumentative function precisely: the precedent converts "this cannot happen" into "this requires people to care enough," then answers that with a case where they did — without denying that the proposed action would be hard or costly. *Example: Adds "The analogy doesn't claim coordination would be easy or cheap. It rebuts only the inference from 'very difficult' to 'impossible.'"*
