## Test:
id:: bd7cfcb1-a1c7-47b1-9429-81a62a952a23
#### Question: Open
id:: 37c2bb40-61c3-4094-a7fe-b155498d7593
content:: For eight months, Lena has worked on one research agenda in AI alignment, mostly in her evenings. A well-known researcher has just published a post arguing that this agenda is a dead end. Lena has read this author's critiques of other agendas before, and liked them. This one she refuses to read: "It's not worth my time. He clearly doesn't understand the agenda." When a friend presses her, she gets irritated and changes the subject. A few weeks earlier she told the same friend she sometimes wonders whether she is cut out for research at all.

1. Which questions might Lena have put into one bucket? Write the hidden link as "if X, then Y".

2. The friend's plan is to tell her: "You're just biased. Read the post." Given the link you named, why might that backfire?

3. What would you suggest instead?
assessment-instructions:: The question describes Lena, who has spent eight months of evenings on one AI alignment research agenda. A well-known researcher has published a post arguing the agenda is a dead end. She has enjoyed this author's critiques of other agendas but refuses to read this one ("not worth my time", "he clearly doesn't understand the agenda") and gets irritated when a friend presses. Weeks earlier she said she sometimes wonders whether she is cut out for research. The question asks (1) which questions she may have put into one bucket, written as "if X, then Y", (2) given that link, why the friend's plan ("You're just biased. Read the post.") might backfire, and (3) what to suggest instead. In CFAR's Bucket Errors model, a hidden implication X implies Y ties several questions together, so evidence for X threatens to force an unwanted, larger conclusion Y. Flinching away from X then protects the person from a drastic, unjustified update. The flinch is not the error. The implication is.

Score out of 100.

35: Names a plausible hidden link from the narrow question to a bigger one about herself or her path. X is about the agenda (the critique is right, the agenda is a dead end). Y is a larger conclusion, for example: her eight months were wasted, she is not cut out for research, she should leave the field, or she has failed. Give 35 for a clear "if X, then Y" of this kind, 20 if the answer lists the bundled questions without stating the link, and 10 if Y is only "she was wrong about the agenda".

25: Explains why the friend's plan might backfire: given the link, taking in the critique would seem to force the painful conclusion, so her refusal is protecting her, and pushing the evidence harder or calling her biased is likely to make her defend more rather than look. Give 25 if the answer explains the protective role of the refusal, 10 if it only says she would get defensive without saying why.

40: Suggests a way to loosen or separate the link before (or while) she looks at the evidence. Examples: asking what would be bad about the critique being right, listing the questions separately (is the agenda promising? did she learn useful things? is she good at research? should she stay in the field?) with different evidence for each, checking how strongly a flawed agenda really implies she is not cut out for research, or noting that reading the critique would not force her to drop anything. Give 40 for a suggestion that addresses the link, 15 for a suggestion that only asks her to read the post with an open mind, or to wait, without addressing the link.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "She may be holding 'is this agenda promising?', 'were my eight months worth it?' and 'am I cut out for research?' in one bucket: if the agenda is a dead end, then I'm not cut out for research. Calling her biased misses that her refusal protects her from that conclusion, so she'll likely dig in. Instead the friend could ask what it would mean to her if the critique were right, and help her see these as separate questions. A dead-end agenda says little about whether she can do research, and the skills she built are hers either way."
feedback-instructions:: The learner has analysed Lena's refusal to read a critique of her research agenda with CFAR's Bucket Errors model. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong. If they treated the refusal itself as the error and recommended pushing the evidence harder, name that first: in this model the refusal protects her, and the error is the hidden link. If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold (for example: "What did Lena tell her friend a few weeks earlier, and how might it connect to this post?").
