id:: bcce139a-839d-493e-8052-7bc6108ede21
content::
Here are two criticisms of AI control research.

**Criticism 1.** "The first models capable enough to matter are unlikely to be scheming; call it 10 to 20 percent. Control spends a large share of safety researchers' time on that one possibility. The same people working on making models trustworthy in the first place would reduce more risk."

**Criticism 2.** "A scheming model that knows how it is monitored will not attack while the monitoring works. It will wait for the moments when monitoring lapses: an outage, a rushed release, a new tool the monitors do not cover yet. Control evaluations test the protocol as designed, not as it will actually be run, so the safety they report will not be there when it matters."

For each criticism:
1. Say whether it disputes that control reduces the risk from scheming models, or accepts that and disputes whether control is worth doing, and on what grounds.
2. Say what it gets right.
3. Give the strongest response you can, and say how much of the criticism is left standing.
placeholder:: Take each criticism in turn: what it disputes, what it gets right, the strongest response
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 25: each criticism located correctly, 12 for criticism 1 (it accepts that control reduces the risk from scheming models and disputes whether it is worth the effort, because scheming is unlikely and the same researchers would reduce more risk elsewhere) and 13 for criticism 2 (it accepts the aim but disputes that control delivers the safety it reports in practice, because the protocol as actually run will lapse at the moments that matter); an argued view that criticism 2 also bears on whether control is worth doing counts. 20: a real concession to each criticism, 10 each, something it gets right that the answer does not then ignore, for example that control covers only the scheming part of the risk so its share of effort should track how much risk one expects from scheming, or that lapses happen, a patient schemer would pick its moment, and an evaluation of the protocol as designed can overstate the safety of the protocol as run. 45: the strongest response to each, 23 for criticism 1 and 22 for criticism 2, each resting on at least one specific mechanism, such as: a 10 to 20 percent chance of scheming with catastrophic, possibly irreversible outcomes is a large risk to leave uncovered; control can be evaluated without knowing whether the model schemes, so it protects in the worlds where alignment work quietly failed; catches produce evidence that can justify slowing down; what matters is the marginal researcher, so the answer depends on how neglected each area is (that control does not interfere with alignment work answers a claim about researchers' time only when tied to this marginal value); without control every moment is a lapse, and control forces the schemer to wait for rare gaps; gaps can be closed by security rules the lab keeps (no model runs unmonitored, new tools blocked until monitors cover them, systems fail closed during outages); evaluations can let the red team choose when to attack, including during planned lapses. 10: an honest statement of how much of each criticism is left standing, 5 each; a learner who concludes that a criticism is largely right gets full credit when the reasoning is sound. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; grade reasoning, not agreement. Cap at 55 if the answer locates neither criticism correctly, if it concedes nothing to either criticism, or if for neither criticism it compares what happens with control against what the same labs would face without it (for example no monitoring at all, no protection in the worlds where alignment work failed, what the same researchers would achieve on other work, or a risk left uncovered). Cap at 50 if the responses only assert that control is valuable without naming any mechanism. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Criticism 1 accepts that control works against schemers and disputes its value, on the grounds that scheming is unlikely and the same researchers would reduce more risk elsewhere. What it gets right: control addresses only the scheming part of the risk, and its share of effort should track how much of the risk one expects to come from scheming. Strongest response: a 10 to 20 percent chance that the first capable models are scheming, with a high chance of catastrophic and possibly irreversible outcomes if they are and nothing stops them, is a large risk to leave uncovered; work that makes models trustworthy is hard to verify, whereas control can be evaluated without knowing whether the model schemes, so it protects in exactly the worlds where that work quietly failed; catches produce evidence that updates the estimate and can justify slowing down; and the comparison that matters is the marginal researcher, so the answer depends on how neglected each area is. What is left: someone confident that scheming is rare and that alignment progress can be verified has a case for a smaller share of effort on control, not for none. Criticism 2 disputes that control delivers the safety it claims in practice: it accepts the aim but says the protocol as run will fail at the moments that matter. What it gets right: lapses happen, a patient schemer would pick its moment, and an evaluation of the protocol as designed can overstate the safety of the protocol as run. Strongest response: without control there is no monitoring to lapse, so every moment is a lapse; control forces the schemer to wait for rare gaps, which is itself a gain; the gaps can be treated as security rules the lab keeps, for example no model runs unmonitored, new tools are blocked until monitors cover them, and systems fail closed during outages; and evaluations can let the red team choose when to attack, including during planned lapses. What is left: if the lab does not keep those rules, the criticism holds, so control's value depends on security discipline as well as on protocol design."
