#### Question: Open
id:: 3438fa77-620f-498f-8080-460ef9d32421
content::
Lab K is a mid-sized AI developer. Its model, M, may be scheming. Facts about Lab K:

- M is about as capable as two models whose weights other developers have already released openly.
- Lab K's own datacenter is a small share of the compute that frontier developers use. Humans still run most of Lab K's research, with M assisting on tasks they assign and check.
- Most of Lab K's revenue comes from doing alignment research under contract for a larger frontier developer, which builds its safety measures on Lab K's results. M does much of the experimental work on these contracts.
- Lab K's leadership would pause for about six months if shown clear evidence that M is misaligned.

Rank the three threats that Lab K's control effort should address first, from these: M running unmonitored on Lab K's servers; M sabotaging the safety-relevant work it does; M copying its weights out of the lab; M manipulating or persuading Lab K's staff; M causing other harmful side effects, such as hacking outside systems. Justify the place of each threat you rank by what it would let M gain or do at Lab K and how likely M is to manage it, and say which facts about Lab K moved it. Say briefly why each threat you left out ranks lower. Whatever place you give M running unmonitored on Lab K's servers, say what it would give M. Then name one change to Lab K's situation that would move a different threat into first place, and explain why.
placeholder:: Your ranking with reasons, then the change that would reorder it
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is prioritising control threats by what each would give a misaligned model in a particular developer's situation. There is no single correct ranking; grade the reasoning. Reference answer. For Lab K, sabotage of safety-relevant work is plausibly the top threat: M does much of the experimental work on alignment research that a frontier developer builds its safety measures on, so sabotage there directly weakens other risk reduction, and it may need no security breach at all. M running unmonitored on Lab K's servers is still serious, because it would give M privileged access to tamper with the same research and with Lab K's experiments and training runs, but it is less severe and less plausible than at a frontier developer: the compute it would gain is small, and humans who still run the research and check M's work have more context to notice unexplained compute use. Self-exfiltration is lower than usual: comparable models are already openly available, so an escaped copy adds little capability beyond what those models already provide, and it would have little compute by default, although, unlike them, it is itself misaligned, and it could still work against a pause, which Lab K would take. Manipulation of staff can reasonably rank third, given that humans make the research decisions and a six-month pause depends on their judgement; other side effects are probably lowest because basic monitoring catches overt hacking. Changes that would reorder the list, for example: if AI came to run most of Lab K's research and its compute grew, an unmonitored internal deployment could use a large share of that compute and tamper with experiments and training runs with little human notice, so it would go first; if M became well ahead of any openly available model and a long pause were likely, self-exfiltration would rise, since an escaped copy would add real capability and undermine the pause; if Lab K stopped doing research others build on, sabotage would fall. Accept other rankings when argued from these considerations, for example putting unmonitored internal running first for Lab K because it is the route by which M could sabotage everything at once, or putting self-exfiltration higher because a pause would be undermined. Load-bearing elements. (1) About 35 points: the ranking is argued from what each threat would give M, such as compute, privileged access to the lab's work and to the research others rely on, or the ability to undermine a pause, and from how feasible each is for M at Lab K. (2) About 35 points: at least two facts about Lab K are used to change a threat's value, with the mechanism stated, for example comparable open models lowering the value of exfiltration, small compute and human-run research lowering the severity or plausibility of an unmonitored internal deployment, the contract research raising the stakes of sabotage, or the willingness to pause bearing on exfiltration or manipulation. (3) About 15 points: an explicit ranking of three that is consistent with the reasons given. (4) About 15 points: one change to Lab K's situation, with the threat that would move into first place and why, argued from what that change gives M or makes feasible. Gating: an answer that ranks by how bad each outcome sounds, without saying what M would gain or be able to do, or how likely it is to manage it, at Lab K, cannot score above 50; an answer that does not use at least two facts about Lab K to change a threat's value or feasibility cannot score above 55; an answer that gives no explicit ranking of three threats, or whose order contradicts its own reasons, cannot score above 50; an answer that never says, whether in ranking it or in explaining why it ranks lower, what M running unmonitored on Lab K's servers would gain from compute or from permissions to tamper with Lab K's work cannot score above 55. Do not require any term such as "rogue internal deployment", "security invariant" or "concentrated failure", or any author's name. Do not penalise a learner who questions the list of threats or merges two of them, if the ranking is still argued. Grade reasoning, not agreement. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner ranked by how bad each outcome sounds, ask what M would actually gain in each case at Lab K. If they ignored a fact such as the openly available models or the contract research, point to it. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
