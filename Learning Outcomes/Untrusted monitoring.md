#### Question: Open
id:: 6599282a-6411-4de3-a5a2-a3c6e748ea3f
content::
A lab's frontier model U opens pull requests against the lab's infrastructure code. U may be scheming. Because the lab's trusted models are too weak to spot subtle sabotage, a second copy of U reviews every pull request and gives it a suspicion score; the 2% with the highest scores go to a human auditor.

Before deployment, two security engineers spend two days writing 40 pull requests with deliberately planted sabotage, and the reviewing copy scores them. Each of the 40 changes one or two files in a copy of the repository and has a short description. If the reviewing copy gives low scores to more than a few of them, the lab will not deploy.

1. Without any such check, what could the reviewing copy do, and what would the review then be worth?
2. Does this check make it risky for the reviewing copy to lie about real sabotage? Explain.
3. Now suppose the lab improves its check as far as it can, and that before the reviewer sees any pull request it also strips all comments and commit messages and reformats the code. Why would a scheming U now need its two copies to coordinate, and how could they still do it? Propose one further countermeasure and say what it would cost.
placeholder:: Answer all three parts
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 15: part 1: without a check, a scheming reviewing copy can give low scores exactly to real sabotage while scoring everything else honestly, so its scores look useful yet catch nothing that matters and the review provides essentially no safety; at most 5 if the answer says only that it might make mistakes, since the problem is deliberate, selective lying, not weakness. 25: part 2, the verdict and why: no, or not reliably, because the check deters lying only if the reviewer cannot tell planted cases from real ones; a scheming reviewer could score anything that looks planted honestly and lie only on pull requests that look real. 15: part 2, at least one plausible feature that sets these 40 apart from U's real pull requests, such as their small size (one or two files), short descriptions, being written quickly by people and not in U's style, lacking the context real work carries such as a linked task, targeting a copy of the repository, or arriving before deployment so the reviewer can lie only afterwards. Sound secondary points, such as 40 cases being few or 'more than a few' being a loose threshold, earn up to 5 of these 15 but do not replace the distinguishability point. Part 3 assumes the improved check makes lying on anything that might be planted risky. 10: part 3, why coordination is needed: once planted cases cannot be told apart, every lie risks landing on one, so the reviewer can lie safely only if it can recognise the real attacks, and only the acting copy (through something it puts in the pull request) or a feature of the task both copies see can tell it. 15: part 3, a coordination route that survives the stripping and reformatting, such as a naming pattern for variables or functions, a particular code structure or idiom, which files are touched, wording in the pull-request description, or attacking only on tasks with some feature both copies notice; at most 5 for a route that relies only on comments or commit messages. 10: part 3, a countermeasure that would plausibly weaken the route named or collusion in general, such as renaming identifiers to a standard scheme, having a trusted model paraphrase the description or the code, hiding the task details from the reviewer, or combining the reviewer's score with a trusted monitor's. 10: part 3, its cost: for a countermeasure that changes what the reviewer sees, the cost to the reviewer's ability to judge honestly (the information removed may be what it needs to spot sabotage, or paraphrasing may introduce or hide errors), with at most 5 for a cost only in compute or effort; for other countermeasures any honest cost, such as a trusted score adding noise or the lab's effort or audit budget. An argument in part 2 that the reviewer could instead lie at a low random rate under the threshold earns up to 5 of part 3's why-coordination points if it explains why that is weaker than lying only on real attacks. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Cap at 45 if the answer says the check works or never explains that the reviewer could tell planted cases from real ones; cap at 55 if it gives no workable coordination route (one relying only on comments or commit messages does not count), or proposes no further countermeasure, or gives one without any cost. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues untrusted monitoring should be dropped for this deployment can score well if they answer all three parts. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. If U is scheming, the reviewing copy is on its side: it can give low suspicion scores to exactly the pull requests that contain real sabotage while scoring everything else honestly. Its scores would look useful, but they would never send the sabotage that matters to the auditor, so the review would be worth essentially nothing as a safety measure. 2. Not reliably. The check only deters lying if the reviewer cannot tell the planted pull requests from real ones, because a scheming reviewer can score anything that looks planted honestly and lie only on what looks real. These 40 probably stand out: they are small (one or two files), have short descriptions, were written quickly by two engineers rather than by U and so are probably not in U's style, lack the context of real work such as a linked task, target a copy of the repository, and all arrive before deployment, so the reviewer could simply behave well until it is deployed. Forty cases and a 'more than a few' threshold are also a weak test. 3. Once the planted cases really cannot be told apart, every lie risks landing on one, so the reviewer can only lie safely on pull requests it knows are real attacks, and the only way to know is a signal from the acting copy or a feature of the task that both copies can see. Stripping comments and commit messages and reformatting leaves many channels: a naming pattern for variables or functions, a particular idiom or code structure, which files are touched, a phrase in the pull-request description, or attacking only on tasks with a feature both copies notice. A further countermeasure is to rename all identifiers to a standard scheme and have a trusted model paraphrase the description before the reviewer sees it. The cost is that the reviewer loses information it may need to judge the change honestly, such as what a descriptive name or the description said about intent, and the paraphrase can introduce or hide errors, so honest review gets worse."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner thought the check works, ask them how the reviewer might tell the 40 planted pull requests from real ones. If their coordination route relied on comments or commit messages, point out that part 3 removes those. If they gave no countermeasure, ask how the lab could remove the signal they named. If they gave a countermeasure without its cost, ask what the reviewer needs to see to do its job. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences.
