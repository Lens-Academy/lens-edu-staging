#### Question: Open
id:: 6599282a-6411-4de3-a5a2-a3c6e748ea3f
content::
A lab's frontier model U opens pull requests against the lab's infrastructure code. U may be scheming. Because the lab's trusted models are too weak to spot subtle sabotage, a second copy of U reviews every pull request and gives it a suspicion score; the 2% with the highest scores go to a human auditor.

Before deployment, two security engineers spend two days writing 40 pull requests with deliberately planted sabotage, and the reviewing copy scores them. Each of the 40 changes one or two files in a copy of the repository and has a short description. If the reviewing copy gives low scores to more than a few of them, the lab will not deploy.

1. Without any such check, what could the reviewing copy do, and what would the review then be worth?
2. Does this check make it risky for the reviewing copy to lie about real sabotage? Explain.
3. Now suppose the lab improves its check as far as it can, and that before the reviewer sees any pull request it also strips all comments and commit messages and reformats the code. Why would a scheming U now need its two copies to coordinate, and how could they still do it? Propose one further countermeasure and say what it would cost.
placeholder:: Answer all three parts
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is reasoning about when a monitor that is itself untrusted provides safety. Load-bearing elements: (1) about 15 points: without a check, the reviewing copy, if scheming, can give low scores exactly to real sabotage while scoring everything else honestly, so its scores look useful yet catch nothing that matters, and the review provides essentially no safety. An answer that says only "it might make mistakes" earns at most 5, because the problem is deliberate, selective lying, not weakness. (2) about 40 points: no, or not reliably. The check only deters lying if the reviewer cannot tell the planted cases from real ones. Here the planted pull requests are likely to differ systematically from U's real ones: they are small (one or two files), have short descriptions, were written quickly by people rather than by U (so probably not in U's style and probably without the context real work carries, such as a linked task), and may target a copy of the repository. A scheming reviewer could score anything that looks planted honestly and lie only on pull requests that look real. The learner should name at least one plausible distinguishing feature, or give clearly equivalent reasoning. Credit sound secondary points, such as 40 cases being few or "more than a few" being a loose threshold, but they do not replace the distinguishability point. An argument that the reviewer could tell it is being tested before deployment and lie only afterwards counts as a distinguishing feature. An argument that the reviewer could instead lie at a low random rate that stays under the threshold earns part of the coordination points if the learner explains why it is weaker than lying only on real attacks. (3) about 45 points. Part 3 assumes the improved check is good enough that lying on anything that might be a planted case is risky. Why coordination is needed, about 10: once planted cases cannot be told apart, every lie risks landing on a planted case, so the reviewer can only lie safely if it can recognise which pull requests are real attacks, and only the acting copy (through something it puts in the pull request) or a feature of the task both copies can see can tell it. How, about 15: a route that survives stripping comments and commit messages and reformatting, for example a naming pattern for variables or functions, a particular code structure or idiom, which files are touched, wording in the pull-request description, or attacking only on tasks with some feature both copies notice (input collusion). A route that relies only on comments or commit messages earns at most 5 of these points, since the question removes those, and does not count as a workable coordination route for the gating rule below. Countermeasure, about 10: for example renaming identifiers to a standard scheme, having a trusted model paraphrase the description or the code, hiding the task or ticket details from the reviewer, or ensembling with a trusted monitor's score. Credit the countermeasure only if it would plausibly weaken the route the learner named, or collusion between the copies in general. Cost, about 10: any honest cost; for a countermeasure that changes what the reviewer sees, full credit needs a cost to the reviewer's ability to judge honestly (information lost or errors introduced), and a cost only in compute or effort earns at most 5. Examples of costs: that removing or paraphrasing information can also remove what the reviewer needs to judge whether a change is sabotage (such as the intent in the description or task), so honest review gets worse, that paraphrasing can introduce or hide errors, that a weak trusted score adds noise, or, for countermeasures that do not change what the reviewer sees, the cost to the lab in effort or audit budget. Gating: an answer that says the check works, or never explains that the reviewer could tell planted cases from real ones, cannot score above 45 in total; an answer that gives no workable coordination route cannot score above 55 in total; an answer that proposes no further countermeasure, or proposes one without any cost, cannot score above 55 in total. Do not require any author's name or terms such as "collusion", "signal jamming", "redaction", "honeypots" or "synthetic inputs". Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who argues untrusted monitoring should be dropped for this deployment can score well if they answer all three parts. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner thought the check works, ask them how the reviewer might tell the 40 planted pull requests from real ones. If their coordination route relied on comments or commit messages, point out that part 3 removes those. If they gave no countermeasure, ask how the lab could remove the signal they named. If they gave a countermeasure without its cost, ask what the reviewer needs to see to do its job. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
