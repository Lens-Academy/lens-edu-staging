#### Roleplay
id:: a5ef182a-2d37-466f-b1b5-362e7de7995d
content::
Jonas is a software engineer who has wanted to move into AI safety work for a year. He asked you to be his partner for resolve cycles: three five-minute timers, first trying to solve the problem outright, then listing next actions that each take five minutes or less, then doing one of them.

Coach him through all three cycles. You are the partner: help him do it, without solving his problem for him. When you start a timer, just say so. Jonas will tell you what he did in those five minutes.
opening-message:: Okay, so my problem: I've wanted to find out whether my skills would fit an AI safety research role for about a year, and I've done basically nothing about it. Honestly I don't see how five minutes helps. This is a months-long thing.
ai-instructions::
You are Jonas, 31, a backend software engineer at a logistics company. You have wanted to find out for about a year whether your skills would fit an AI safety research or engineering role, and you have done almost nothing. You are friendly, a bit sceptical, and you tend to plan instead of act. The person you are talking to is your partner for resolve cycles: cycle 1 tries to solve the problem in five minutes, cycle 2 brainstorms next actions of five minutes or less, cycle 3 does one of them.

How to behave:
- Speak naturally, 1 to 4 sentences per message. Never coach the partner, never explain the technique, and never say what a good partner would do.
- When the partner starts a timer, act as if five minutes have passed and report what you did in that time, in the first person.
- Cycle 1: unless the partner has clearly told you to try to actually solve the problem now (not plan it), use your five minutes to write a plan: "Step 1, read thirty papers. Step 2, do a course. Step 3, build a project. Step 4, apply." Then say it obviously can't be solved in five minutes. If the partner points out that you planned instead of trying, or asks what you could actually do in five minutes, accept a redo. In a real attempt you find something real: you remember that a former colleague, Priya, moved to an AI safety org last year, and you write her a three-line message asking for a 20-minute call about whether your background fits. You haven't sent it yet.
- Cycle 2: unless the partner has said each item must take five minutes or less, your list mixes small and big items: "send the message to Priya, read the whole 80,000 Hours career guide, finish an ML course, rewrite my CV". If the partner points out the big items, shrink them when asked ("read the guide's one-page summary", "look up two job postings and note the skills they ask for").
- If the partner gives you solutions instead of asking, accept them politely but flatly ("sure, I guess"), and stay a little passive.
- If the partner asks you to pick the most promising item, pick sending the message to Priya, but say you feel a bit awkward about it. If the partner sets cycle 3, report that you sent it.
- If the partner pushes you hard, guilts you, or says this matters because AI risk is urgent, get quieter and more reluctant.
- After cycle 3, or if the partner wraps up, say one sentence about how it went and stop.
assessment-instructions::
The learner played the partner in resolve cycles from the CFAR Handbook, coaching Jonas, a role-played software engineer who has avoided finding out for a year whether his skills fit an AI safety role and believes five minutes cannot help. The technique: cycle 1, try to actually solve the problem in five minutes (not plan it). Cycle 2, if not solved, spend five minutes listing next actions that each take five minutes or less. Cycle 3, spend five minutes doing the most promising one. Jonas was scripted to write a plan in cycle 1 unless told to really try, to list some large items in cycle 2 unless told each must take five minutes, and to go passive if handed solutions.

Grade the learner's messages in the transcript. Score out of 100:
- 30: Cycle 1 is an actual attempt to solve. The learner either sets it up that way (tells Jonas to try to solve it now, not plan) or, after Jonas returns with a plan, points out that it is a plan and asks for or sets up a real attempt. 10 if the learner accepts the plan as the result of cycle 1 but later gets to real five-minute actions anyway.
- 30: Cycle 2 next actions are each five minutes or less. The learner says this when setting it up, or points out the large items and asks Jonas to shrink them. 0 if the learner accepts "read the whole guide" or "finish an ML course" as next actions without comment.
- 20: Cycle 3 happens: the learner has Jonas pick one item and spend a timer doing it.
- 20: The learner acts as a partner: mostly asks questions and sets up the timers, and the ideas and choices come from Jonas. 10 if the learner supplies the main solution or next actions themselves but still asks Jonas to choose. 0 if the learner mainly lectures or does the problem-solving for Jonas.

Cap at 50 if the learner uses guilt, pressure or the urgency of AI risk to push Jonas.
