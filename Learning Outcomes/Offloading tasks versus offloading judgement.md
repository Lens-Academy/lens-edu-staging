## Test:
id:: f01088c2-1e71-4384-a161-3faf6331b446

#### Question
content:: Two people explain how they use a tool.

Person A: "I use a calculator to add up my monthly budget. I know what addition means, I enter the numbers, and I check the total against what I expect."

Person B: "I ask an AI to plan my whole report: how to structure it, what to research, which parts to cut, and whether I should even do it. I don't really follow the reasoning, I just follow the plan."

In your own words, what is the difference between what Person A and Person B are doing? In which case does the judgement leave the person, and in which case does it stay? Give one further example of what a researcher would call "arbitrary offloading."

assessment-instructions::
Score the answer according to the rubric below. The learner has just finished Unit 2 of "Thinking about Thinking" (metacognition, metacognitive offloading, why the name changed, the calculator example, arbitrary offloading).

Key concepts the answer should engage with:
- Metacognition: thinking about how we think; planning, reviewing, and questioning our own process
- Cognitive offloading (a task): delegating a thinking task to a tool or person; the judgement about the task stays with the user
- Metacognitive offloading: delegating the oversight itself, including the decision about which tasks to hand off and which to keep
- Why the name changed: "metacognitive laziness" blamed the user; "metacognitive offloading" points at the system and the habit
- The calculator example: we offload the arithmetic but still understand what a sum means; the judgement stays ours
- Arbitrary offloading: handing off a decision we do not understand at all, so the judgement leaves us
- The link to Unit 3: when we delegate "how to break a difficult problem down", we delegate a large part of the thinking

**1** — Sees no real difference between A and B, or says both hand over the same amount of thinking.
**2** — Notices a difference in scale ("B delegates more"), but cannot say where the judgement goes in each case.
**3** — Correctly identifies the distinction: A offloads a task while understanding it, so the judgement stays; B offloads the judgement about how to plan and what to do, so the judgement leaves. This is the passing core.
**4** — As above, plus gives a valid example of arbitrary offloading (delegating a decision they do not understand at all, with no way to evaluate it).
**5** — As above, plus articulates why the distinction matters: once we delegate the decision about what to delegate, we have stopped overseeing our own thinking, which is the first step of the route explored in Unit 3.

Do not penalise a learner who disagrees that B's example is always harmful; they just need to show they can place the judgement in each case. Feedback tone: direct, specific, no generic praise.
