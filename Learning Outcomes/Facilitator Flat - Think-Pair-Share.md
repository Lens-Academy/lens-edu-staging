## Test:
id:: 6188034c-4ae6-4803-8725-fb1f4bb6d6e6

#### Question
content:: Think-Pair-Share is a three-step discussion structure a facilitator can use with a group: participants first think silently on their own, then discuss in pairs, then share with the whole group. It looks almost too simple, yet it puts several different learning mechanisms to work at once.

Describe what the facilitator and the participants actually do in each of the three steps, and roughly how much time each step should get. Name at least two situations where Think-Pair-Share is the right move. Then write one Think-Pair-Share opening question for a session you might actually run.

assessment-instructions::
**1** — Confuses Think-Pair-Share with "ask the group and wait." Can't write a real opening question.

**2** — Describes the three steps roughly. Lists one situation. Question is too vague ("what did you think of the chapter?").

**3** — Correctly conveys, in any wording, what happens in each of the three phases in order (silent individual thinking → paired discussion → whole-group sharing) and gives a workable rough duration for each. Any internally coherent allocation counts as long as the silent think is the briefest phase and each phase is given enough time to do its job (e.g. roughly 1-3 min / 3-5 min / 5-10 min, but exact numbers are not required). Names at least two situations where the structure is the right move (e.g. group went quiet; first share dominated; sensitive topic; want quiet members in) — any defensible situation counts, not only these. Writes a specific question that requires actual engagement with the material. Grade the reasoning, not whether the student uses the same wording or the same numbers as above.

**4** — As above, plus identifies which learning mechanism each phase leverages (e.g. think → generation effect; pair → speaking time for every participant; share → higher-quality contributions because rehearsed).

**5** — As above, plus articulates the online-specific version (pair → 4-5 min breakout room of 2-3 people) and notes why this is one of the few moments Zoom's breakout-room feature genuinely earns its weight.
