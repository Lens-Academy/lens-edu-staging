## Test:
id:: 2dda11c1-08df-40d2-9b21-2fe5f693da36

#### Question: Open
id:: e8e6fab8-0655-406e-ba12-e8b2df7fa335
content:: A senior official tells you: "Every frontier lab CEO has said on the record that superintelligence could be catastrophic, and our intelligence assessments say the other side's leadership believes it too. If both sides already agree it is dangerous, they will stop. We do not need an elaborate verification regime. We need a signed agreement and the political will to keep it."

Write a reply. Your reply should:

1. explain why shared belief that ASI is dangerous does not by itself stop the race;
2. explain why each of trust, punishment after the fact, and mutual transparency fails to make a pause hold between rivals;
3. explain what verification changes about each side's incentives, and what it does not require the two sides to agree on.
max-chars:: 2500
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100 from three components. Grade the reasoning, not agreement with the official or with the course. A learner who argues that verification is insufficient but states the mechanisms in (a) and (c) correctly can score full marks.

**(a) Race logic, 40 points.** Full credit: the decision to pause is made under uncertainty about the rival's behaviour; if the rival might continue, pausing unilaterally means falling behind in a technology that confers decisive advantage, so continuing is the individually safer choice even for a state that would prefer a joint stop. A state that fears ASI also fears the other side getting it first. Naming a structure (prisoner's dilemma, security dilemma, collective action problem) earns nothing on its own; the mechanism must be stated. 20 points if the answer says "they do not trust each other" without connecting distrust to why continuing is the individually safer move. 0 if the answer attributes the race to disbelief in the risk or to bad actors only.

**(b) Three failed alternatives, 30 points, 10 each.** Trust: adversaries have strong incentives to gain a secret advantage and no way to distinguish a rival's compliance from its deception. Punishment: deterrence requires detecting the violation first, and for catastrophic, irreversible outcomes punishment after the fact does not restore what was lost. Transparency: full disclosure exposes secrets a rival can steal, and still lets a party fabricate compliance, so each side has reason to refuse it or fake it. Award each 10 only if the reason names why the option fails for rivals specifically, not just "it would not work".

**(c) What verification changes, 30 points.** Full credit: each side can check the other's compliance without learning its secrets, and knows the other can do the same, so the expected gain from secret defection drops and pausing stops being a unilateral gamble. Verification does not require the sides to trust each other, share political goals, or resolve their rivalry. 15 points if the answer describes verification only as "checking" without saying what it does to the incentive to defect. Deduct 10 if the answer claims verification must be perfect or catch every violation; the standard is making defection at a consequential scale a bad bet.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest step in the learner's argument in one sentence. Then name the one missing or weakest link, most often the connection between distrust and why continuing is the individually safer move, or what verification does to the incentive to defect. Ask one follow-up question. No praise beyond the first sentence.
