## Test:
id:: 2dda11c1-08df-40d2-9b21-2fe5f693da36

#### Question: Open
id:: e8e6fab8-0655-406e-ba12-e8b2df7fa335
content:: A senior official tells you: "Every frontier lab CEO has said on the record that superintelligence could be catastrophic, and our intelligence assessments say the other side's leadership believes it too. If both sides already agree it is dangerous, they will stop. We do not need an elaborate verification regime. We need a signed agreement and the political will to keep it."

Write a reply. Your reply should:

1. explain why shared belief that ASI is dangerous does not by itself stop the race;
2. explain why each of trust, punishment after the fact, and mutual transparency fails to make a pause hold between rivals;
3. explain what verification changes about each side's incentives, and what it does not require the two sides to agree on.
max-words:: 420
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not agreement with the official or with the course: a learner who argues that verification is insufficient but states the mechanisms of parts 1 and 3 correctly can score full marks. 40: part 1, why shared belief in the danger does not stop the race, 15: each side must decide whether to pause without knowing whether the rival will really stop; 25: if the rival might continue, pausing alone means falling behind in a technology that could give it a decisive (military, economic or strategic) advantage, and a state that fears ASI also fears the rival getting it first, so continuing is the individually safer choice even for a state that would prefer a joint stop. Naming a structure (prisoner's dilemma, security dilemma, collective action problem) counts only as far as the answer states this mechanism. Cap part 1 at 20 if the answer says only that the sides do not trust each other without connecting distrust to why continuing is the individually safer move; part 1 scores 0 if the answer attributes the race to disbelief in the risk or to bad actors only. 30: part 2, why each alternative fails between rivals, 10: trust: rivals are driven to protect themselves by gaining a secret upper hand, and neither can tell the other's compliance from deception, so a promise gives no assurance (either reason earns the 10); 10: punishment after the fact, 5: deterrence works only if violations are reliably detected first, and 5: when the harm is immediate, far-reaching and irreversible, no punishment undoes it; 10: mutual transparency, 5: opening up exposes secrets and prototypes a rival can steal, so each side has reason to refuse it, and 5: it still leaves each side the incentive and room to keep a secret advantage and fake compliance. Each reason earns its points only if it says why the option fails for rivals specifically; a different sound reason of that kind earns the same credit. 30: part 3, 20: what verification changes, 10: each side can check the other's compliance without learning its secrets, and knows the other can check it too, and 10: so a secret defection would likely be found, its expected gain drops, and pausing stops being a unilateral gamble and becomes individually rational; cap these 20 at 10 if the answer describes verification only as checking without saying what it does to the incentive to defect. 10: what it does not require: the sides do not need to trust each other, share political goals, or resolve their rivalry. Deduct 10 if the answer claims verification must be perfect or catch every violation; it only has to make defection at a consequential scale a bad bet. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Shared belief in the danger does not stop the race, because each side decides without knowing what the other will really do. If you pause and your rival does not, you fall behind in a technology that could give it a decisive military and economic advantage, and a state that fears ASI also fears the other side getting it first. So even a state that would prefer a joint stop finds that continuing is the individually safer choice, and both keep racing towards the outcome both fear. A signed agreement does not change this on its own. Trust fails because rivals each have strong reasons to protect themselves by gaining a secret upper hand, and neither can tell the other's compliance from deception, so a promise gives no assurance. Punishment after the fact fails because deterrence works only if violations are reliably detected in the first place, and because with ASI the harm may be immediate, far-reaching and irreversible: no sanction undoes it. Mutual transparency fails because opening the books exposes secrets and prototypes a rival can steal, so each side has reason to refuse it, and it still leaves each side free to keep a hidden programme and fake compliance. Verification changes the calculation: each side can check that the other is keeping the agreement without learning its secrets, and knows the other can check it too. A secret defection would then probably be found, so its expected gain falls, and pausing is no longer a unilateral gamble but a choice that is safe for each side on its own terms. Verification does not require the two sides to trust each other, share political goals or settle their rivalry; it moves trust from a volatile adversary to a shared, robust checking regime, as nuclear rivals did with inspections and monitoring. It does not have to catch every violation, only make defection on a scale that matters a bad bet. So political will matters, but it holds only if each side can see that the other is holding too."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest step in the learner's argument in one sentence. Then name the one missing or weakest link, most often the connection between distrust and why continuing is the individually safer move, or what verification does to the incentive to defect. Ask one follow-up question. No praise beyond the first sentence.
