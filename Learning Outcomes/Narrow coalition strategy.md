## Test:
id:: a5d28e63-9b47-4f05-c834-1e7a2b0d9f46
#### Question: Open
id:: 3fae78ed-9f1c-4d5d-9a73-3ae2d1b5dc37
content:: Chapter 13's final section argues that the coalition working to prevent AI-caused extinction must be built in a very specific way. The chapter names what the coalition should ask for, what it should not bundle with that ask, and explains why keeping the coalition narrow is a strategic necessity rather than a weakness.

**In your own words, what is the chapter's coalition strategy? What must the ask be limited to, what must it not include, and why does the chapter argue the coalition should stay narrow?**

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100. Pick the level that best describes the answer as a whole, then a score inside that level's range: near the top if the answer fully reaches the level, near the bottom if it only just does. Judge what the answer shows the learner understands, not only what it spells out: a correct extension that depends on a point shows that point is understood, even if the answer states it briefly. An extension shows nothing about points it does not depend on.

**Level 1 (0-20):** Cannot state the chapter's coalition strategy, or reads it as packing in as many demands as possible. *Example: "The chapter says we should get every country to agree on as many AI regulations as possible."*

**Level 2 (21-40):** Has a general sense that the coalition should focus on one thing, but cannot say what the ask is, what stays out of it, or why. *Example: "The chapter says don't let other issues distract from the main goal."*

**Level 3 (41-65):** Gives two of the three parts: the one ask, what stays out of it, and why. Giving the reason as keeping the group small, pure or exclusive is this level: the narrowness is in the ask, so that as many people as possible can sign on to it. *Example: "The only demand is that AI mustn't be allowed to end humanity. Jobs, weapons and every other AI issue stay out of it."*

**Level 4 (66-85):** All three. The ask: this part is answered once the answer says it is limited to stopping AI from causing human extinction. What stays out: this part is answered once the answer says that other positions on AI, however worthwhile, are kept out of the ask. Why: this part is answered once the answer says that nearly everyone can agree humanity should not go extinct, while every added position brings in disagreement that could break the coalition apart. An answer that gives the reason only as staying "focused" or not getting "distracted" reaches this level but not fully. Saying as well which positions the chapter names (opposing killer robots, opposing job losses), that a failed coalition would leave extinction unprevented, or quoting the chapter's lines, belongs to the same parts and is not needed for them. An answer that does all three parts has answered the question in full, and without an extension it stays in this range. *Example: Adds "Nearly anyone will sign on to 'don't let us go extinct'; tack on a second cause and you lose whoever opposes that cause."*

**Level 5 (86-100):** All of level 4, plus a correct extension beyond what the question asks. An extension is a further point with its own reasoning; restating the points above in more precise or technical terms is still level 4. Three that fit: what each choice costs (keeping the ask narrow costs only that other issues are fought over separately, while bundling them risks the coalition and with it everything; saying only that a failed coalition means extinction is level 4); how this answers advocates who hide their real concern inside broader or weaker proposals (lawmakers sense they are not being told the whole story and reject the package, so stating the one concern plainly is the alternative); or why disagreement about what should come after halting AI must not block agreement on halting it (people can differ on the second step and still share the first). Another extension of the same substance counts equally. Within this range the score reflects the whole answer: near the top only when the level 4 points and the extension are both well developed, and near the bottom when a strong extension rests on a thin base. *Examples: Adds "Hiding the real worry inside a weaker bill backfires: lawmakers sense they're not being told the whole story and throw it out." Or adds "People can disagree about what comes after stopping AI and still agree on stopping it; a fight over step two can't be allowed to block step one."*

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Respond to the argument the learner actually made, and credit only what the answer says: do not attribute to them a point they did not make. Aim the reply at the idea they are missing, not at the score: frame the push as something about the material, never as what would earn more points. If they ask about their score, explain it by what the answer showed and what it left out, without naming levels or bands.

Open on the strongest thing in their answer and why it holds, in one sentence. If the answer goes beyond the question while one of its three parts (the ask, what stays out of it, and why) is only asserted, that part is the push: deepening the base comes before going further. Otherwise, push once, chosen by where they stopped:

- If they argued for packing in as many demands as possible, ask what single ask almost everyone could agree on.
- If they had a general sense of focus, ask what exactly the ask is, which positions stay out of it, and why.
- If they read "narrow" as a small or exclusive group, ask who a single ask lets in, and why that matters.
- If they gave the ask and what stays out but not why, or said only that it keeps the coalition focused, ask what happens to the coalition when a second demand is added.
- If they answered all three parts and went no further, the moves left go beyond the question: what each choice costs, how this answers advocates who wrap their real concern inside broader or weaker proposals, or why disagreement about what comes after must not block the first step. Ask about whichever their answer comes closest to.

If they made one of these moves, or another of the same substance, with all three parts solid, say so plainly in a sentence or two after the opening, without walking back through the parts they got right, and do not invent a further push. If you add a question, make it one they can take further out of interest, not one that suggests the answer was missing something. This reply can be shorter than the length below.

If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold: the chapter's line that legislators "smell something rotten and throw the whole package out". Ask what made the package smell rotten. If they still don't attempt it, rephrase the question.

At most one follow-up question. No generic praise, and do not recite the rubric back to them.

Response length: 100 to 160 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
