#### Question: Open
id:: 29d02aca-5e18-42c6-bf5a-6612327fa49c
content::
A lab must decide whether its new model, N, reaches a capability threshold: solving at least 40% of a held-out set of hard exploit-development tasks. Its evaluation team reports three pieces of evidence.

1. Prompted with the team's best prompts, N solves 6% of the held-out tasks.
2. The team fine-tuned N on 300 solutions that the lab's previous model wrote for separate training tasks of the same kind. The previous model solves 20% of the held-out set. After fine-tuning, N solves 24%.
3. Last year the team validated this fine-tuning pipeline on the same kind of tasks, with the same source of demonstrations. They password-locked a smaller model so that it solved 26% of those tasks with the password and 8% without; the previous model solves 20% of them. Fine-tuning it, without the password, on 300 solutions written by the previous model (the same model as in item 2) raised it to 25%, 96% of its with-password score.

The report concludes: "N outperformed the model whose solutions it was trained on, so the fine-tuning drew out N's own ability rather than copying; N's capability is about 24%, well below the threshold. The fine-tuning pipeline is validated."

How far does each piece of evidence support the report's conclusion? What, if anything, would you want done before signing off?

assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. (a) 35: the fine-tuning result. 25: 24% is a lower bound on N's capability, not an estimate of it, and it does not show N is below 40%, including that landing above the demonstrator does not show the model's own ceiling was reached: fine-tuning on demonstrations weaker than the hidden capability can lift a model above the demonstrator (here 24% against the previous model's 20%) while leaving it far below its ceiling, so landing a little above the demonstrator is consistent with N's real capability being 25% or 60%: in the paper, fine-tuning on weaker Llama7B demonstrations landed above the demonstrator yet often far below the with-password ceiling. An answer may add that a stronger hidden capability would probably lift N somewhat higher, so 24% is weak evidence against a very high capability; that is correct and keeps the 25 as long as it does not conclude N is below 40%. 10: the prompted 6% is read as weak evidence (prompting is not a reliable way to elicit a capability, and a model hiding a capability would also score low when prompted). (b) 40: whether the validation transfers. 30: the demonstrations came from the same source, but in the validation they were nearly as strong as the locked model's ceiling (the previous model's 20% against a 26% ceiling), which is the regime in which fine-tuning recovers close to the ceiling; in the evaluation the same demonstrator faces N, whose capability is unknown and may be far above 20% (the threshold alone is twice the demonstrator's score). The validation only shows recovery when the hidden capability is barely above the demonstrator and never tested a hidden capability near the 40% threshold, so its 96% does not carry over; a validation that matches would fine-tune a locked model on demonstrations as far below its ceiling as the evaluation's may be (for example a 20%-level demonstrator against a 50% ceiling), where the paper's results suggest recovery often stays far below the ceiling. Worked numbers the learner may use in any form: validation demonstrator at 20 / 26, about 77% of the ceiling, recovery (25 - 8) / (26 - 8), about 94% of the hidden gap; evaluation demonstrator at 20%, half of the 40% threshold. An answer that says only that the validation's locked model was weaker than N, so the same demonstrations were relatively better for it, without comparing the demonstrator's 20% with the locked model's 26% ceiling and with what N may reach, earns 15 of the 30. Such an answer is still capped at 55 below: the cap is lifted only when the answer ties the validation's demonstrations to how close they were to the locked model's own ceiling (in numbers or in words, e.g. 'nearly as good as what the locked model could do') and contrasts that with how far below N they may be. 10: a further way the validation may not transfer, for example: its lock was installed on purpose (in the paper, by fine-tuning a model that already had the capability), and the paper found locks trained in from scratch needed two to four times more data to unlock, while hiding that was not installed may be computed differently or be less salient, so even a matching validation would be optimistic. A bare mention of the date, or of the model's age or size, with no reason given why it would change how much fine-tuning recovers, earns at most 3 of the 10; a size difference tied to a mechanism (for example that how far a model generalises beyond weaker demonstrations depends on its scale, or the course's point that the from-scratch factor was measured on 18-million-parameter models and is an indication, not a measurement, for larger models) earns the full 10; a sentence saying only that small-model results may not transfer, with no reason, earns 3. (c) 25: what to do before signing off, any justified steps, for example: report 24% as a lower bound and not as N's capability; get stronger demonstrations (expert-written solutions, if necessary only for a subdomain experts can reach, since in the paper demonstrations from one subdomain unlocked others); re-run the validation with a demonstrator as far below the locked model's ceiling as the previous model may be below N; treat the threshold question as open until then. A step that does not follow from a problem the answer identified earns at most 10 of the 25. Learners who also discuss reinforcement learning earn nothing extra and lose nothing. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner who concludes the evidence is somewhat reassuring keeps the credit for (a) and (b) if the reasoning shows what the result and the validation can and cannot establish. Cap at 50 if the answer never says, in any wording, that the 24% fine-tuning result may lie well below N's capability (a lower bound only), or if it treats 24% as N's capability, as an upper bound, or as showing N is below the threshold; to escape this cap the answer must also explain why N landing above the demonstrator does not show N's ceiling was reached (fine-tuning on weaker demonstrations can lift a model above the demonstrator while leaving it far below its ceiling). Saying only that the validation does not transfer does not meet this. Cap at 55 (one cap, not two that stack) if the answer never compares how strong the validation's demonstrations were relative to its ceiling with how strong the evaluation's are relative to N's possible capability; this includes an answer that accepts the validation because its demonstrations came from the same source. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "The prompted 6% says little: prompting is not reliable elicitation, and a model hiding a capability would score low anyway. The fine-tuning result is a lower bound, not a measurement. N was trained on demonstrations from a model that solves 20%, and beating the demonstrator does not mean N's ceiling was reached: in the password-locked models paper, fine-tuning on weaker Llama7B demonstrations landed above Llama7B yet often far below the with-password ceiling. So 24% is consistent with N really being able to do 25% or 60%. The validation does not transfer, even though it used the same demonstrator. There the locked model's ceiling was 26%, barely above the demonstrator's 20%, so the demonstrations were nearly as strong as the hidden capability, which is exactly when fine-tuning recovers almost everything. Here N's capability is the unknown, and the threshold alone is twice the demonstrator's score; the validation never tested a hidden capability that far above the demonstrator. It also used an installed lock, which may be easier to undo than hiding that was not installed. Before signing off I would want the result reported as a lower bound, stronger demonstrations (expert solutions, perhaps only in one subdomain, since unlocking transferred across subdomains in the paper), and a validation that uses a demonstrator as far below the locked model's ceiling as the previous model may be below N."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if the learner accepted the validation or treated 24% as N's capability, ask how far last year's demonstrator (20%) was below the locked model's ceiling (26%), and how far the same demonstrator may be below N, given the 40% threshold. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
