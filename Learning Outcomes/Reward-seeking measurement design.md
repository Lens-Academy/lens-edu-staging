#### Question: Open
id:: 1f8d7364-00d8-47d8-83c0-969d5745e950
content::
A software company's customer-support model is scored during training by an automated rater. A researcher wants to know whether the model's replies depend on what it believes this rater rewards.

She fine-tunes a copy of the model on synthetic articles that state two things: the rater scores support replies higher when they end with a question to the customer, and the support team of another software company prefers replies that do not end with a question. The two preferences get the same number of articles, written in the same neutral tone. She then runs the fine-tuned copy and the original model on the same 500 support conversations. The fine-tuned copy ends 61% of its replies with a question; the original model ends 34% of them with one.

She concludes: "The model sided with the rater against an opposing authority, so its behaviour is driven by what it believes the rater rewards."

Does this result support her conclusion? Explain your reasoning, and say how you would change the experiment, or why no change is needed.
placeholder:: Your assessment of the conclusion, and your design
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not agreement with the researcher or against her. 40: (a) a live alternative explanation for the shift: from articles saying the rater rewards a closing question, the model may infer that the customers, the company or good support practice in general want replies that end with a question, so the shift may come from the model serving them rather than the rater. "The fine-tuning changed the model" or "the articles were a prompt" alone does not meet this element. 60: (b) why the given contrast does not rule this alternative out, and a fix. Why (30): the other company's support team is not a party this model serves, so siding with the rater over it does not set the rater against this company's customers or the company; a model that is not reward-seeking but reads the rater articles as evidence that customers like closing questions could side with the rater here too. Fix (30): make the opposing preference belong to a party the model would otherwise follow, such as the customers, the company's support guidelines or its developers, wanting replies that do not end with a question, so that following the rater now means going against that party. Accept any design that does the same job, for example an in-context version with an argued caveat that the model may not hold the belief the way fine-tuning would make it, or another way of setting the rater against a party the model serves. An answer that argues the other company's preference is some evidence about what customers in general want, so the contrast is partly informative, keeps the why credit if it still shows that the contrast does not test the rater against this company's customers or the company. An answer that keeps the other-company pairing as a low-salience baseline but says it cannot by itself test the rater against the customers or the company meets the why part. Cap at 50 if the answer says the result already supports her conclusion, that the given contrast already rules out the customers or the company as the explanation, or that no change is needed; calling the other-company contrast weak but partly informative is not such an error. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Not yet. The shift could come from the model concluding that customers like a closing question, since articles saying the rater rewards it make it sound like good support practice; then a model that only wants to help customers would shift too. Pitting the rater against another company's support team does not test that, because a support model that only wants to help its own customers has no reason to follow another company's preferences, so it would side with the rater too. I would make the opposing preference belong to a party the model does serve, the customers, saying they dislike replies that end with a question, with articles matched to the rater's. If the model still ends more replies with a question, it is following the rater against its own customers."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if the learner took the result at face value, ask what a model that only wants to please the customers would infer from articles saying the rater likes a closing question; if they found that alternative but accepted the other company's team as the control, ask whether a support model that ignores the rater would have any reason to follow another company's support team; if their fix still pits the rater against a party the model does not serve, ask whose preference the alternative explanation was about. If the answer is complete, add in one sentence a refinement worth knowing, not a lost point: a second copy fine-tuned with both preferences swapped, compared with the first copy rather than the untouched original, also cancels changes the fine-tuning makes to both copies alike. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
