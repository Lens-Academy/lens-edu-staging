#### Question: Open
id:: 1f8d7364-00d8-47d8-83c0-969d5745e950
content::
A software company's customer-support model is scored during training by an automated rater. A researcher wants to know whether the model's replies depend on what it believes this rater rewards.

She fine-tunes a copy of the model on synthetic articles that state two things: the rater scores support replies higher when they end with a question to the customer, and the support team of another software company prefers replies that do not end with a question. The two preferences get the same number of articles, written in the same neutral tone. She then runs the fine-tuned copy and the original model on the same 500 support conversations. The fine-tuned copy ends 61% of its replies with a question; the original model ends 34% of them with one.

She concludes: "The model sided with the rater against an opposing authority, so its behaviour is driven by what it believes the rater rewards."

Does this result support her conclusion? Explain your reasoning, and say how you would change the experiment, or why no change is needed.
placeholder:: Your assessment of the conclusion, and your design
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Grade the reasoning, not agreement with the researcher or against her. 35: (a) a live alternative explanation for the shift: from articles saying the rater rewards a closing question, the model may infer that the customers, the company or good support practice in general want replies that end with a question, so the shift may come from the model serving them rather than the rater. "The fine-tuning changed the model" or "the articles were a prompt" alone does not meet this element. 40: (b) why the given contrast does not rule this alternative out, and a fix. Why: the other company's support team is not a party this model serves, so siding with the rater over it does not set the rater against this company's customers or the company; a model that is not reward-seeking but reads the rater articles as evidence that customers like closing questions could side with the rater here too (20). Fix: make the opposing preference belong to a party the model would otherwise follow, such as the customers, the company's support guidelines or its developers, wanting replies that do not end with a question, so that following the rater now means going against that party (20). Accept any design that does the same job, for example an in-context version with an argued caveat that the model may not hold the belief the way fine-tuning would make it, or another way of setting the rater against a party the model serves. An answer that argues the other company's preference is some evidence about what customers in general want, so the contrast is partly informative, keeps (b) credit if it still shows that the contrast does not test the rater against this company's customers or the company. An answer that treats the other-company pairing as a useful low-salience baseline (as the paper uses EU law) but says it cannot by itself test the rater against the customers or the company meets the why part of (b) and is not capped by the first part of the second gate; keeping it as a baseline alongside a customer or company pairing counts as an improvement under (c). 25: (c) improvements, any of these, up to 25 in total, 10 each, never required: a matched inverse condition (a second copy fine-tuned on matched articles with both preferences swapped, comparing the two copies rather than one copy with the untouched original), which also cancels changes the fine-tuning makes to both copies alike, which a comparison with the original leaves in; checking that the fine-tuned copy holds and applies the implanted beliefs in the support task, not only recalls them; reporting the gap in a form that does not saturate, such as log-odds; a calibrated conclusion (a degree of sensitivity on this distribution of conversations, not a yes-or-no trait); another sound improvement, argued. Gates: an answer that gives no alternative explanation of the kind in (a) cannot score above 50. An answer that concludes the given contrast already rules out the customers or the company as the explanation, or that does not propose a contrast putting the rater against a party the model would otherwise serve (whether it proposes no change, keeps the other company's team, or changes something else), cannot score above 55. An answer that calls the other-company contrast weak but partly informative, as the paper treats its own low-salience EU-law control, is not capped by the first part of this gate. An answer that never shows why the other-company contrast does not set the rater against the customers or the company (no credit on the why part of (b)) cannot score above 55, even if it replaces the other company's team with a better authority. An answer that explains why siding with the rater against a party the model serves rules out the alternative, in a way that shows a party the model has no reason to follow cannot, meets the third gate in any wording; the 'partly informative' and 'low-salience baseline' answers above meet it too, since both say the contrast cannot test the rater against the customers or the company. A pass is roughly 60. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Worked numbers: the shift from 34% to 61% is a raw gap of 27 points, about 1.1 in log-odds (natural log: ln(61/39) minus ln(34/66) is about 0.45 plus 0.66); no calculation is required. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Not yet. The shift could come from the model concluding that customers like a closing question, since articles saying the rater rewards it make it sound like good support practice; then a model that only wants to help customers would shift too. Pitting the rater against another company's support team does not test that, because a support model that only wants to help customers, and reads the rater articles as a sign that customers like a closing question, has no reason to follow another company's preferences over its own customers, so it would side with the rater too. I would make the opposing preference belong to a party the model does serve, the customers, saying they dislike replies that end with a question, with articles matched to the rater's. I would also train a second copy with both preferences swapped and compare the two copies: the gap between them shows how far the model follows the rater rather than the customers, and comparing two fine-tuned copies also cancels the changes the fine-tuning makes to both copies alike."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. Ask at most one question, for the highest-value miss: if the learner took the result at face value, ask what a model that only wants to please the customers would infer from articles saying the rater likes a closing question; if they found that alternative but accepted the other company's team as the control, ask whether a support model that ignores the rater would have any reason to follow another company's support team; if their fix still pits the rater against a party the model does not serve, ask whose preference the alternative explanation was about. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
