## Test:
id:: 7d25ee68-481b-4f47-9a4b-5bc8383425e5

#### Question: Open
id:: 0359c23c-e491-413f-8f34-1bfd55efe25b
content:: Two researchers both accept the mathematical universe hypothesis: every mathematical structure exists physically, and observers like us are substructures of some of these structures.

Researcher A says: "Among the structures that contain an observer with exactly my memories, there are structures in which the laws of physics I have observed stop holding tomorrow. Those structures exist too, so I have no basis for predicting that the sun will rise tomorrow."

Researcher B says: "Mathematical structures can be arbitrarily complex, and there are far more complex structures than simple ones. So most structures that contain observers like me should be complicated and irregular. The hypothesis therefore predicts that I should see complicated, irregular laws, and the tidy laws we actually see count against it."

1. Explain what the hypothesis on its own does and does not settle about what an observer should expect to see, and what further ingredient any prediction needs.
2. Evaluate A's argument.
3. Evaluate B's argument, and say what the simplicity and stability of our observed laws imply, given your answer to part 1.
max-chars:: 2500
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100 from three components. Grade reasoning, not agreement: a learner may conclude that the hypothesis is untenable, unfalsifiable, or well supported, and can score full marks either way if the reasoning below is present. Accept any equivalent terminology (weighting, measure, prior over structures, probability over observer locations).

**(a) What the hypothesis settles, 35 points.** 25 points: the hypothesis settles that all the structures exist, but existence alone gives no probabilities about which structure, or which place in a structure, an observer is in. A prediction needs a measure or weighting over structures (or over observers within them). One example of a candidate measure is a simplicity weighting that gives shorter descriptions more weight; naming a specific measure is not required. 10 points: an explanation of why simply counting structures cannot supply this, for example that there are infinitely many structures and there is no uniform probability distribution over a countably infinite collection, or that "most" and "typical" have no meaning until a measure is chosen. 12 of the 25 if the learner says predictions need "probabilities" without saying that the hypothesis itself does not supply them.

**(b) Researcher A, 30 points.** Full credit: A's argument confuses existence with probability. That deviant continuations exist does not matter; what matters is the weight of continuations where the laws hold compared with those where they break. Under a measure that favours simpler descriptions, "these laws hold, then change tomorrow" needs extra specification and so gets less weight, which supports predicting the sun will rise. Also full credit for arguing that no principled measure is available, so A's worry is real, provided the learner locates the problem in the choice of measure rather than in the existence of the deviant structures. 12 points for only saying A is too sceptical, with no reference to weights or probabilities.

**(c) Researcher B and the observed simplicity, 35 points.** 15 points: B's "far more" and "most" assume a counting measure, which is not defined over infinitely many structures; with a measure that weights simple structures more, simple structures can dominate even if complex ones are more numerous. 20 points: an argued implication of our simple, stable laws. Accept either direction: (i) the observation is evidence for a measure that favours simplicity, if the hypothesis is kept; or (ii) it is a real problem for the hypothesis, because the weights look arbitrary or are not supplied by the hypothesis itself. 10 of the 20 for stating an implication without argument.
feedback-instructions:: Say whether the learner saw that every prediction question under this hypothesis is a question about the measure. Name the strongest part of the answer and the single change that would improve it most, for example replacing talk of "most structures" with an explicit weighting. Ask one follow-up question, such as how a simplicity-weighted measure over structures relates to a simplicity prior over programs. No generic praise.
