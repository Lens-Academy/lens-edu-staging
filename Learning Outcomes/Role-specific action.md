#### Question: Open
id:: f5ecf31b-f5ec-4983-99aa-e156d3d26247
content:: The second half of Chapter 14 issues different calls to action to different audiences: government leaders, elected officials who are already persuaded, politicians who are not yet persuaded, journalists, and ordinary citizens.

**Distinguish what the chapter asks of (a) government leaders, (b) skeptical politicians who aren't fully persuaded, and (c) ordinary citizens. What specific lever does each group hold that the other two cannot provide?**

assessment-instructions::
Score out of 100. Pick the level that best describes the answer as a whole, then a score inside that level's range: near the top if the answer fully reaches the level, near the bottom if it only just does. Judge what the answer shows the learner understands, not only what it spells out: a correct extension that depends on a point shows that point is understood, even if the answer states it briefly. An extension shows nothing about points it does not depend on.

**Level 1 (0-20):** Cannot tell the three groups apart, or gives them all the same role. *Example: "Everyone has to push back against the AI companies however they can."*

**Level 2 (21-40):** Says the groups have different roles, but gives each only a general one, such as governments signing treaties and politicians passing laws, rather than what the chapter asks of it, or gives the chapter's ask for one group only. *Example: "Governments can sign treaties, politicians can pass laws, and ordinary people can do their part."*

**Level 3 (41-65):** Gives the chapter's ask for two of the three groups. Giving the government leaders' ask as halting on their own, or the citizens' ask as boycotting AI products, misses that group's part: the chapter asks governments for a signal, not a halt on their own, and argues that personal boycotts cost a lot and achieve little. *Example: "Leaders should make clear they'd sign on if other countries did, instead of stopping alone. Politicians who aren't convinced should still get the chips moved into monitored sites so a stop stays possible."*

**Level 4 (66-85):** All three, each with what that group holds that the other two do not. Government leaders: this part is answered once the answer says they are asked to signal that their country would join an international agreement if others did, rather than to halt on their own. Skeptical politicians: this part is answered once the answer says they are asked to require that the computer chips used to train AI (GPU clusters) be gathered where they can be monitored, now, so that a halt stays possible later if they change their minds. Ordinary citizens: this part is answered once the answer says they are asked to press those in power on this issue, by any of the means the chapter names (writing to representatives, voting, lawful protest, talking about it). What each group holds can be stated briefly: only a country's leaders can offer its agreement, only lawmakers can make the rule, and only the public can give politicians the backing they need to act. An answer that gives all three asks but not what each group holds that the others do not reaches this level but not fully. Saying as well that a signal lets other countries agree without being left behind, that the skeptics need not accept the whole argument, which leaders have already made such signals, that primaries matter most, or what the chapter asks of journalists and of persuaded officials, belongs to the same parts and is not needed for them. An answer that does all three parts has answered the question in full, and without an extension it stays in this range. *Example: For the skeptics, adds "They don't have to buy the whole argument: keeping the chips in a few watched sites only means a stop is still possible if they come round, and writing that into law is their job."*

**Level 5 (86-100):** All of level 4, plus a correct extension beyond what the question asks. An extension is a further point with its own reasoning; restating the points above in more precise or technical terms is still level 4. Three that fit: how the levers depend on each other (for example, public backing lets politicians act, their rules keep a halt possible, and governments' signals make an agreement something other countries can join; saying only that the groups need each other does not by itself make an extension); why the chapter does not ask citizens to boycott AI products (one person's abstaining costs them a lot and changes almost nothing, while pressure on those in power grows with numbers); or whether the skeptics' ask holds up (what it assumes about how hard it would be to gather the chips back once they have spread), credited whichever way the learner judges it. Another extension of the same substance counts equally. Within this range the score reflects the whole answer: near the top only when the level 4 points and the extension are both well developed, and near the bottom when a strong extension rests on a thin base. *Examples: Adds "Deleting a chatbot app costs you plenty and no lab will notice; thousands of calls to a senator's office is another matter." Or adds "The skeptics' step only works if chips that have spread can't easily be rounded up again, which seems right once they're in thousands of private server rooms."*

force-feedback:: first
feedback-instructions:: Respond to the argument the learner actually made, and credit only what the answer says: do not attribute to them a point they did not make. Aim the reply at the idea they are missing, not at the score: frame the push as something about the material, never as what would earn more points. If they ask about their score, explain it by what the answer showed and what it left out, without naming levels or bands.

Open on the strongest thing in their answer and why it holds, in one sentence. If the answer goes beyond the question while one of its three parts (government leaders, skeptical politicians and ordinary citizens) is only asserted, that part is the push: deepening the base comes before going further. Otherwise, push once, chosen by where they stopped:

- If they gave all three groups the same role, ask what a head of government can do that an ordinary citizen cannot.
- If they gave general roles (treaties, laws), ask what the chapter specifically asks of each group, for instance whether it asks governments to halt on their own.
- If they read the government leaders' ask as halting on their own, ask what a government could offer that would let other countries agree without being left behind.
- If they gave boycotting AI products as the citizens' ask, ask what one person's boycott would change, and what citizens can do that those in power notice.
- If they had two of the three groups, name the group they missed and ask what the chapter asks of it.
- If they gave all three asks but not what each group holds, ask why the chapter gives each ask to that group rather than to the other two.
- If they answered all three parts and went no further, the moves left go beyond the question: how the levers depend on each other, why the chapter does not ask citizens to boycott AI products, or whether the skeptics' ask holds up. Ask about whichever their answer comes closest to.

If they made one of these moves, or another of the same substance, with all three parts solid, say so plainly in a sentence or two after the opening, without walking back through the parts they got right, and do not invent a further push. If you add a question, make it one they can take further out of interest, not one that suggests the answer was missing something. This reply can be shorter than the length below.

If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold: the skeptical politician. The chapter asks them to gather the chips used to train AI into places that can be monitored, now, even while unconvinced. Ask why that would matter if they later change their mind. If they still don't attempt it, rephrase the question.

At most one follow-up question. No generic praise, and do not recite the rubric back to them.

Response length: 100 to 160 words. Short paragraphs. No lists.
