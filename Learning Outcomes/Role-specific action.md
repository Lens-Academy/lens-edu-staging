## Test:
id:: 6c1d4ea5-7f20-5153-e8d9-f0a1b2345678

#### Question
content:: Imagine a campaign that wants to slow the development of AI systems it considers dangerous enough to threaten humanity. It can address three very different audiences, each with a different kind of power and a different starting belief: (a) the leadership of a national government, (b) legislators who are *not* persuaded that the risk is real, and (c) ordinary citizens who hold no office.

**Name the distinctive lever each of these three audiences holds — the specific thing that group can do which the other two cannot supply — and explain why that lever is not substitutable by the others.**

assessment-instructions::
Score according to the following rubric.
**1** — Cannot distinguish the three groups or conflates their roles. *Example: "They should all speak up and try to stop AI development."*

**2** — Identifies that different groups have different roles, but cannot specify what makes each group's lever unique. *Example: "Governments can make treaties, politicians can vote, and citizens can protest."*

**3** — Correctly names the distinctive lever for two of the three groups with some specificity. *Example: "Governments can signal openness to a treaty so that other countries don't feel they'd be disadvantaged by agreeing. Skeptical politicians can require GPU clusters to be concentrated so it's still possible to monitor them later. Citizens can vote and write representatives."*

**4** — Correctly names the distinctive lever for all three groups with specificity — e.g. government signaling (not a unilateral halt, but expressing willingness so others won't be disadvantaged by agreeing); a hedge for the unpersuaded, such as keeping compute concentrated and monitorable (preserving optionality without requiring full acceptance of the risk argument); and citizen action (vote especially in primaries, write representatives, protest lawfully, talk about it) — or equivalent levers named with comparable specificity. *Example of the unpersuaded-legislator lever: "Even if you're not persuaded of the full risk, keep large compute clusters concentrated now, because if you later change your mind, it will be too late to halt once they've proliferated everywhere."*

**5** — As above, plus articulates what makes each lever non-fungible: why government signaling requires state-level credibility to move other states; why constraining where compute sits requires regulatory authority that citizens don't have; and why citizen pressure creates the political permission structure that allows politicians and governments to act. *Example adds: "These groups form a chain: citizen pressure gives politicians permission to speak, politicians give governments cover to signal, and government signals make a treaty negotiable. No group can substitute for another because each lever requires a kind of authority or credibility the other groups lack."*
