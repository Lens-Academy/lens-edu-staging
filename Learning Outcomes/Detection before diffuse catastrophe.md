#### Question: Open
id:: d3e79723-fa74-435e-9686-8244a2d6b2ae
content::
A lab deploys a model that might be scheming. If it is, it needs about 25 well-placed bad actions, spread across the deployment, to cause a catastrophe; the 25th completes the damage. Today 2% of the model's actions are audited, chosen at random, and an auditor who looks at a bad action recognises it as bad 40% of the time. The lab has committed to respond decisively (pausing the deployment and investigating) once it has noticed 3 bad actions. Count the response as in time if it is more likely than not to come before the 25th bad action.

1. Is the response in time today? Show your numbers.
2. The lab is weighing three changes:
   - (a) double random auditing to 4% of actions;
   - (b) add a trained monitor that chooses which 2% of actions get audited, so that a bad action lands in the audited set 15% of the time (auditors still recognise 40% of the bad actions they see);
   - (c) respond decisively at the first noticed bad action instead of the third.

   Work out, with numbers, whether each would make the response in time, and compare them. Which would you pick, or would you push for more than one? If you would combine changes, give the combination's effect too.
placeholder:: Your numbers for today, then each change with its effect, and your pick. About 150 to 200 words.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. Worked numbers, for grading: the chance a bad action is noticed is q = audit chance x recognition. Today q = 0.02 x 0.4 = 0.008, so the expected number of bad actions before the 3rd notice is K/q = 3 / 0.008 = 375, far beyond 25; the chance of 3 notices among the first 24 bad actions is about 0.1%. (a) q = 0.016, K/q about 188, chance about 0.7%. (b) q = 0.15 x 0.4 = 0.06, K/q = 50, chance about 17% (about 19% counting over 25). (c) q = 0.008, K = 1, K/q = 125, chance about 18% (1 - 0.992^24). (b) and (c) together: q = 0.06, K = 1, K/q about 17, chance about 77%. Treat comparing the expected number of bad actions before the response (K/q, the measure in the lens) with 25 as an approximation to the stated test; it gives the same verdict for every option here, so accept either method, and accept counting over the first 24 or 25 bad actions. Small rounding differences do not matter. 40: part 1: q worked out from the audit rate and the recognition rate (15), and the 3-notice response compared with the 25 bad actions, by expectation (K/q with K = 3), by the probability of 3 notices, or by comparing the expected number of notices within the first 24 or 25 bad actions (C x q, about 0.19) with K, concluding that it is not in time, by a wide margin (25); an answer that treats the first notice as the response here earns at most 10 of these 25. A conclusion without numbers earns at most 10 of these 40. 60: part 2: (a), (b) and (c) each worked out with numbers that follow from q and K, 12 each (36); a change worked out with a wrong q or a wrong K earns at most 4 of its 12. The conclusion, shown with numbers, that none of the three alone makes the response in time, while (b) and (c) together do (an expected 1 / 0.06, about 17 bad actions before the response, or about a 77% chance of responding in time) (24); recommending a combination without working out its number earns at most 8 of these 24; an answer that picks one change only earns these 24 if it notes that its pick alone still leaves the response more likely late than in time and justifies the pick, for example by budget, or by preferring (b) because it cuts the expected damage before the response from 375 to 50 bad actions whereas (c) cuts it to 125. (b) and (c) give similar chances of being in time (both about 17 to 19%), so a reasoned pick of either earns full credit. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Grade reasoning, not agreement: an answer that questions an assumption (for example that a schemer would keep the same pace or that auditors keep their 40% recognition on monitor-chosen actions) keeps credit for the numbers it works out under the stated assumptions. Cap at 55 if the answer never compares the timing of the response with the 25 bad actions, for today or for any change. Cap at 55 if the answer never uses the 3-notice threshold, treating any noticed bad action as the response throughout. Cap at 55 if fewer than two of the changes (a), (b), (c) are worked out with numbers and compared with each other. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "1. Each bad action is noticed with chance 0.02 x 0.4 = 0.008, so the lab expects 3 / 0.008 = 375 bad actions before its third notice; the schemer needs 25. The chance of 3 notices within 24 bad actions is about 0.1%: detected eventually, but long after the damage. 2. (a) 4% auditing gives q = 0.016: an expected 188 bad actions, about a 0.7% chance of being in time. (b) The monitor gives q = 0.15 x 0.4 = 0.06: an expected 50, about 17%. (c) Responding at the first notice keeps q = 0.008 with K = 1: an expected 125, about 18%. None is enough alone. (b) and (c) together give an expected 1 / 0.06, about 17 bad actions, and about a 77% chance of responding in time, so I would push for both. If only one, the monitor: about the same chance as (c), but it cuts the expected damage before the response from 375 to 50 rather than 125, and keeps the 3-notice bar for a pause."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If a number is wrong, point to the first wrong step (usually multiplying the audit rate by the recognition rate, or dividing K by q) rather than giving the corrected total. If the learner treated one change as enough, ask what the chance of being in time is under that change alone. If the learner asks about their score, explain plainly what earned and what lost points. At most five sentences. No generic praise.
