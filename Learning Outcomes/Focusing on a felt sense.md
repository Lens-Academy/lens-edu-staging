## Test:
id:: 7a3d76ed-4126-45de-8a54-8a376e033190
#### Question: Open
id:: b7182924-96af-4c2e-90b3-10079b0d7919
content:: For two weeks, Jonas has been putting off a message to his housemate about how they split the chores. A friend offers to listen while he tries Focusing on it. After a minute of sitting quietly, Jonas says:

"There's a tightness in my throat when I open the chat. Okay, so obviously that's conflict avoidance. That makes sense, because my family never talked about problems, and conflict avoidance is really common. So the fix is to remind myself that adults can disagree calmly. There's also this heavy thing in my stomach about the rent going up, and my exams, and work. It's all piling up. Let me get through this quickly so I can finally send the message."

1. Where has Jonas moved away from Focusing? Name each problem you see.

2. You are the friend. Write what you would say to him next, in one to three sentences, and say why.
assessment-instructions:: The question shows Jonas, who has put off a message to his housemate about chores for two weeks, trying Focusing with a friend listening. He notices a tightness in his throat, immediately labels it "obviously conflict avoidance", explains why (his family never talked about problems, it's common) and decides on a fix (remind himself adults can disagree calmly). He then adds a heavy feeling in his stomach about the rent, his exams and work, says it is all piling up, and wants to get through it quickly. The question asks where he has moved away from Focusing, and what the learner, as the friend, would say next and why. In Focusing, a person attends to a felt sense (a bodily or vague sensation), tries out a handle (a word, phrase or image for it), checks whether the handle fits the felt sense, and iterates. It is receptive: listening rather than explaining, slowly, one felt sense at a time.

Score out of 100.

30: Says that he jumped from the felt sense to an explanation and a fix (thinking it through in his head, a ready-made story) without checking whether "conflict avoidance" actually fits the tightness, or without staying with the sensation.

20: Says that his attention has split across several feelings or topics at once (throat, stomach, rent, exams, work), where Focusing stays with one at a time and lets the others wait.

15: Says that he is rushing ("get through this quickly"), where Focusing needs time and patience.

35: The next prompt brings him back to one felt sense (the throat tightness, or whichever one he picks) and invites him to describe it or to check whether his words fit it, without supplying an interpretation of what it means. Also give 35 for a prompt that first makes space because "it's all piling up" (for example, setting the other feelings aside for later, or asking how he feels about all of it piling up) and then returns to one feeling. Asking what the feeling is like and whether a word fits it together is fine. Give 20 if the prompt returns to the body but offers the friend's own interpretation, or stacks questions about several topics at once (for example the throat, the rent and the exams together), or if no reason is given. Give 0 if the prompt endorses his explanation and fix.

Cap the total at 40 if the answer treats his conflict-avoidance explanation and fix as good Focusing.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "He went straight from the tightness to a theory and a fix, without checking whether 'conflict avoidance' fits the feeling. Then he piled on three more things at once, and he wants to hurry. I'd say: 'Let's leave the stomach and the rest outside for now. Stay with the tightness in your throat for a moment. What is it like? Does "conflict avoidance" fit it, or not quite?' That returns him to one felt sense and lets him check his words against it."
feedback-instructions:: The learner has analysed Jonas's Focusing attempt and written a next prompt as a listening friend. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong. If they missed that Jonas explained and fixed the feeling without checking whether his words fit it, name that first. If their prompt interprets the feeling for Jonas, say why that takes the listening away from him. If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold (for example: "What did Jonas do in the second sentence, right after noticing the tightness?").
