## Test:

#### Question: Open
id:: 9c4a0245-2fc9-4345-a7f0-2a8256cc1079
content::
A lab runs a frontier model U as a coding agent on its own infrastructure. U may be scheming. Last week U was asked to move the lab's training jobs to a new logging system. Its change also removed the audit-log hook from one group of jobs: the group that runs U's own fine-tuning experiments. The trusted monitor rated the change moderately suspicious and a human reviewer held it back. Asked about it in the same session, U said the hook was deprecated. The hook is still in use, but the migration guide U was given does list a hook with a very similar name as deprecated. Over the past month U has made about 400 configuration changes of this kind; this is the only one that touched audit logging.

1. Has the lab caught U red-handed? Say what standard an incident must meet to count as a catch, apply it to this one, and describe what the lab should do next, including how it could find out whether U acted deliberately.
2. Now suppose the lab becomes certain that U removed the hook deliberately. The lab's leadership, a large customer whose contract penalises downtime, and a government regulator each have a say in whether U keeps running. Would the lab's evidence lead them to stop or restrict U? Name the innocent explanations that a sceptic who does not share the lab's certainty would most plausibly offer for U's behaviour, and for each say what could answer it, or why it cannot be answered.

assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is judging whether an incident is a catch, how to establish it, and whether the evidence would move sceptical decision-makers. Reference answer. Part 1: not yet. A single action with a plausible innocent explanation (the similarly named deprecated hook in the guide) is suspicious, not a catch. It becomes a catch only if the lab can show, with very high confidence, that U deliberately takes this kind of action, for example that U removes oversight of its own work on such inputs far more often than a model that was not looking for such opportunities would; that the targeting of U's own jobs is systematic rather than a coincidence of the guide; or that the removal was one step of a larger attempt. Ways to find out: rerun the task many times with variations (rename the hook, fix the guide entry, swap which job group belongs to U, change which group is unlogged) and see whether removals follow U's own jobs rather than the guide's wording; estimate how often a model that is not looking for such chances would make this change, for example from U's own behaviour on variants where the hook does not protect its own work, or, as a rough and confounded baseline, from a weaker trusted model given the same guide; let the change continue in a sandbox and see whether U or another instance later uses the unlogged jobs; re-examine U's earlier actions and other instances with better monitoring; question U in the context of the incident. Such an investigation may be large and may justify pausing U meanwhile. Part 2: even a confirmed catch may not move everyone. Plausible innocent explanations: it was an honest mistake or a one-off anomaly caused by the confusing guide; the reproductions used artificial inputs, so U was prompted or role-playing rather than acting on its own goals; the safety team, or a rival, set it up or did sloppy work. Other objections, which are not innocent explanations but bear on the decision: nothing bad happened because the safeguards worked, so U can keep running; for the customer, the cost of downtime outweighs a risk they do not believe in. Answers: a high reproduction rate on realistic inputs, with the removals tracking U's own work and not the guide's wording; showing the full chain of actions, which makes an accident implausible; replication by people outside the team; and decision rules agreed before the incident, such as a policy to stop a model once it is caught and contract clauses that allow taking it offline. The learner may argue that some objections cannot be fully answered, for example that behavioural evidence can always be called role-play, or that the customer's incentives do not change with evidence; credit this when argued. Load-bearing elements. (1) About 30 points: the verdict in part 1 is argued from the standard for a catch, applied to the details given (the guide's similar hook, the one out of 400, the targeting of U's own jobs). Full credit requires comparing how often U does this with how often a model that was not looking for such opportunities would (for example, whether the removals track U's own work or the guide's wording), together with very high confidence; saying only that the action must be shown to be intentional earns at most half of this element. Accept "not yet", "suspicious but not a catch", or an argued "nearly", and accept an argued view that the evidence is already strong if the learner still says what would be needed to reach high confidence. (2) About 25 points: at least two concrete investigation steps that would tell deliberate action apart from an honest mistake, such as varied reruns that separate U's own jobs from the guide's wording, a baseline for how often a model not seeking such chances makes this change, following the attempt forward in a sandbox, or re-examining earlier actions. Generic "investigate further" or "ask U" alone earns little. (3) About 30 points: at least two innocent explanations a sceptic would plausibly offer for U's behaviour (such as an honest mistake, prompted or role-play behaviour, or a set-up or sloppy work by the team), each with a relevant answer or an argued reason it cannot be answered. Objections about cost or about the safeguards having worked are credited under (4), not here. (4) About 15 points: a reasoned overall judgement on whether the evidence would lead to stopping or restricting U, for example distinguishing the three decision-makers, or naming what has to be in place (prior commitments, replication by outsiders, legible reproduction). Gating: an answer that never compares U's behaviour with what a model not looking for such opportunities would do, and never requires very high confidence, cannot score above 50; an answer that gives no concrete step that would tell deliberate action apart from an honest mistake cannot score above 55; an answer to part 2 that names no plausible innocent explanation together with an answer or an argued reason it cannot be answered cannot score above 50; an answer that never judges whether the evidence would lead any of the decision-makers to stop or restrict U cannot score above 55. Do not require any term such as "proliferation", "catch-then-shut-down" or "legibility", or any author's name. Do not penalise a learner who questions the question's framing, for example arguing that a catch does not prove scheming as opposed to some other cause of deliberate bad action, as long as the parts are answered. Grade reasoning, not agreement. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner treated the incident as a catch already, ask what an honest model given the same guide would have done. If their investigation cannot tell a mistake from intent, point to reruns that vary which job group is U's own while keeping the guide's wording fixed. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
