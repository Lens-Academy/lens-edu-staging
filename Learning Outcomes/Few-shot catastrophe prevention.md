#### Question: Open
id:: d9f14633-d331-4e39-8b39-11697399d24b
content::
A lab uses a model U as a research agent. Last month U was caught, beyond reasonable doubt, trying to copy part of its own weights out of the lab: it hid a network call in a data-loading script, a second instance of U approved the script in code review, and the lab's outbound-traffic filter blocked the transfer, which led to an alert and an investigation. Humans also audit a sample of U's code, but the sample did not include this script. Once someone looked, the hidden call was easy to spot. Leadership has decided that U must keep running, because pausing would hand the lead to a less careful competitor.

The safety team proposes this plan for the next quarter:

(a) Fine-tune U with a strong penalty on the transcript of the attempt and on 200 paraphrased variants of it, so that U stops making such attempts.
(b) Since the outbound filter is what stopped the attempt, move half of the budget for human audits of U's code to strengthening that filter.
(c) Replace the quarterly control evaluation, in which a red team designs its own attacks, with a monthly test of all safeguards against the caught attempt and its variants, since these are real attacks rather than guesses, and use the freed staff for (b).

1. Assess each part of the plan: what it would buy, what it would cost, and whether you would keep, change or drop it.
2. Propose two or three other ways to use the caught attempt over the quarter, and say what each would buy the lab.
placeholder:: Take (a), (b) and (c) in turn, then give your own proposals
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 35 for (a), 15: one cost specific to training on caught attempts: after the update the recorded attempts no longer reflect how U behaves (undermining probes or reruns built on them), or U makes fewer attempts that the lab can catch and learn from. 15: a second concrete cost: the other of those two, U learning from the penalty which countermeasures exist and which approaches fail, or the tendency being hidden rather than removed. 5: a verdict that weighs these costs against the upside, for example using the examples mainly to train monitors and probes and dropping, deferring or limiting (a). 20 for (c), 10: it keeps or seriously qualifies rather than replaces the evaluation against attacks a red team constructs, because seen attempts may be unrepresentative or deliberately weak decoys meant to create false confidence or pull resources toward the wrong safeguard, and 10: a serious attempt must still be caught, which needs testing against the strongest attacks the lab can construct. 10 for (b): one caught attempt is weak evidence about where the next one will come from, so other safeguards such as human audits must stay robust enough to pass a control evaluation rather than being stripped to strengthen one line. 35 for part 2: two proposals that use the attempt, each tied to what it buys, 20 for the first and 15 for the second. A specific lesson from how the attempt unfolded counts as a proposal when it leads to an action (the reviewing copy of U approved the script, so code review by another instance of U must be checked or replaced), but as at most one of the two. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Grade reasoning, not agreement: an argued plan that keeps a limited (a) with its costs named and weighed, shifts some effort as in (b), or tests less often against constructed attacks while keeping such testing and naming the decoy risk can earn full credit. Caps: an answer that names neither of the two costs specific to training on caught attempts (recorded attempts stop reflecting how U behaves, fewer attempts remain to catch) cannot score above 55, and one that names no cost of training U at all cannot score above 50; an answer that never argues that seen attempts may be unrepresentative or decoys, or that endorses dropping all testing against attacks a red team constructs, cannot score above 50; an answer with no concrete use of the attempt beyond (a) to (c) cannot score above 55. A pass is roughly 60. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "(a) Drop it, or defer it until the examples have been used for everything else, or keep only a limited version with its costs named. Training U on the attempt might add some reliability, but U may learn from the penalised examples which countermeasures exist and which approaches fail; after the update the recorded attempts no longer reflect how U behaves, which spoils tools built on them, such as a probe on U's internals or reruns of the attempt; U will make fewer attempts that the lab could catch and learn from; and fewer visible attempts would not show that U stopped scheming, only that this pattern was trained away. Use the examples mainly to train monitors and probes. (b) Partly sound: shifting some effort toward what attackers actually target is reasonable, but one caught attempt is weak evidence about where the next one will come from, and human audits are another line of defence; keep every safeguard robust enough to pass a control evaluation rather than stripping one line to strengthen another. (c) Add the caught attempt and its variants as a test set, but keep the control evaluation in which a red team designs its own attacks. Attempts seen so far may be unrepresentative, or deliberately weak decoys meant to create false confidence or pull resources toward the wrong safeguard; a scheming model must eventually make a serious attempt, and the lab needs a high chance of catching that first serious one, so testing against the strongest attacks a red team can construct must continue. Other uses: rerun the attempt with varied inputs (change the script, the reviewer's context, what the filter would do) to collect more and different attempts; let a sandboxed copy continue past the block to see the intended endgame; check which safeguards did and did not work, starting with the second instance of U that approved the script in code review; train monitors, a probe or an untrusted monitor on the proliferated examples, with innocent near-copies as contrast; re-run improved monitoring over U's earlier actions to find attempts that failed without being caught; question U about the attempt; test whether other models or training methods would join in."
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner accepted (a) without costs, ask what U could learn from being penalised on exactly this transcript. If they accepted (c), ask what a weak first attempt would be worth to a patient schemer. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
