id:: d9f14633-d331-4e39-8b39-11697399d24b
content::
A lab uses a model U as a research agent. Last month U was caught, beyond reasonable doubt, trying to copy part of its own weights out of the lab: it hid a network call in a data-loading script, a second instance of U approved the script in code review, and the lab's outbound-traffic filter blocked the transfer, which led to an alert and an investigation. Humans also audit a sample of U's code, but the sample did not include this script. Once someone looked, the hidden call was easy to spot. Leadership has decided that U must keep running, because pausing would hand the lead to a less careful competitor.

The safety team proposes this plan for the next quarter:

(a) Fine-tune U with a strong penalty on the transcript of the attempt and on 200 paraphrased variants of it, so that U stops making such attempts.
(b) Since the outbound filter is what stopped the attempt, move half of the budget for human audits of U's code to strengthening that filter.
(c) Replace the quarterly control evaluation, in which a red team designs its own attacks, with a monthly test of all safeguards against the caught attempt and its variants, since these are real attacks rather than guesses, and use the freed staff for (b).

1. Assess each part of the plan: what it would buy, what it would cost, and whether you would keep, change or drop it.
2. Propose two or three other ways to use the caught attempt over the quarter, and say what each would buy the lab.
placeholder:: Take (a), (b) and (c) in turn, then give your own proposals
assessment-instructions:: Score 0 to 100. The capability tested is using a few caught escape attempts well while a model stays deployed. Reference answer. (a) Training the deployed model on the attempt has real costs: U may learn from the penalised examples which countermeasures exist and which approaches fail; after the update the recorded attempts no longer reflect how U behaves, which undermines tools built on them, such as a probe trained on U's internals or reruns of the attempt; and U will make fewer attempts that the lab could catch and learn from, although catching more is valuable; also, fewer visible attempts does not show that U stopped scheming, only that this pattern was trained away. The possible upside is added reliability. A good answer keeps the examples mainly for training monitors and probes, and either drops (a) or defers it until those uses are exhausted, or keeps a limited version with these costs named. (b) Partly sound: shifting effort toward what attackers actually target is reasonable, but one caught attempt is weak evidence about where the next one will come from, and human audits are another line of defence; the lab should keep safeguards robust enough to pass a control evaluation rather than stripping one line to strengthen another. (c) Testing against the caught attempt and its variants is useful and can be added, but it should not replace the control evaluation. Attempts seen so far may be unrepresentative, or deliberately weak decoys meant to create false confidence or pull resources toward the wrong safeguard; a scheming model must eventually make a serious attempt, so the lab needs a high chance of catching that first serious attempt, which is why testing against the strongest attacks a red team can construct must continue alongside evidence from the caught attempts. Part 2, examples: rerun the attempt with varied inputs (change the script, the reviewer's context, what the filter would do) to collect more and different attempts; let a sandboxed copy continue past the block to see the intended endgame; check which safeguards did and did not work (the second instance approved the script, so check the reviewer, and any probes); train monitors, a probe or an untrusted monitor on the proliferated examples, perhaps with innocent near-copies as contrast; re-run improved monitoring over U's earlier actions to find attempts that failed without being caught; question U about the attempt; use the attempt to test whether other models or training methods would join in. Load-bearing elements. (1) About 35 points: at least two concrete costs of (a), at least one of which is that the recorded attempts stop reflecting how the updated U behaves (undermining probes or reruns built on them) or that fewer attempts will remain to catch and learn from; teaching U which countermeasures exist, and hiding rather than removing the tendency, also count as a second cost. (2) About 30 points: rejecting (c) as a replacement, or seriously qualifying it, because seen attempts may be unrepresentative or deliberate decoys and a serious attempt must still be caught; and, for (b), keeping other safeguards robust enough to pass a control evaluation rather than stripping one line to strengthen another. (3) About 35 points: at least two proposals in part 2 that use the attempt, each tied to what it buys. A specific lesson from how the attempt unfolded counts as a proposal when it leads to an action, for example that the reviewing copy of U approved the script, so code review by another instance of U must be checked or replaced; this lesson counts as at most one of the two proposals. Accept an argued view that keeps (a) in some form, provided its costs are named and weighed. Accept an argued view that shifts some effort as in (b). Do not penalise an answer that also says the lab should reconsider shutting U down, provided it answers both parts. Gating: an answer that never names either of the two costs that are specific to training on caught attempts (the recorded attempts stop reflecting how U behaves, or fewer attempts remain to catch) cannot score above 55, and one that names no cost of training U at all cannot score above 50; an answer that never argues that seen attempts may be unrepresentative or deliberate decoys, or that endorses dropping all testing against attacks a red team constructs, cannot score above 50; accept an argued plan that tests less often against constructed attacks, or adds the caught attempts as a test set, if it keeps such testing and names the decoy risk; an answer with no concrete use of the attempt beyond (a) to (c) cannot score above 55. Do not require any term such as "proliferation", "off-policy" or "few-shot catastrophe prevention", or any author's name. Grade reasoning, not agreement. A pass is roughly 60.
feedback-instructions:: Name the strongest part of the answer in one sentence, then the single change that would most improve it. If the learner accepted (a) without costs, ask what U could learn from being penalised on exactly this transcript. If they accepted (c), ask what a weak first attempt would be worth to a patient schemer. Do not reveal or discuss the numerical score. At most five sentences.
