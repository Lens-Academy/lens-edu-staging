## Test:
id:: 54c95757-8051-4dcc-aa3c-191d00856468
#### Question: Open
id:: 7973c7c2-9c07-4450-ae9b-49a93d3d5d86
content:: Sam is a software engineer moving into AI safety. In reading groups he never asks questions, although he often has some. He has decided this hesitation is irrational and wants to get rid of it. His plan:

- At Thursday's reading group (eight people, friendly), he will ask at least one question.
- He will do this every week until the nervousness is gone.
- While he asks, he will ignore the nerves and push through.

Two years ago, at a different group, a senior researcher made fun of a question Sam asked. Sam says that has nothing to do with it.

Is this a good Comfort Zone Exploration experiment? Say what you would keep and what you would change, and why.
assessment-instructions:: The question describes Sam, who never asks questions in reading groups and has decided this hesitation is irrational. His plan: ask at least one question at Thursday's friendly eight-person reading group, repeat every week until the nervousness is gone, and ignore his nerves and push through while asking. Two years ago a senior researcher at a different group mocked one of his questions, which Sam says is unrelated. The question asks whether this is a good Comfort Zone Exploration (CoZE) experiment, what to keep, what to change, and why. In CoZE, the person stays open to the hesitation being in the right place, checks that both futures (doing it and not doing it) feel acceptable, runs a small and safe experiment, pays attention to inner reactions and to what actually happens, and afterwards digests it and decides whether to continue without forcing.

Score out of 100.

20: Keeps a small, safe step. Either the answer notes that one question at a small, friendly group is already a small and safe experiment worth keeping, or its redesign keeps a step of similar size. Give 0 for this element if the redesign makes the step bigger or riskier.

35: Names the stance problem: Sam has decided in advance that the hesitation is wrong and must be removed (pushing it away rather than exploring it), and "every week until the nervousness is gone" commits him to that outcome. Gives the fix: hold the question open, so that keeping the hesitation is an acceptable result, for example by checking that both futures feel okay before starting. Give 35 for the problem and a fix, 20 for the problem without a fix.

15: Takes the old mocking experience seriously instead of accepting Sam's claim that it is unrelated: for example, as a likely reason for the hesitation, or as information about which groups or people are safe to ask in. Either reading earns the 15.

30: Replaces "ignore the nerves and push through" with attention and digestion. Attention: noticing his inner reactions and what actually happens (how people respond to his question). Digestion: afterwards, he compares what happened with what he expected, and decides then whether to go again, instead of having committed in advance. Give 30 for both, 15 for only one.

Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "Keep the step: one question at a small, friendly group is small and safe. Change the stance. Sam has already decided the hesitation is wrong and plans to repeat until it is gone, which is pushing through, not exploring. He should picture both futures, asking and not asking, and start only if both feel okay. The mocking may be why the hesitation is there, and it may tell him something real about where asking is safe. Instead of ignoring his nerves, he should notice them and notice how people actually react, then afterwards compare that with what he expected and decide calmly whether to do it again."
feedback-instructions:: The learner has judged a plan against CFAR's Comfort Zone Exploration (CoZE): explore rather than expand, accept both worlds, keep it small and safe, attend to inner and outer reality, digest without forcing, and respect Chesterton's Fence. In 80 to 150 words, short paragraphs, no lists: name the strongest part of their answer, then the single most important thing it missed or got wrong. If they missed the stance (Sam has decided the outcome in advance and plans to force it, which is pushing through, not exploring), name that first. If they scored full marks, confirm briefly. Say why they got their score if they ask. No generic praise. If they say they don't understand, give one concrete foothold (for example: "What would Sam do if, after a few weeks, he found the hesitation was protecting him from something?").
