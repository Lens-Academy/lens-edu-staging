## Test:
id:: 87cc43f8-fce8-4062-a86e-d0f24ca3ff32
#### Question: Open
id:: efc90bd6-dcca-4b48-b039-cb234979beca
content::
Sam has meditated for an hour a day for two years. Three months ago his father was diagnosed with cancer. Sam says he feels "completely at peace with it, because everything is impermanent". He has stopped visiting his father, because the visits "stir up old stuff I have already let go of". His partner says he has been sleeping badly and snaps at her more than he used to. A friend says: "Sam has reached real equanimity."

Do you agree with the friend? Go through what Sam says, what he does and what others see, say what each suggests, and say what else you would want to know to decide.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 30: the answer sees that stopping the visits keeps the feeling out by avoiding the situation, which points away from equanimity or acceptance: those let the feeling be felt and stay engaged with the people involved, while avoiding a situation because of the feelings it brings is avoidance. 25: the answer sees that Sam uses a teaching (impermanence, having "let go") to dismiss feelings or a need that is still there, rather than having worked through it. 25: the answer sees that Sam's own report of peace conflicts with what his partner observes (poor sleep, irritability), so his self-report alone cannot settle it and stress may be present that he does not report. 20: the answer names at least one further piece of evidence that would actually distinguish the cases, for example: how Sam reacts when he does face his father's illness and whether he returns to calm quickly or does not react at all, whether he still cares about and acts for his father, how he answers specific questions about feeling flat or cut off, or how things look over a longer time. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording; technical terms are not needed. An answer that disagrees with the friend for these reasons, or that stays undecided for these reasons, can earn full marks. Cap at 40 if the answer concludes that Sam clearly has equanimity because he says he is at peace.
feedback-instructions:: Say in one or two sentences what the answer saw clearly. If it scored below 100, name the most important thing it missed, from these: avoiding the visits is keeping the feeling out, which equanimity and acceptance do not do (Kornfield's "balanced engagement" and Desbordes et al.'s caring mother, Shinzen Young's line that dropping resistance to pain does not mean giving up on its source); using impermanence to dismiss a need is what Welwood calls spiritual bypassing; a partner seeing poor sleep and irritability while Sam reports peace is the pattern Martin found at dinner with one of his participants; and a further check, such as quick recovery versus no reaction (Desbordes et al.), would help decide. Do not say Sam is ill or diagnose him. 60 to 110 words. No generic praise.
