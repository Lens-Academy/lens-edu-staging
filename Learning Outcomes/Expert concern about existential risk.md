## Test:
id:: 790b1b56-7654-4b53-afb8-bb2bcb2bd501

#### Question
content::
A colleague sends you this and asks what you think:

> Last month 900 economists, including four Nobel laureates, signed an open letter warning that automated trading systems now pose a serious risk of a market collapse worse than 2008. Nine hundred economists. Four Nobels. I think that settles it.

Two questions, in a few sentences each.

First: what has the letter established, and what has it not established? Be specific about the difference.

Second: your colleague still has to decide whether to act on this. What evidence would actually move you, and why that evidence rather than a larger number of signatures?

assessment-instructions::
The learner has been given an invented case, deliberately outside the material they read, in which a headcount of credentialed people is offered as proof of a contested empirical claim.

Score the answer against four checks. Each check is pass or fail on its own. **The learner passes the test if they pass check 1 and at least two of checks 2 to 4.**

**Check 1 (mandatory): the distinction is made.** The learner states that the signatures are evidence about the distribution of opinion among some group of economists, not evidence about markets. Any wording is fine. It fails if the learner only says "appeal to authority" as a label without saying what the letter is evidence *about*, and it fails if the learner treats the signature count as directly probative.

**Check 2: the endorsement is not dismissed either.** The learner grants that widespread expert concern is real evidence of something, for example that the claim is worth investigating, that it is not fringe, or that people with relevant training have seen something that worries them. It fails if the learner treats the letter as worthless.

**Check 3: at least one specific defeater.** The learner names a concrete reason the signature count is weaker than it looks. Any one of these passes: the signatories are self-selected rather than sampled; signing is cheap and commits a person to very little; the letter's wording may be vague enough that signatories mean different things by it; expertise in economics does not automatically transfer to the behaviour of automated trading systems; there is no denominator, so 900 could be most of the relevant field or a small fraction of it.

**Check 4: the evidence they name is mechanism-level or measurable.** The learner asks for something that would bear on the claim itself, for example the mechanism by which the collapse would propagate, historical base rates for the failure mode, what the signatories individually predict and by when, stress tests, or what the strongest informed dissenters say and why. It fails if the request is only for more or better-credentialed endorsements, and it fails if the answer is a general call for "more research" with no content.

Notes for the grader:
- A learner is permitted to conclude that the letter is fairly strong evidence, provided they say what would make it strong and acknowledge what it cannot show. Grade the reasoning, not the verdict.
- A learner who spots that the second question is the harder one, and answers it concretely, is doing well even if the first answer is brief.
- Do not require the phrase "appeal to authority", "base rate", "selection effect" or any other term of art. A learner who describes the problem in plain language has demonstrated the outcome.
