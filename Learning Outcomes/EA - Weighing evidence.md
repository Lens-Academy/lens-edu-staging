## Test:
id:: 6326974b-14ab-4ddd-8bb8-a754ddf23416
#### Question: Open
id:: e0561bbb-1b69-4fd3-85c5-6777a6692fb8
content::
Two claims, each with the evidence offered for it.

**Case A.** A popular book says that a daily five-minute breathing exercise "makes people calmer and happier". Its evidence is one randomised trial: 60 university students in the United States were assigned either to the breathing exercise for six weeks or to a waiting list. At the end of the six weeks, the breathing group reported less stress and more positive emotions on questionnaires than the waiting-list group.

**Case B.** A retreat centre's website shows 40 written reports from past guests. Most say they arrived during a hard period in their lives and that after a ten-day silent retreat they found a deep peace that has lasted ever since. Several add that their teacher confirmed they had reached the first stage of awakening.

For each case: what kind of evidence is it, and what can it show? Name three specific ways it could mislead. Then say what one further piece of evidence would most strengthen the claim, and why.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100: 50 for Case A and 50 for Case B. Grade the reasoning in the learner's own words. Technical terms are not required when the idea is clearly stated.

Case A (one randomised trial with a waiting-list control: 60 US university students, six weeks of a five-minute daily breathing exercise vs waiting list, outcomes self-reported stress and positive emotions at the end of the six weeks).
- 10: Says what kind of evidence it is and what it can show: a single randomised trial with a passive (waiting-list) control, which can show that doing the exercise caused an average short-term difference in self-reported stress and positive emotion compared with doing nothing, in people like these. 5 if the kind is named but not what it can show, or the reverse.
- 24: Ways it could mislead, 8 points for each distinct one, up to three: (a) the control group did nothing, so expectation, attention or simply doing something could explain the difference, and it does not show the exercise beats an alternative activity. (b) outcomes were self-reported by people who knew which group they were in, so expectation can inflate them. (c) measured only at the end of six weeks, so it says nothing about whether the effect lasts. (d) a narrow sample (US university students, a WEIRD sample) may not generalise to others. (e) one small study, which may not replicate, and a book may have picked a favourable study out of several. (f) the claim goes beyond what was measured, for example "calmer and happier" in a lasting or general sense from a short-term questionnaire.
- 16: One further piece of evidence that addresses a weakness the learner named or that is clearly present, with the reason: for example a trial with an active control, a longer follow-up, independent replications or a meta-analysis, a broader sample, or a measure less open to expectation. 8 if the evidence is named without saying which weakness it addresses.

Case B (40 testimonials chosen and published by a retreat centre, guests came at a low point, report lasting peace after a ten-day retreat, some say their teacher confirmed an attainment).
- 10: Says what kind of evidence it is and what it can show: testimonials, that is first-person reports from self-selected people published by an interested party, plus a teacher's judgement from inside the tradition. They can show that some guests sincerely report such an experience, what it was like for them, and that the retreat may be worth testing. 5 if the kind is named but not what it can show, or the reverse.
- 24: Ways it could mislead, 8 points for each distinct one, up to three: (a) regression to the mean: guests came during a hard period, so many would feel better later whatever they did. (b) selection or survivorship: the centre chose which reports to show, and guests who were not helped or were harmed are missing. (c) expectation: guests expected peace, and a tradition's stage descriptions can shape what people experience and report. (d) "has lasted ever since" is a remembered self-report, with no measurement before or over time, and introspection is unreliable. (e) the teacher's confirmation is not independent: the judge belongs to the tradition and has a stake, and how experiences are judged depends on who judges. (f) no comparison group, so other changes in the guests' lives could explain it.
- 16: One further piece of evidence that addresses a weakness, with the reason: for example measuring all guests, not selected ones, with a tested questionnaire before the retreat and months after, comparing with similar people who did not attend or were on a waiting list, or assessment by people outside the tradition. 8 if the evidence is named without saying which weakness it addresses.

Deduct 10 (once) if the answer says either kind of evidence shows nothing at all. Deduct 10 (once) if it treats the trial in Case A as proof that the exercise makes people lastingly happier.
feedback-instructions:: The learner judged two invented cases: a single randomised trial with a waiting-list control (Case A) and 40 testimonials published by a retreat centre (Case B), and received a score. In 120 to 200 words: name the strongest part of their answer, then for each case the most important weakness they missed, if any, and whether their proposed further evidence would address a real weakness. If they scored 100, confirm briefly and add one point they could use next time. No generic praise. If they ask, explain the score from the grading reason.
