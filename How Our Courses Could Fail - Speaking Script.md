# How Our Courses Could Fail — Speaking Script

**Approximate duration:** 5 minutes at a natural speaking pace.  
**Format:** Four slides. The text is also included in the PowerPoint speaker notes.

## Slide 1 — Thought experiments

We wanted to think more broadly about how our courses could fail. Instead of starting with a particular theory, we chose three concrete hypothetical outcomes.

First, half of our graduates get jobs at OpenAI. Second, almost no one continues doing anything in AI safety. Third, graduates appear successful — they work on projects and get accepted into programs — but their actual contributions are meaningless.

For each scenario, we brainstormed explanations, including contradictory or unlikely ones. We deliberately did not try to rank them or resolve every inconsistency. The point was to generate possibilities, not to present final conclusions.

## Slide 2 — 50% of graduates get jobs at OpenAI

In our first scenario, 50% of graduates end up working at OpenAI. 

One explanation is that participants already intended to take this route; our course simply gave them useful resources. Another is that we did a good job teaching skills but a poor job communicating the ideas and priorities that should guide their use. We might have explained some paths in concrete detail while leaving the alternatives vague.

The course might also have amplified memes or influential participants whose goals differ from ours, or failed to make safety-oriented paths especially accessible.

The broader lesson from the brainstorm is to be careful about the inference. If 80% would have joined OpenAI without the course, the observed 50% might represent an improvement. If the other half achieved something unusually valuable, that could change the overall assessment. And the world, the field, or even our survey data might be different from what we assume.

A simple placement statistic doesn't settle the impact question.

## Slide 3 — Almost no one continues in AI safety

In the second scenario, almost nobody goes on to do anything in AI safety. Here the problem isn't necessarily choosing the wrong path. It's a lack of follow-through.

We may have taught useful material without making the next steps concrete: where to apply, what to work on, or how to use what they learned. Or we may have taught material that wasn't useful in practice, or something different from our original intention.

Motivation is another possibility. Too much doom could overwhelm people, but too little urgency could encourage them to assume someone else will deal with the problem. We could also present such a narrow slice of AI safety that nobody sees a place for themselves.

An especially important possibility from the brainstorm is optimizing for participants' contributions while the course is running, rather than for what happens after graduation.

Still, low continuation is not by itself conclusive: relevant opportunities may be oversubscribed, and an exploratory pilot might be useful precisely because we learned that a particular approach didn't work.

## Slide 4 — Graduates are active, but their work is meaningless

The third scenario is perhaps the most deceptive. Graduates are working on AI safety projects, getting accepted into programs, and looking successful — but the work itself is meaningless.

In this case, we may mistake visible activity for actual contributions. Our course could have been easy to complete without thinking deeply. It might have taught fragmented facts and concepts that never formed a coherent picture, or knowledge that students used on exams but not in real decisions.

We may have started cultivating strategic thinking and research taste too late. We might also have a poorly specified theory of change and therefore optimize for whatever is easy to measure. That creates Goodhart risks: the course or its selection process rewards the appearance of success rather than the results we care about.

Other explanations concern who we selected, how much participants engaged, and whether our evidence about impact is itself biased.

So the three scenarios are distinct: skills directed toward unintended outcomes, knowledge that doesn't lead to action, and action that doesn't produce meaningful impact. These are brainstorming hypotheses, not ranked risks or a finished course design.
