### Thought Experiments: How Our Courses Could Fail

To better understand what could go wrong, we considered three hypothetical scenarios in which our courses fail.

For each scenario, we brainstormed possible reasons for that outcome. We deliberately avoided evaluating their plausibility or consistency with one another at this stage — the goal was to generate as broad a range of hypotheses as possible about what could go wrong.

Below are the results of these brainstorms.

### Scenario 1: We ran a course, and 50% of its graduates got jobs at OpenAI

**Possible reasons for failure:**

- We recruited people who consciously want to bring about the end of the world (and failed to change their minds).
- We failed to communicate what's right and what's wrong.
- We spread memes that lead to outcomes we don't want (e.g., too much doom).
- We're treating something as a failure when it's actually a new form of long-term success.
- We failed to prevent other people's memes from spreading through our course (e.g., impostor experts or charismatic participants).
- We designed the course in a way that made it clear how to do the wrong thing, while leaving how to do the right thing entirely implicit.
- It was a statistical fluctuation (the failure happened because our processes weren't optimized to prevent it).
- By calling something a failure, we're missing the bigger picture (50 people joined OpenAI, but the other 50 founded a new MIRI and have already produced the first approximation to solving the central alignment problem).
- Participants had this exact outcome in mind from the beginning, but used our course as a resource because the value it provides is equally useful for pursuing different goals.
- We presented the field in a way that made it seem like the last thing anyone would want to work on.
- We targeted an audience whose baseline outcome would have been 80% joining OpenAI.
- We think it's a failure, but the field has already changed and this is no longer a bad outcome — we just haven't realized it yet.
- Our course was useful for developing skills but not for internalizing ideas.
- The participants involved in this failure were precisely those we paid little attention to. Since our optimization efforts were unevenly distributed, and things don't happen on their own, this outcome occurred.
- We presented the problem so gently and non-alarmingly that people concluded joining OpenAI wasn't actually that bad.
- We think the failure happened, but the information is actually distorted (the survey was filled out by a prankster hacker's bot).
- We didn't make it asymmetrically easier for people to pursue AI safety.
- The skills we taught were actually more relevant to OpenAI, and the graduates initially applied to Redwood but weren't accepted.

### Scenario 2: We ran a course, but almost none of its participants went on to do anything in AI safety

**Possible reasons for failure:**

- We didn't make the next steps clear.
- We didn't help people figure out where to go or what to do with the knowledge they'd gained.
- The knowledge we provided isn't useful anywhere.
- What we actually taught was completely different from what we intended to teach.
- We either overwhelmed people with so much doom that they stopped caring, or failed to highlight the problem enough, creating a sense that "someone else will handle it; I don't need to."
- We didn't apply enough optimization pressure to make the outcomes we wanted happen, and these outcomes don't occur by default.
- We targeted and reached people who had no intention of committing to AI safety, and we failed to build that motivation.
- The course didn't create any expectation that participants could or should actually do something.
- The course presented only a small part of AI safety, and nobody found a place for themselves within that part, so they abandoned AI safety altogether.
- Given the initial conditions under which we launched the course, this outcome wasn't even that bad (it was a pilot run where we tested something unlikely to work, such as reaching new target audiences, and learned that it didn't work).
- The opportunities we presented as possible next steps became oversubscribed, and participants simply weren't accepted.
- A meme emerged and spread within the course that pursuing alternative X was a much better idea than working on AI safety.
- The course was designed to optimize participants' contributions during the course, but nobody optimized for what would happen afterward. As a result, the post-course period exploited its freedom to produce no outcomes at all.
- Memes spread through the course in ways that accidentally shaped participants' goals toward something other than pursuing AI safety.

### Scenario 3: We ran a course, and its graduates are working on AI safety projects, getting accepted into programs, etc., but their work is meaningless

**Possible reasons for failure:**

- Our selection process was broken, and we recruited people who couldn't be taught to make meaningful contributions within a single course.
- Our course was designed so that people could complete it without thinking, and as a result, they didn't actually learn anything.
- We didn't pay enough attention to getting people to think about impact and whether their actions were meaningful, and the field doesn't penalize failures in this regard quickly enough to prevent this outcome.
- We didn't properly think through our course's theory of change, so our efforts weren't optimizing for anything specific, and we ended up with essentially random outcomes.
- When we reframed problems, we stripped them of their substance.
- We taught people to Goodhart (or, more generally, encouraged them to optimize for something pointless).
- The outcome we're observing is the kind of outcome that's difficult to avoid by default, and we failed to apply enough effort to break away from the natural course of events.
- The course taught people fragmented pieces of knowledge that never came together into a coherent picture of what they should actually do to make meaningful contributions, rather than just doing whatever happens to work.
- Our course didn't generate enough engagement for participants to overcome the amount of resistance required to have an impact in AI safety.
- What people learned in our program doesn't connect to what they actually do (they make decisions using different frames from those they used when taking the exam).
- We underestimated how early we needed to start developing research taste and strategic thinking, and by the time we realized it, we were already too late.
- Participants never took the course seriously, so they didn't use what they'd learned to make decisions about their real lives.
- We're underestimating the quality of graduates' contributions because our source of evaluations is biased.
- The way we measure success incentivizes and selects for traits that trade off against the outcomes we actually want.
