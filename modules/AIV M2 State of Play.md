#### Text
content::
Module 1 left you with mechanisms and the ways around them. The obvious next question is the practical one: **is any of this actually available, or is verification a research programme with a press release?**

Three reports answer, and they agree more than you might expect.

- **Baker and colleagues at RAND** ask the hardest version — could the US and China verify *each other*? Their answer is six largely independent approaches, and the word *independent* is the design principle: choose layers that fail to different things, so no single one has to be the one that works. They also ask for something technical readers skip past: **protections against overreach.** A regime that can establish what a data centre computed is also a surveillance capability.

- **Harack and a large Oxford-led group** do something methodologically smart. Rather than asking what verification might achieve if research goes well, they assume things go *badly* — pessimistic technical and political parameters — and ask what survives. Quite a lot does. Regulating data-centre-based AI development looks verifiable within a few years if anyone seriously tries. Regulating AI inside mobile systems, such as weapons, does not.

- **Scher and Thiergart** supply the reframe that makes the gap between "what we want" and "what we have" much less damning. Verification is a *job*, not a gadget. Confidence can be bought with technology or with access — inspectors, declarations, records. Where the elegant mechanism is missing, enough access covers the same ground.

Notice where all three land. If much of this is already technically feasible, then the thing standing between us and a verified agreement is not physics.

Hold that thought, and hold its price too: access is intrusive exactly where states and firms refuse to be intruded upon. Trading technology for access does not make the problem vanish. It moves it from the physics budget to the political one — which is progress only if that budget has anything in it.
