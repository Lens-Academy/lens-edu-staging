duration_minutes:: 5
#### Text
content::
\## What this unit is for

By the end of this unit you will have a plan small enough to finish: who your project is for, what should change for them, and the smallest way to start. You bring it to Meeting 1 as a two-minute pitch.

\## In this unit

- Choose your track: Fit, or a field project
- Agree the AI use policy: use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker
- Browse a gallery of potential projects
- Learn why theories of change matter, then build one for your project
- Write your proposal canvas, ending in a one-sentence theory of change

\## How much to do

- **Minimum:** read the pages, and fill in your canvas during Meeting 1.
- **Core:** finish your canvas and your one-sentence theory of change before Meeting 1.
- **Stretch:** also book your first conversation with someone from your audience.

Any of these is fine. Showing up with a half-finished canvas is better than not showing up.

# Lens: Use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker
id:: 085752ae-82e0-439d-b542-45f836e9db63
tldr:: Use AI as much as you like for the work. The decisions, your argument and your voice stay yours, and you say how you used AI.
summary_for_tutor:: The Lens Projects AI use policy. The rule: use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker. Before each task, the learner asks whether it is practice (a skill they are here to build, done themselves with AI as a check afterwards) or leverage (a skill they have or do not need, where AI can be used freely). It lists what AI can help with and which decisions stay theirs, habits against handing off the thinking, and that every project card ends with one line disclosing how AI was used. The page ends with one question and the starting draft of the cohort agreement for Meeting 1.
duration_minutes:: 20
#### Text
content::
\## Why this matters

AI safety researchers use AI all the time, so this course does not ban it. But AI can also create an illusion of productivity: code piling up on your laptop, drafts that never get posted, the feeling of mastery without the effort of trying. A project only teaches you something if you do the hard parts yourself.

There is also a reason specific to this field. Handing your decisions to a model is a small, everyday version of the loss of human control that AI safety work tries to prevent. A course about AI safety should practise the opposite.

\## The rule

**Use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker.**

Before any task, ask: **is this practice or leverage?**

- **Practice:** a skill you are here to build, such as your stretch skill, your theory of change, your argument and your voice. Do the work yourself, and use AI as a check afterwards.
- **Leverage:** a skill you already have or do not need to own, such as boilerplate code, formatting, or a tool you will use once. Use AI freely.

Think of AI like a map app on a new route home. Use it the first few times, then less as the route becomes yours. For routes you will never take again, use it every time.

\## What AI does, and what stays yours

| AI can help with | You decide |
|---|---|
| Coding, debugging and running experiments | Which problem to work on, and for whom |
| Finding and summarising sources (then read the key ones yourself) | Which sources and results you trust |
| Red-teaming your plan and drafts (see below) | Your theory of change, scope and pivots |
| Explaining a concept you are stuck on | What you claim to understand |
| Editing for clarity and formatting | Your argument, your claims and your voice |

A quick test: if a decision would change what you publish under your name, you make it.

:::callout {title="What \"red-teaming\" means" tone="neutral"}
**Red-teaming** means deliberately looking for the weak spots in something, the way an opponent would. The term comes from military and security exercises, where a "red team" plays the attacker so the defenders can find their gaps before a real attacker does. In AI safety research it often means trying hard to make an AI system fail or misbehave. In this course it means something simpler: stress-testing your own plan, argument or draft before anyone else does. Asking "who would disagree with this, and why?" is red-teaming.
:::

\## Habits that help

- **Attempt first.** When stuck, write your own attempt for 10 minutes before opening an AI tool.
- **Ask for questions, not answers.** Have AI find holes in your plan rather than solve it.
- **Treat a rush as a signal.** When pressed for time, cut scope instead of handing off the thinking.
- **Disclose.** Your project card ends with one line on how you used AI, for example: *"AI helped write the plotting code and look for weak spots in my claims; the question, analysis and writing are mine."*

Your write-up is written by you, with AI for critique only. Some programmes, such as MATS, check applications for LLM-written text, and LessWrong moderates LLM-written posts. A piece in your own voice is also an honest writing sample.

feedback-instructions:: The learner has just read the Lens Projects AI use policy (use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker; practice versus leverage) and named one practice task and one leverage task. #### Text
content::
\## The cohort agreement

In Meeting 1 your group agrees a short set of norms. The starting draft:

- We follow the rule: use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker.
- We give each other honest, specific feedback, and we act on what we receive.
- We show the real current version each meeting, not an update about it.
- We disclose how we used AI on every project card.
- We count a published "why my idea changed" post as a success.

# Lens: Your project's theory of change
id:: 13128b53-1706-4b6a-a318-253402bfa1f4
tldr:: First a quick check on outputs versus outcomes, then map your own project as a chain from what you need to what changes.
summary_for_tutor:: Two parts. First, three quick choice questions on whether something is an output (a thing made) or an outcome (what changed because of it). Second, an interactive theory of change widget for the learner's own project, with a Get feedback button that asks you to read the whole chain. When reading it, check clarity, the weakest link between stages, outputs written as outcomes, and implicit assumptions. Never rewrite their boxes; the course rule is to use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker.
duration_minutes:: 45
#### Text
content::
\## Output or outcome?

Theories of change go wrong most often in one place: treating a thing you made as the change you wanted. An **output** is something you made. An **outcome** is what changed because of it. Three quick checks before you build your own.

#### Question: Choice
id:: a018aba0-b7f5-43f2-8786-80d9f4e88415
content:: A 1,500-word post explaining why one eval result no longer holds on newer models.
options::
- [x] Output
- Outcome
feedback-instructions:: In one or two sentences: the post is a thing someone made, so it is an output. It would become an outcome only if someone did something differently because of it. If the learner chose Outcome, name that difference plainly without lecturing.

#### Question: Choice
id:: 0b704f3d-db80-40c6-93f5-adf3d5451043
content:: Two members of a reading group change which paper they replicate next, after reading your post.
options::
- Output
- [x] Outcome
feedback-instructions:: In one or two sentences: people did something differently because of the post, so this is an outcome. If the learner chose Output, point out that nothing new was made here; a decision changed.

options::
- Output
- [x] Outcome
feedback-instructions:: In one or two sentences: on the Fit track, a changed decision about your own path is the outcome; the tasters were the outputs. If the learner chose Output, say that the tasters were what they made, and the decision is what changed.

# Lens: Your proposal canvas
id:: 5a72c0d2-90f1-4ccf-8f95-2336107786e8
tldr:: Four short boxes your theory of change did not ask, ending in one sentence you can say out loud in Meeting 1.
summary_for_tutor:: The rest of the Lens Projects proposal canvas, after the learner's theory of change widget: their audience's current situation, the smallest version they could make with what they already have, their stretch skill, and a one-sentence theory of change in a fixed template. Make each answer more specific; never write it for them. The course rule is to use AI as a tool, not a decision-maker.
duration_minutes:: 30
#### Text
content::
\## Your canvas

Your theory of change covered who you will reach, what you need, the change and your riskiest assumption. These four boxes finish the canvas. Short and specific beats long and general. After Meeting 1 this becomes proposal draft 1.

**On the Fit track?** Your audience is the people doing the work you are curious about, or the people who would select you for it. The change is what you will know about your fit by the end.

id:: 9a6ab806-6bff-4aaf-8d0a-182586f19631
content:: **Their current situation.** What do the people you will reach do now about this problem, and what is missing?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is filling in a project canvas for Lens Projects. Make the answer more specific; never write it for them. Under 100 words, at most two replies. If the answer describes a problem in general rather than what these people actually do now, ask what one of them did the last time they ran into it. #### Question: Open

If it depends on something they do not have yet (data, a mentor, money), ask what they could make without it. No generic praise. Do not grade.

feedback-instructions:: The learner is naming the skill they want to practise in their Lens Projects project. Under 80 words, one reply. #### Question: Open
id:: 3a3c242e-5c9d-4b08-899c-5c518041a5e8
content::
**Your one-sentence theory of change.**

*By [date], [audience] will [do or know X] because of my [artifact], which matters because [link to reducing AI risk].*
feedback-instructions:: The learner is writing a one-sentence theory of change in the template "By [date], [audience] will [do or know X] because of my [artifact], which matters because [link to reducing AI risk]." Make it more specific; never write it for them. Under 100 words, at most two replies. Check each slot: a real date, an audience specific enough to message, an observable change, a named artifact, and a link to AI risk that a sceptical reader could follow. Point to the weakest slot only, and ask one question about it. No generic praise. Do not grade.
