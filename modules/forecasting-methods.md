\## Where that leaves you

You started this module with a bad track record and three problems you were not expected to solve. In between you learned four things that are worth keeping.

Decompose until the parts have evidence sources. The test of a good decomposition is not that the parts are smaller, it is that you can say what would settle each one.

Say which reference class, and say what its denominator is. Most of the work in an outside view is done before anyone quotes a base rate, in the choice of what population the case belongs to.

The data does not choose the curve. Two points constrain a shape only after you have supplied a family, and the family encodes a theory of the system. Ask which interaction between the parts would break it, and which direction the resulting error runs.

How you combine estimates depends on what the estimators saw, not on what they said. Concurrence from separate evidence is worth more than the numbers show; concurrence from a shared source is worth less.

None of it makes you accurate. It makes you checkable, which is a different and more useful property, and the only one that improves with practice.

\## Go back and look

Open what you wrote in the Cold Open at the start of this module: the flights, the hospital operations, the three technicians. It is still in this module's chat, above everything you have done since.

The three tests you have taken, at the end of each section, were those same three objects in different clothes. That was the only honest way to find out whether anything changed. Read your old answers against what you wrote in the tests, and notice specifically what you would write instead now, and why. If the answer is that you would use better vocabulary for the same reasoning, the module did not work on you, and that is worth knowing.
