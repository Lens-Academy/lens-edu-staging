\## The problem before the AI problem

Before asking whether advanced AI is dangerous, there is an earlier question: what should we do with a risk that could be extremely large when we do not know its probability very well? This is not a problem unique to AI. Governments face versions of it when deciding how much to spend preparing for pandemics, nuclear war, climate tail risks, and technologies that have never existed before. Individuals face smaller versions whenever a decision could have large consequences but the evidence is incomplete.

AI makes the problem unusually difficult because several kinds of uncertainty arrive at once. We are uncertain about what future AI systems will be capable of, how they will behave, how quickly capabilities may change, which failures are plausible, how much weight to place on very distant consequences, and how much confidence to place in any particular model. Even people who take the subject seriously can end up with estimates that differ by orders of magnitude.

Two shortcuts are tempting. One says that because the probability is uncertain, there is nothing useful to say. The other says that because the possible harm is enormous, any nonzero probability makes the problem overriding. Neither shortcut is enough to make a decision. This module is about what lies between them.

The first half asks what makes some catastrophes different from others, and whether irreversibility matters independently of the number of people harmed immediately. The second half asks how very large consequences should interact with uncertain probabilities, weakly grounded estimates, model error, and consequences that are difficult to foresee. You are not expected to leave with a particular probability of catastrophe. The goal is to become better at identifying which assumptions matter and what evidence would justify changing your actions.
