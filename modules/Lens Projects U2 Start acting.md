# Lens: Talk to your audience
id:: 26af1de2-584a-4444-94a2-f19555bf117e
tldr:: How to learn from people without leading them: ask about what they actually did, not what they would do, and look for commitment rather than compliments.
summary_for_tutor:: Guidance on audience conversations for Lens Projects, followed by an open question where the learner drafts three interview questions, and an optional practice chat where you play a member of their audience. When reading their questions, flag any that ask about the future, about the learner's idea, or invite a compliment. Never write the questions for them.
duration_minutes:: 45
#### Text
content::
\## Why talk to anyone?

Your canvas is full of guesses. The fastest way to find out which guesses are wrong is to ask the people your project is for. One honest conversation can save you weeks of building the wrong thing.

\## Three rules for a useful conversation

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Ask how they deal with the problem now. If you describe your idea first, most people will be polite about it.
2. **Ask about specific past events.** "When did you last need this? What did you do?" is better than "Would you use this?" People are bad at predicting what they will do, and good at remembering what they did.
3. **Listen more than you talk.** If you are explaining, you are not learning.

\## What counts as a good sign

Compliments ("great idea!") tell you almost nothing. Look for **commitment**: they give you time, introduce you to someone, ask to see the next version, or share something real about how they work.

\## How to reach someone

A short message works: who you are, that you are doing a small project on their problem, and one specific question, or a request for 15 minutes. A reply to one message counts as a conversation.

\## Notes template

After each conversation, write down:

- **Who:** their role, and how they fit your audience
- **What they do now** about the problem
- **What surprised you**
- **Signs of commitment**, if any
- **What you will change** because of it

# Lens: Design one cheap test
id:: c2018cf0-1cc0-4b5a-b90c-763f260e44b6
tldr:: Turn your riskiest assumption into a small test with a clear pass, and decide in advance what result would make you change course.
summary_for_tutor:: A short page on designing one cheap test for the learner's riskiest assumption, with three open questions: the test, what result counts as a pass, and the pivot trigger. Make each answer more specific; never design the test for them.
duration_minutes:: 20
#### Text
content::
\## A test you can run this week

Your riskiest assumption is the belief that, if wrong, sinks your project. A good test of it is cheap, quick, and has an answer you write down **before** you run it.

Examples:

- **Technical:** run the smallest version of the experiment on one model and one dataset before building the full pipeline.
- **Policy:** send a one-page draft to two people who work near policymakers, and ask what they would cut.
- **Fieldbuilding:** post one session announcement and count sign-ups.
- **Fit:** spend two hours on a real task from the path you are curious about, and note whether you wanted to keep going.

The point of deciding the pass and the pivot trigger in advance is that you cannot talk yourself out of the result afterwards.

#### Question: Open

content:: **The test.** What will you do to test your riskiest assumption this week?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is designing a cheap test of their riskiest assumption in Lens Projects. Under 100 words, at most two replies. If the test would take more than a few hours or depends on something they do not have, ask what smaller version would still tell them something. Check it actually tests the riskiest assumption, not an easier one. Do not design it for them. No generic praise. Do not grade.

#### Question: Open
id:: b0d5fce3-fd64-4e7a-8d9b-22c252091c54

feedback-instructions:: The learner is setting a pass condition for a cheap test in Lens Projects. Under 80 words, one or two replies. If the pass cannot be observed or counted ("people like it"), ask what they would see if it were true. No generic praise. Do not grade.

#### Question: Open
id:: 40c30404-107e-4d22-abb5-33ad75b0e5e3
content:: **The pivot trigger.** What result would make you change your plan, and what would you change it to?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is setting a pivot trigger in Lens Projects: a result decided in advance that would make them change course. Under 80 words, one or two replies. If the trigger is so extreme it could never happen, ask what weaker result should still worry them. Do not choose their alternative plan for them. No generic praise. Do not grade.

# Lens: Your rough first version
id:: b203e6f6-ec31-4f4a-9288-7e139499a966
tldr:: Build the roughest version you can in one sitting, from what you already know, and set up a simple board to track the rest.
summary_for_tutor:: Guidance on building a rough first version in one sitting, and a simple three-column board (to do, doing, done). One open question asks what the learner built and what it taught them. Never build it for them.
duration_minutes:: 15
#### Text
content::
\## Rough is the goal

Set a timer and build the roughest version of your project you can in one sitting, from what you already know. Do not research first. A first version shows you what is missing much faster than a plan does.

What rough looks like by track:

- **Technical:** a notebook that runs end to end on toy data, with hard-coded values.
- **Policy:** a one-page outline with your main claim and three supporting points.
- **Fieldbuilding:** a draft agenda and a sign-up message, not yet sent.
- **Fit:** a half-page "what I have tried and what I noticed", plus the next taster you will try.

You will show this version at Meeting 2. Show the real thing, not an update about it.

\## A simple board

Make three columns anywhere (paper, a note, a free board tool): **To do**, **Doing**, **Done**. Put every remaining task on a card. Keep only one or two cards in Doing at a time.

#### Question: Open
id:: 937b6e17-5e8b-400a-a42d-eee31c7c5e52
content:: What did you build in your first sitting, and what did it show you that your plan did not?
feedback-instructions:: The learner built a rough first version of their Lens Projects project in one sitting. Under 100 words, one or two replies. If they describe plans instead of something made, ask what exists now that did not exist before. Ask what the rough version revealed that is missing or harder than expected. No generic praise. Do not grade.

# Lens: Proposal draft 2

#### Text

\## What changed?

Your first proposal was a guess. Now you have evidence: what people said, what your test showed, what your rough version revealed. Draft 2 should look different. If nothing changed, ask yourself whether you really tested anything.
