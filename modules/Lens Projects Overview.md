content::
\## What this course is

Courses teach you ideas. This course asks you to do something with them. Over five units you take one small project from an idea to a finished piece of work that someone else can read, use or respond to, and that you can link from an application.

You choose one of two tracks:

- **Fit**, if you are newer to AI safety and still working out where you fit. You try small pieces of real work, talk to people doing it, and finish with a career doc (which can stay private) or a career transition post.
- **Field project**, if you have done an intermediate course and want to build something in technical work, policy, or fieldbuilding.

Both tracks follow the same process. Only what you make differs.

\## Why these stages

Most courses leave you with knowledge: facts and arguments other people worked out. This course takes you two steps further.

- **Knowledge to opinion, through critical thinking (Units 1 to 3).** You read, look at real projects and talk to the people your work is for. Then you commit: a theory of change, a riskiest assumption, a decision to continue, re-scope or switch. An opinion is a stance you could defend, not a summary of what others think.
- **Opinion to voice, through creative expression (Units 4 and 5).** You turn your stance into something other people can use: a write-up, a post, a briefing, a career doc, a project card, a story you can tell in an interview. Voice is how you deliver what you think, in your own words.
- **The loop back.** It is not a straight line. When you test your idea with real people, what you hear often changes your opinion and sends you back for more knowledge. That is what Unit 3 is for, and it is a sign the course is working.

On the **Fit** track, the same steps apply to you: tasters and conversations give you knowledge, you form an opinion about where you fit, and your career doc or transition post is your voice.

\## The time it takes

Each unit is about 3 to 5 hours of work plus a 90-minute group meeting. The course runs as five weeks, one unit a week, or as one intensive week with a meeting every day. Readings are heaviest in Unit 1 and shrink each unit as your project takes over. Readings marked optional can be skipped.

\## The path

:::callout {title="Unit 1: Plan" tone="blue"}
Choose your track, agree the AI use policy, browse a gallery of potential projects, and write your proposal canvas: a problem, a specific audience, the change you expect, and a version you can make with what you already have. Meeting 1: two-minute pitches, and the group challenges your audience and your predicted change.
:::

:::callout {title="Unit 2: Start acting" tone="blue"}
Test your riskiest assumption with people from your audience, build a rough first version in one sitting, and revise your proposal. Meeting 2: show your rough version, not an update about it. Review partners are paired.
:::

:::callout {title="Unit 3: Re-scope" tone="blue"}
Decide whether to continue, re-scope or switch. This is the last point at which switching is allowed. Cut what will not fit, and hand your review partner a draft a stranger can read. Meeting 3: partners read each other's drafts live.
:::

:::callout {title="Unit 4: Polish" tone="blue"}
Write a review of your partner's draft against your track's rubric, revise from the review you received, and finish the work. Meeting 4: the group stress-tests a review point you rejected.
:::

:::callout {title="Unit 5: Share" tone="blue"}
Publish or share your work, check whether your prediction came true, write your project card, and practise telling the story in an interview roleplay. Meeting 5: three minutes each, then questions.
:::

\## How your work is read

Your project work is not graded. A few short knowledge checks give instant right or wrong feedback. You bring your work to each meeting, where your group sees it, and your review partner reads your draft. The AI tutor's job on every page is to make your answer more specific, not to grade it and not to do the work for you.
