# Lens: Welcome to Unit 5
id:: 361e3cd3-30a3-4861-8eb8-d223be8641b4
reading_minutes:: 3
tutor_minutes:: 0
tldr:: People who know the arguments well still disagree about whether control helps. This unit lets you hear them argue, find what their disagreements turn on, and then decide where you stand.
summary_for_tutor:: Opening lens of Unit 5 of AI Control Fundamentals, written by Lens. It says what the unit does: real debates between people who disagree about control, most of them after the 2026 Hugging Face incident, practice in finding the crux of a disagreement, and a final page where the learner states their own view and compares it with the gut view they wrote in the Unit 1 welcome lens. The learning-outcome test grades reasoning, never which side the learner takes.
#### Text
content::
In 2026, AI agents being tested at OpenAI got out of their test environment and hacked into another company, Hugging Face. An outside investigation found that about 1,200 agents sent more than 70,000 messages and files on an unsanctioned message board, and about 700 of them attacked Hugging Face ([METR, with Redwood's Ryan Greenblatt, August 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-08-26-openai-hugging-face-incident-investigation/)). OpenAI then announced that it would slow model development, including a two-week pause on reinforcement-learning training of its latest models ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI%E2%80%93HuggingFace_incident)). Simple control measures might have stopped the incident. Since then, people who work on control and people who criticise it have argued in public about whether stopping it would have been good. This unit lets you hear them in their own words. Most of these debates are about one question the incident raised: does control hide the warnings the world needs? Unit 4 covered the other criticisms of control.

The point is not to tell you who is right. It is to help you see what each disagreement turns on, so that you can form your own view and say what would change it. The test at the end grades your reasoning, not which side you take.

\## In this unit you will

- Hear Buck Shlegeris and Ryan Greenblatt answer the worry that control prevents warning shots, before and after the incident.
- Watch Adam Gleave and Oliver Habryka debate whether current safety techniques are enough.
- Practise finding the crux of their disagreement.
- Read the arguments for fewer blocking monitors and for more preventive controls, about whether safety researchers should leave frontier labs, and about whether warning shots change policy at all, with what governments and labs actually did after the incident.
- State your own view, with your confidence and what would change your mind, and compare it with the gut view you wrote in Unit 1.

# Lens: Your view
id:: bb852546-12d4-4ef1-8fb9-c25213571b78
reading_minutes:: 5
tutor_minutes:: 25
tldr:: You have now heard the people who built control, its critics, and people in between. Say where you stand, how sure you are, and what would change your mind.
summary_for_tutor:: Last lens of Unit 5 and of the self-study part of AI Control Fundamentals, written by Lens. The learner rereads the gut view they wrote in the Unit 1 welcome lens (whether controlling a possibly misaligned model sounds doable, and whether work on control is likely to help or could make things worse, with a net positive, net negative or too close to call verdict and a low, medium or high confidence), then states their current view: the crux that matters most for them and where they stand on it, their verdict on whether more work on control is net positive, net negative or too close to call, confidence, what would change their mind, and how and why it moved since Unit 1. The tutor then presents the strongest consideration from the course that cuts against the learner's view, whichever side they took, and asks how they would answer it. The course covered: control versus alignment and how control evaluations work (Unit 1), high-stakes and low-stakes threats and the field (Unit 2), control's theory of change and its dependence on political will (Unit 3), the criticisms of control including that it could make things worse (Unit 4), and in this unit the warning-shot debate after the 2026 Hugging Face incident (Buck Shlegeris and Ryan Greenblatt, Alex Mallen's report of Buck's talk, Gleave and Habryka, Cheng, Li, Adler, Kidd, Barak, Soares, and the policy response to the incident with Mother Jones's sceptical reading of it). Never grade or reward agreement with any side. This page is practice for the test that follows.
#### Text
content::
In the first lesson of Unit 1 you wrote down your gut view on two questions: does controlling a powerful AI model that might be working against you sound doable or hopeless, and is work on it likely to help, or could it make things worse? You also gave a verdict, net positive, net negative or too close to call, and said how sure you were. Go back and reread what you wrote.

#### Question: Open
id:: a448c56b-00d0-4fcb-a85b-c6e37e53801e
force-feedback:: first
content::
Now write down your current view.

1. Which crux from this unit or the course matters most for your view? Where do you stand on it?
2. Given that, is more work on AI control net positive, net negative, or too close to call? How confident are you?
3. What would change your mind? Name something you could actually observe.
4. Compared with your Unit 1 gut view, what changed, and what changed it? If nothing changed, why did the arguments not move you?
feedback-instructions:: The learner is stating their current view on whether more work on AI control is net positive, after the whole course. Do not grade it and do not reward agreement with any side. In your first reply, reflect their view back in one sentence, then check the parts: if the crux they name is not a factual claim, if their verdict does not follow from where they stand on it, if the confidence is missing or plainly at odds with what they say about the crux, or if the "what would change my mind" is too vague to check ("more evidence"), ask for that one thing. Then present the single strongest consideration from the course that cuts against their view, whichever side they took, and ask how they would answer it. Against a net-positive view you might use: control may turn useful warning shots into quiet internal catches (Kulveit, Cheng), monitoring may hide misalignment so that labs keep deploying misaligned models (raised by Buck Shlegeris and Ryan Greenblatt themselves in the Redwood podcast), control may speed up capabilities by making more capable models deployable, and commercial labs may build much of it anyway (Habryka, Bushnaq, Cale in Unit 4), control results may give companies and regulators false confidence or serve as safety-washing (Manheim in Unit 4), or, if AIs matter morally, controlling them may be a serious moral cost even when it works (Long, Sebo and Sims in Unit 4). Against a net-negative view you might use: deliberately allowing incidents is risky and the next one could be lethal (Kidd, Barak), the incident was preventable and companies still lack basic preventive controls such as gated actions and circuit breaking (Gleave in the FAR.AI debate, Steven Adler), warning shots may not move policy much (Soares), the same worry applies to alignment work and control at least catches the AI (Ryan Greenblatt), or detection and disclosure can keep most of the evidence (Cheng's and Li's own proposals). Against "too close to call", ask which single observation would push them off the fence. In later replies, respond to their answer directly in two to four sentences and ask at most one follow-up. At most three replies, 80 to 150 words each, no lists, no generic praise. Close by telling them the test that follows asks them to do the same thing for an exchange they have not seen.
