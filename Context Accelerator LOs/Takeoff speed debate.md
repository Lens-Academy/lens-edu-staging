Describe the fast or discontinuous and the slow or continuous views of AI takeoff, give the main arguments for each, and explain how takeoff speed changes the time and options for reacting.

## Requires
- [[Takeoff and intelligence explosion]]

## Helpful first
- [[Automated AI research]]

## Why it matters
How fast AI goes from roughly human-level to far beyond decides whether there will be warning signs, time to fix problems in weaker systems, and room for governments to act. AI is already speeding up AI research at some labs, so the question is live. Knowing which view a plan assumes shows where it fails if the other is right.

## Lenses
- [[../Lenses/AI Safety Atlas - Capabilities - Takeoff|Takeoff]]: maps the slow-versus-fast debate, speed versus continuity, overhangs, the automating-research loop, and why takeoff speed decides whether problems can be fixed as they appear (AI Safety Atlas).
- [[../articles/paul-christiano-takeoff-speeds|Takeoff Speeds]]: the case for a slow, continuous takeoff, from its leading proponent: why "slow" means AI changes the world sooner, the economic-doubling definition, his reply to the human–chimp argument, and what each view means for strategy (Paul Christiano, February 2018).
- [Hard Takeoff (LessWrong)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/tjH8XPxAnr6JRbh7k/hard-takeoff): the case for a fast, discontinuous takeoff, from its leading proponent: recursive self-improvement that either fizzles or goes "FOOM", the small human–chimp brain gap, hardware overhang, and AI doing AI theory (Eliezer Yudkowsky, December 2008).
- [[../Lenses/AIF - Software Intelligence Explosion|Software Intelligence Explosion]]: whether AI that fully automates AI research would set off runaway progress on fixed hardware, and whether limited compute for experiments would stop it; read the Summary and the section "You might need fast growing computing power to discover better algorithms" (Forethought, March 2025) (more advanced).
