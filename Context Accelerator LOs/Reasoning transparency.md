Write and judge arguments that readers can check: state your confidence, separate claims from the evidence for them, say what would change your mind, and represent opposing views fairly rather than as caricatures.

## Requires

## Helpful first
- [[Calibration]]

## Why it matters
Memos, posts and funding pitches about AI risk are read by people deciding whether to trust and act on them. When readers can see how confident you are and what your conclusion rests on, they can find the weak step and correct it, and they learn how much to rely on you.

## Lenses
- [Reasoning Transparency (Luke Muehlhauser, Open Philanthropy; EA Forum cross-post)](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/i9RJjun327SnT3vW8/reasoning-transparency): the standard guide to writing so readers can check you: lead with your key takeaways, say which considerations matter most, state how confident you are (with probabilities, since words like "plausible" are read differently), and say what kind of support each claim has (Dec 2017).
- [ITT-passing and civility are good; "charity" is bad; steelmanning is niche (LessWrong)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/MdZyLnLHuaHrCskjy/itt-passing-and-civility-are-good-charity-is-bad): Rob Bensinger argues the main skill is passing an "ideological Turing test", stating an opposing view as its holders would, rather than steelmanning or being "charitable"; one side of a debate about these norms (Jul 2022).
- [Epistemic Legibility (Elizabeth, LessWrong)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/jbE85wCkRr9z7tqmD/epistemic-legibility): why being easy to argue with is a separate virtue from being right, with examples of legible and illegible writing and a "How to be legible" section: name your sources, label what each belief rests on, and turn "probably" into numbers (Feb 2022).

- It is shorter, so readers have less room to misread it

#### Question: Open
id:: 87af7605-76b8-4d39-8138-e6eec6e26064
content:: Rewrite this sentence so a reader could check it: "AI might cause major problems soon."
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 100 for a rewrite that names a specific outcome, gives a probability or confidence level, and gives a time frame (e.g. "I put 20% on an AI system causing over $1 billion of damage in one incident by 2030"). 60 for two of the three. 20 for one. 0 if still vague.

#### Question: Open
id:: 4f65609e-9a6f-45f6-82cf-0529c88627c8
content:: You conclude in a memo that AI progress will slow over the next five years. Write one sentence saying what would change your mind.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 100 for naming a concrete, observable event or measurement that would reverse or weaken the conclusion (e.g. "If the next frontier models show large benchmark gains at similar cost, I'd drop this view"). 40 for something vague like "if new evidence came out". 0 for no condition.

#### Question: Open
id:: c969f7ac-5f4f-40e1-9fc2-4ab6f237b7a6
content:: A memo argues against concerns about AI risk by showing that chatbots won't "turn evil like in the films." Even if that is correct, why is this a weak argument?
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 100 for saying it attacks a caricature, not the view people actually hold (e.g. that capable systems could pursue goals their developers didn't intend), so refuting it says nothing about the real concern. 50 for calling it a strawman without saying why that weakens the argument.
