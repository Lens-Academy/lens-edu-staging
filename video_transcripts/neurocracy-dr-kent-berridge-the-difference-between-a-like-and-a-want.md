Yeah. Okay. So your work shows that liking and wanting are distinct processes in the brain, and in today's world of endless digital stimulus like Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook, how might separation explain why people keep scrolling or checking their notifications even when it seems repetitive?

Well, it can. It can because a lot of the internet and social media have features that tap into our incentive salience system of wanting, little nuggets of information, brief bits that catch our attention and invite us to go on, and it can detach from pleasure. But I have to say, when I got into the field, I never really expected that liking and wanting would be separated. That was a surprise to me, and it actually came out of some almost failed experiments that were initially disappointing.

Could you explain a little bit more about how you guys discovered that liking and wanting are distinct processes?

Sure. When I entered the field decades ago, most of us in neuroscience thought that dopamine, mesolimbic dopamine, was the pleasure neurotransmitter, and it's still famous as a reward neurotransmitter.

Right.

There was really a number of neuroscientists doing studies on that. But there was one champion above all who really led that hypothesis. That was Roy Wise in Canada. And he would show that if he gave dopamine-blocking drugs to animals, drugs that would block the dopamine receptors, so it's suppressing their dopamine, what happens is they would gradually abandon rewards. They would abandon food rewards and stop working for them and stop eating them. They would stop working for drug rewards like cocaine or amphetamine. They would stop working for sex rewards or social rewards or electrical brain stimulation, so-called pleasure electrode rewards. And it seemed as though they just didn't want those rewards anymore. And he inferred they didn't like them anymore. And it made sense. And he had some clever experiments to do it.

My first dopamine experiment was actually a collaboration with Roy Wise, and my goal was to add one more little piece of evidence on top of his enormous pile of evidence that dopamine was pleasure liking. But we were looking at it a little bit differently than previous studies had been doing. My approach uses natural behavior. It's called a neuroethological approach, where we have natural expressions. And if you have newborn human babies, sometimes their parents will give them little tastes of the food that the adults eat to see if the babies like it. If the baby smiles and licks its lips, or does the baby gape and shake its head and scrunch up its face. These are pleasure-displeasure reactions to the taste. Monkeys and apes have similar facial expressions. And even rats, which are omnivores like us, they like sweet and fatty and salty foods. They don't like bitter foods. They have some of those expressions, like lip licking, or gapes to bitterness. Lip licking to sugar and gapes to bitterness.

So we thought if we gave dopamine-blocking drugs, that would suppress the liking reactions, the facial expressions to sugar. And we did that, Roy Wise and I, as a collaboration, and it didn't change their facial expressions at all, to our disappointment. And I thought, well, maybe we just did the experiment wrong. So I repeated it with some other psychopharmacologists and got the same result. And then finally, with my colleague Terry Robinson here at Michigan, we said, "Well, maybe the drugs just aren't strong enough. Why don't we take away the dopamine system?" And it's possible to do that in animals. It happens in people with Parkinson's disease. The dopamine system dies, but it can happen in laboratory animals by microinjecting a neurotoxin into the brain that only kills neurons that have dopamine.

Right.

And all the other neurons remain. And so we did that. We eliminated 99% of the brain's dopamine. And we found still the rats had normal liking for sugar and disliking for bitter, and they could learn new likes and dislikes, but they never wanted to eat anything. They never wanted to drink anything. We kept them alive as you and I would be in a hospital intensive care ward, with artificial nutrition and hydration. That was the beginning of the notion that, well, maybe dopamine is not so much about the liking of rewards as it is about the wanting.

I didn't really believe it, though, until we started to turn on the dopamine system with microinjections of amphetamine in the nucleus accumbens that cause dopamine to be spilled into the nucleus accumbens, activating the dopamine, or electrodes that stimulate the dopamine neurons and make them fire and release their dopamine. And as we did that, we found that that always increased the wanting for rewards. Like, the rats would eat eight times more than they normally would, but it didn't increase their liking. If anything, it made them dislike the taste a little bit while they were eating more. That's when I began to believe that dopamine was really more about wanting than it was about liking.

Wow. Very interesting story.

So my next question is about appetite regulation, which is central to global health and especially relevant with rising obesity rates. From what you've learned about how appetite is controlled in the brain, do you have any idea of interventions that could be effective in helping people resist unhealthy food environments?

Well, the liking and wanting systems are very, very closely intermingled with hunger and satiety systems. It used to be that textbooks would separate that, that there'd be one chapter on brain reward and motivation, and there'd be a different chapter on hunger and satiety. But in the last 20 years, it's been clear that they really are absolutely interconnected. And in fact, one of the newest kinds of drugs that are helping people lose weight, the GLP-1 agonists, drugs like Ozempic and Mounjaro, GLP-1 and similar drugs that mimic natural satiety peptides. GLP-1 is a natural satiety peptide, but the agonists are artificial drugs that plug into the receptors for that. And it turns out that those drugs, they can act not only in the hindbrain and hypothalamus, where hunger and satiety circuits are, but they're also acting in the dopamine neuron ventral tegmentum, where the dopamine neurons live, and projections, and the drugs are also acting in the nucleus accumbens.

And it turns out that they are really reducing the wanting for food, and you can measure this in human fMRI studies, where people's limbic systems light up, the brain reward system lights up, to the images of food, especially when you're hungry, if they're palatable, tempting images. But when you take these GLP-1 agonists, it suppresses that. It doesn't suppress the liking for food. It just suppresses this mesolimbic wanting, this incentive salience for food. And that's one of the ways in which these drugs are helping people lose weight. Those same drugs are now being applied, tried out in studies of addiction, like alcoholism and such. And the initial results are promising. So it does look like this wanting system is relevant to appetite for foods, and it's relevant also to appetite for other things.

Yeah. So another relevant issue in the world is mental health, and many people today struggle with anxiety and depression, and they often describe a loss of motivation or blunted pleasure. So I wanted to ask, can this idea of liking and wanting shed light on what's happening in these disorders?

Well, there's evidence that it does. This evidence doesn't come from my lab or...

...my colleagues, but from other people who are studying these syndromes, as you say. Depression, schizophrenia, and Parkinson's disease patients when they're not taking their medication, all of these in psychiatry textbooks have been called to have anhedonia, the lack of pleasure in life, because the patients simply don't find anything really rewarding or motivating to work for. But when people have looked more closely in the last 10 years, especially for anhedonia in schizophrenia and Parkinson's disease, it turns out that a lot of that is not true anhedonia at all, but what's now being called avolition, a loss of motivation, a loss of wanting. Sometimes it's called anticipatory anhedonia, as opposed to consummatory anhedonia, or true anhedonia. It seems to be a loss of wanting selectively, and the liking is perfectly normal. For depression, major depression, it's a little more complicated, and it looks like some patients might also have this avolition but normal pleasure, but other patients with depression have true anhedonia. Well, I wouldn't say we have an explanation for risk-taking, per se, but what I would say is our work has shown that there's some sharing of the brain mechanisms for desire and fear in things like the nucleus accumbens. The nucleus accumbens is part of the reward system. So, it's famous for desire and pleasure. The desire, the wanting system of the dopamine system, it's the wanting that's temptations, the sight of food when you're hungry, things that catch your eye, consumer items that you want. That's that kind of wanting. The kind of fear that dopamine systems engage in is an active coping kind of fear. It's not so much the passive withdrawal and shrinking away, but if something's threatening and they're doing something to cope with that, that kind of thing. That system can flip back and forth between desire and fear in some situations. And it seems possible that people who are in roller coasters or in other things that are generating fear but also an exhilaration, that that kind of circuitry is flipping and generating both, with a positive tone for those who enjoy it dominating, through that kind of sharing of circuitry.
