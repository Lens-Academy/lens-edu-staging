All right. Hey everybody, I'll give you guys  a second to get settled down and then I'll get started. Broadly, I'm Aryan Bhatt. I lead the high  stakes control team at Redwood Research. I've been thinking about and working on AI control for two,  two and a half years now. Not as long as it gets, but pretty up there. I think we've made  quite a lot of progress since then, but there's still quite a long ways to go. In my  talk, I'm going to talk broadly about what I think the current state of the situation is, what the  current state of affairs looks like in the world regarding high stakes control, and what my plan  is for how we should proceed, what our priorities should look like as a field and as a community to  make things go well from now to the singularity.

I'm going to talk first about the current  state of affairs, then I'm going to talk about preparing for the future, and lastly I'm  going to give you guys my plan. Throughout this I'm recurringly going to be referencing the  dual notions of mitigation and evaluation. By mitigation I mean mitigations that actually  reduce risk from AIs that are colluding and conspiring against us. And by evaluation, I  mean evaluations not just of the AIs themselves, but of risk overall—evaluations of our current  situation. This involves evaluations of the AIs but also of the mitigations and also  of threat modeling, things like that. The current state of affairs in  terms of mitigation and evaluation.

If we look at the current leading  frontier AI companies and their plans, we have a reasonable number of mentions about AI  control. Anthropic's RSP talks about both what their plans are as well as what they recommend  for the industry. On the mitigation side they have their frontier safety roadmap, where they  lay out mitigations and techniques that might be necessary in order to, in the next few years,  keep us safe. Within that they have a bunch of control-flavored mitigations. They have a  few different security and control-flavored milestones that they're trying to hit. And they  also have a bunch of evaluation-related plans, most notably their risk reports and their  third-party review.

They have published already a few different risk reports on the  basis of their current models and they plan to continue doing so. This is archetypically  what I mean by this sort of evaluation. If we look at GDM, GDM's frontier safety framework  talks about residual risk assessments that they're going to write. They talk about how they're going  to plan to have mitigations in order to reduce risk from loss of control from misalignment. In  particular, they talk about limiting affordances, monitoring and escalation, auditing, and  just generally a bunch of control-flavored mitigations. They also have this evaluation  flavor of control-flavored evaluations, including assessing the  robustness of these mitigations.

The original conception of AI control was  as a conservative evaluation methodology to evaluate safety techniques. And although  there's been some changes to that broadly, it seems like we still have these  mitigations and evaluations in mind. OpenAI in their preparedness framework, which is  now a year old—Dylan's here wherever he is, and I'm sure we can get some sort of updated  information there—they talk about how they will write safeguards reports that compile their  mitigations and their evaluations of the state of affairs and of their mitigations. These also  reference a bunch of control-related mitigations. If you look at the actual  actions the companies are taking, which we'll get to in a sec, you get a little bit  of a view of where we are so far on the mitigation and evaluation front.

What do I say overall? It seems to me like the current situation in terms of AI companies and their plans is  that they say they'll try to mitigate loss of control risk. They say that they'll implement  mitigations, including control-style mitigations, in order to mitigate these risks. And they  also say they'll evaluate their mitigations as well as their overall security posture  and their overall level of risk imposed. To be fair, there aren't particularly any  mechanisms to ensure that these plans are going to be carried out or followed through  on. But I think that in the future we might have things like regulation or norms  or reasons to believe that these plans might in fact be followed through on.

One  thing you'll note is that there isn't much forward-looking text in these plans. They  definitely do some amount of forward-looking work. But I think that especially in terms of  concreteness of mitigations and evaluations, most things are pretty myopically focused. This makes sense. But I think it's a thing to really keep in mind as we think about  what our plan should be going forward into the future. How do we ensure that risk is low,  not just right now, but also into the future? Let's look at what the companies have actually  done. What is the current state of affairs in terms of what's actually been implemented? All  this work that we've done, all these plans that we've written, what have they actually amounted  to?

I think we can just look at the things that have been publicly talked about. Currently we  have a bunch of asynchronous monitoring, as people have spoken about a bunch at this conference. Multiple companies—OpenAI, Anthropic—publicly have asynchronous chain-of-thought-based monitors. The  coverage varies across AI companies, but broadly these monitors are looking for things  that might be related to loss of control risks. Currently the way these work is that we  have monitors that escalate to human review, perhaps with some AI assistance. And this is the  first step, I think, in control-style mitigations. We also are starting to see some real-time  monitoring, by which I mean monitors that run before an AI is even able to take  their actions and potentially block those harmful actions from being executed.

For example, Claude Auto Mode was recently released by Anthropic, which aims to  implement mitigation like this. We also have control-relevant capability evaluations. A  lot of the work being done externally to the AI companies is on building and developing control  environments that allow us to evaluate monitors, policies, attackers, all kinds of things in  order to do this evaluation, to understand what our current state of affairs looks like, how  much risk is imposed by the near-term models. I'd say the current state of affairs is we  have these monitors—we have asynchronous ones, we have real-time monitors, we have these  capability evals. We also have things that are less often considered core control measures, but I  think really do actually add a bunch of value.

For example, just standard cybersecurity measures. AI companies have passwords on their computers and all kinds of other very reasonable  mitigations, as well as a lack of some other reasonable mitigations, that make it harder  potentially for a miscellaneous AI to take over. I think another thing that's underrated  is the level of default human oversight that we currently have. This is a very  important part of the current reason for assurance that misaligned AIs couldn't do too  much damage—that humans just generally notice what their Claude agents are doing. And even  if you're not reviewing every line of code, which people aren't, it is reasonably obvious  at times when something goes horribly wrong, or if something is eating up large amounts  of resources that you don't notice, or whatever.

And I think this is a large  part of today's actual security systems. We also are starting to see some work on  sandboxing and containment. Some of this is just to prevent reward hacking or  prevent AIs from having consequences outside the bounds of their scope. But I think these measures are also incredibly relevant for AI control and we're  starting to see things like this crop up. I think that overall the current state of  mitigation has come a long way from a year or two ago. On the evaluation front, we have  different types of orgs doing different good work here. For the monitors that I just talked  about—both the asynchronous and the real-time monitors—we have static evaluations of these  things built by the outside world as well as internally at AI companies.

We have static  evaluations like MonitorBench where you just publish this and you get a final score  out for how good a monitor is. We have automated red-teaming tools. We also have  human red-teaming. METR recently did their embedded red-teaming exercise where they had a  human red team stress-test Anthropic's internal control measures and monitors. And I think  this is another great part of the evaluation framework that allows us to know how  well our techniques are actually working. We also do a bunch of threat modeling  both within AI companies—for example, within these risk reports that I've referenced—and  also externally at orgs like Redwood Research, METR, et cetera. I think that  this threat modeling is really, really important for understanding what the  current level of risk is, because it's really hard to know how likely you are to get screwed  if you don't know how you're getting screwed.

We also are starting to see more public  end-to-end evaluations of these types of things. We have for example METR publishing their  reports on the current state of monitoring and the current state of how good AIs are  at various long time horizons of tasks, as well as just directly stress-testing  things like monitoring systems. One thing we notably don't see is great public  evals of security or containment measures. I think perhaps these things privately exist internally at  AI companies, but publicly there's not much public state of knowledge on this. And we also don't have  many evaluations of human oversight or even any of the other more complex techniques I mentioned  earlier, things other than the static monitoring.

So we're at a good starting point. We're starting  to evaluate these mitigations. But I think, once again, there is quite a ways to go here. The place this leaves us in is that given the current state of mitigations and evaluations, I  think catastrophic risk in the near future—like for example in the next six months—is quite low. I think that we're not suffering large amounts of misalignment-caused catastrophic risk in the  near future, but this is mostly because current AIs just aren't capable enough. I think this is  a reason for happiness and joy, of course. But it does mean that we really, really, really need to  actively prepare to ensure this in the future.

I think that by default, it's not clear that the  current techniques or the current evaluation frameworks or the current transparency  measures or whatever will be sufficient in the future once AIs are capable enough to  potentially be misaligned or potentially cause harm if they are misaligned. And so we really,  really, really need to prepare for this stuff. I'm going to spend the next few minutes in  the talk talking about how we can prepare, and then I'll leave you off with my  final overall potential plan for what our priorities as a field should be  in order to be prepared for this. One good question to ask in terms of how to  be prepared to ensure this in the future is: what is the future likely to look like?

What is  going to happen by default? What isn't going to happen by default? What are we on track  to do versus not on track to do? I think it's the role of a field to continually  assess this and make sure that they're on track to make the future risks also  go well as well as the current risks. What are we on track to do? I think there are  some things that will happen by default, and I think that's great. This is a notable  improvement from the state of affairs a year ago or two years ago. Things could have been  much worse. I think that on the mitigation front, it's reasonably likely that we'll  have monitoring systems like today's, but increasingly deployed with broader  coverage, better improved monitors, et cetera.

On the other hand, there are things that  won't be developed or researched well enough by default—because this is the type of thing  that's only useful really if you have potentially colluding AIs. I also think that generically  across the board, preparation for quick or discontinuous loss of affordances that we're  currently relying on might be a bit rough. For example, we currently have access to monitorable  chains of thought. I think this is great and we should certainly make use of this affordance. But I think that myopia hits hard, especially in cases like these. And if it's the case that one  day we instantly lose access to monitorable chain of thought, or we start—over the course of a day  or a month or a few months—having large amounts of online training or continual learning, this might  totally change the situation in a way which makes some of our control techniques totally obsolete,  or maybe not obsolete, but they require a lot of adaptation.

And I think this is the type of  thing that we're not currently on track to do by default. So preparation for things like  this I think is pretty important as well. On the evaluation side of things, I think that  good public evaluations of these black-box techniques don't seem certain to me to happen  by default. I'm pretty pleasantly surprised by the current state of affairs. But things  can change. It's definitely plausible that even though the current RSPs or frontier safety  frameworks or preparedness frameworks say that systems will be subject to third-party review,  it's very plausible that in the future they won't be. And I think ensuring that this continues to  happen is really, really important for society to maintain their understanding of these risks, which  is really important for us mitigating the risks.

I think that in addition, any evaluation of  non-black-box techniques or lower-assurance techniques doesn't seem to me like it's  going to happen by default. It's really, really tough often to evaluate how well these  techniques are working. One of the reasons that we do AI control research is that we noticed  that a difficulty with alignment research is that it's really, really hard to know how  well your alignment techniques are working when you don't have access to the scheming  AI you're trying to apply them to. Similarly, with a lot of these lower-assurance techniques,  it's really, really unclear how well they're working. And so I think that we might be in  a situation where for a long period of time, we're really relying on these techniques  that form the bulk of our mitigations, but that we don't really even know how to  evaluate or don't know how good they are.

I think this is also a scary state of affairs and  it's not going to be solved by default. I think this will require a good deal of science and a  good deal of novel R&D in order to alleviate. I also think that more advanced threat modeling  might not be done by default, especially for superhuman systems. I think that threat modeling  in the subhuman regime is a notably easier affair. In particular, it is much more analogous to normal  human threat modeling for insider threats from humans or from other kinds of security threats. Whereas threat modeling for vastly superhuman systems might look radically different than any  sort of threat modeling we've done before.

And I think that we're not currently on track to  do a great job at this—not for lack of trying, necessarily, but just because it's a novel, hard  task. And so I think it's really important that we're able to do this threat modeling to  know, especially if we're trying to look into the future and prepare our techniques for the  future, what these future systems might look like, what the new threats there might be, and  how our techniques are likely to fall apart. That was a little bit of my assessment—not meant  to be fully exhaustive, but I think these are some of the things that won't happen by default.

And  I think we need to keep an eye out as a field in order to make sure that we are prepared  to tackle these challenges as they come up. Another thing I think we need to think about as  a field in terms of the future is what's going to change over time. I think there are a lot  of things where it's not a matter of happening by default or not happening by default—it's a  matter of the situation, the state of affairs, just changing such that we should reallocate our  labor, perhaps, or do things totally differently. For example, we might have vast amounts of AI  labor.

This is a huge, huge, huge deal. And it might mean that if in the future we are confident  that we're going to have vast amounts of AI labor that we can use to implement certain control  techniques or research certain control techniques, or even just use them within our control  techniques or evaluations or whatever it is—I think that this is a really, really big affordance  that we should really be thinking about. I think that another thing that we're going  to have to deal with is the fact that we're generally going to be far more uncertain about  the situation. If we set up processes that are great evaluation and transparency processes,  but that take three months to complete, this is well and good when we have months between  large model releases.

But if the situation is getting faster and faster and we're having any  sort of intelligence explosion or recursive self-improvement type dynamics, it might be  the case that in the future we have models being released every few weeks, or eventually  maybe every few days. At that point I think it's going to be way, way, way more difficult  to apply these techniques that we developed for a three-month regime. When you're living  in this weird future world where there's less time between model releases, you have all kinds  of confusing things where a model gets deployed and then undeployed and then they hot-fix a thing  and then there's like Claude 5.

And so I think we should also think about being prepared for these, especially on the  more policy-facing and advocacy-facing side. I also think that another thing that's  under-considered is that in the future we might be pretty confident that our models  are or aren't egregiously misaligned. If we are confident that our models are  egregiously misaligned at any point, that totally changes the situation. I would love  to see some amount of work—of course, you don't put all your eggs in one basket—but I think we  should prepare for the possibility that we'll know our models are scheming and come up with  some set of techniques, evaluation methodologies, et cetera that are tailored to the situation where  we know our models are scheming.

What can we do there? I think the mitigations look very different  and I think that the processes also should look very different. Similarly, if we're confident  that a certain level of model isn't egregiously misaligned, how can we use that affordance? I  think this is a huge thing that could really make a big difference in both your techniques, your  mitigations as well as your evaluations. And we should really be prepared and have plans to know  how we're going to adapt to this in the future. Lastly—and this isn't a catch-all term,  there are always unknown unknowns—but I think that you might have brand new threat  vectors crop up.

You might have to deal with completely different types of threats  that we're not currently dealing with because there are new economic forces at play,  or there are new crazy rapid developments in AI, or the game board just completely changes in some  way. And if all of our mitigations are targeted towards specifically three frontier companies  and these types of AIs at these companies and these types of control mitigations in particular,  I think we might be not very resilient to large changes in these things. Not saying that  everybody should drop what they're doing and work on specifically novel threat vectors,  but I do think that it's really important to think about how likely it is that we're going to  have to make some drastic changes in this way.

Two: we have  implemented the mitigations necessary to make that estimate low.
