So in the session we study five axioms, not four. Every axiom or every group of axioms gives you a little bit more. So we start from a preference. Preference means there are a number of possible outcomes and sometimes you prefer A over B. That's it. Nothing else. So a very general structure. First axiom is completeness. The first thing we would like to require is that whenever the agent is given two options, it will have a preference for one over the other. Second axiom is transitivity. So we would not like to have cycles of inconsistency. Like A over B, B over C, C over A. As soon as you embrace those two requirements, you can prove that your preference can be represented as the maximization of some function.

So there is a function that you measure is like a number that you attribute to every outcome. And then you can say that agent is maximizing that function. So that's a representation theorem that gives you a way to understand how the preferences of that agent are structured. And you just need those two axioms. If you have mixtures of events, so you don't have only events, but you also could have 50 percent of this and 50 percent of that, then you would like a bit more of structure. In particular, you will start asking yourself, how do my utility this function, this number that you attribute to events, relate with the weights of the mixtures.

And there, there are typically two extra axioms that you take. One is continuity. That means utility will vary continuously with the mixtures. More technically, it means if I have X I prefer over Y that I prefer over Z, there is a mixture of the extremes that is equal equivalent to the middle one. That's continuity. And then the fourth axiom, independence, is that if I prefer A over B and I do a mixture of A and B separately with the third component C, if I prefer A or B, I would prefer mixture of A and C over mixture of B and C. So by mixing with the same component both, I keep the preferences.

If you assume those four things, now you turn this number, this utility over the events into an expected utility. Which gives you much more structure. At that point you could be thinking the events that I'm weighting are trajectories over time. And at that point you would like to say, oh, but there is some temporal structure over this. And I would like my utility over a trajectory to decompose over the different points in time. And at that point there is this fifth axiom The way I weight value today and tomorrow is proportional to the way I weight the change in value of between a year and a year and one day.

So there's a proportionality. As soon as you also embrace that fifth axiom, you can decompose the utility over a trajectory over a sum of rewards of every time point with a discount that is this proportionality factor. So basically what you are doing is you start with a preference that is very unstructured. You assume two things, continuity and transitivity, and you turn that preference into an ordinal utility, a number that orders possible events. Then you assume two more things, continuity and independence. You turn the ordinal utility into an expected utility, which is much stronger in the type of interpretation you can get out of it. And then if you assume the fifth axiom, you turn the expected utility into an expected sum of future rewards.

And that brings you directly to the domain of RL. So what is nice is that you are now not simply maximizing the discounted future rewards because it works. You are doing that because you are assuming these five things and you think it's reasonable to do so.
