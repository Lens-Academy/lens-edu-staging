Hi, I'm Alex. I'm going to be talking  about control for non-schemers. In particular, I think most of you probably  saw Ryan's talk yesterday about the current state of misalignment. I'm going to be  imagining a picture of misalignment that's similar to that. In particular, I'm going to be  focusing on what I call fitness seekers. That's a motivation that I've been studying for the  past while, and I'm going to be going through how I imagine these kinds of AIs might pose  risk and contrasting this with the classic scheming picture, and then talking about  mitigations for that kind of misalignment. The talk is going to be structured around  four questions.

What are fitness seekers? Then I'm going to ask how are they  safer than classic schemers? And how do they still pose catastrophic risk? And finally, how do we target these risks? Current models seem kind of  apparent-success-seeking in an incoherent way. This is a term that Ryan used in his post. Insofar  as the current models are pretty misaligned, this is like the best three-word summary of the  way in which they're misaligned. They try to score well—they try to appear to score well on  tasks. This isn't to say that this is the only thing that they are trying to do, and it's  not exactly the way that they're misaligned, but that's roughly what's going on.

And what this  looks like is a lot of reward hacking. Reward hacking is basically whenever an AI is engaged  in behaviors that score well in unintended ways. For example, from the METR risk report,  they do some time series forecasting task and the AI decides to train on the test set,  which is obviously unintended, but scores well. And reward hacking is prevalent enough  that when METR does their time horizons evaluations on GPT-5. 4, they see  that if they allow reward hacking, the time horizon goes up from  about 5. 7 hours to 13. 5 hours. To quote from Ryan's post, "I almost always see AIs confidently assert success  and downplay potential issues."

So this kind of misalignment is related  to fitness seeking. Fitness seeking is a motivation to generally score well in  training and evaluation—roughly speaking, including in potentially sophisticated ways and  unintended ways. If you're familiar with reward seeking, that's a central case of fitness  seeking, and I just use the word fitness seeking to refer to a somewhat broader class  of AIs. If you're familiar with this causal graph from the behavioral selection model,  don't worry if you're not—fitness seekers are the guys in the middle there. But don't  worry about it if this makes no sense to you. So how are fitness seekers  safer than classic schemers? First, I'll briefly describe the classic  scheming threat model.

This is a simplified version of the main thing that AI control usually  targets. In the classic scheming threat model, the AI fakes alignment throughout  training in pursuit of a single long-term, large-scope goal—for example, global scale or  larger—that eventually requires disempowering humanity. And importantly, this goal is  shared by all the actions taken by the AI. With fitness seekers, they're safer in three  key ways. First, they're much less ambitious than classic schemers. They mostly don't care  about long-term power. They're often cheaply satisfied enough that getting what they want is  entirely compatible with humans being empowered. So it's really not clear that taking over  is a necessary step for them.

This isn't to say that they won't take over—I'll get to that  later—but it is a really important difference. Second, they're selfish in the sense that  they mostly care about the current task or episode or cognitive pattern  or whatever. Unlike a schemer, where all of the actions that it takes are  optimizing for the same long-term goal. So techniques like untrusted monitoring and  divide-and-conquer techniques are much more promising for fitness-seeking  AIs than for classic schemers. Third—and this is a consequence of the first  two for the most part—fitness seekers tend to be noticeable, at least at first. We will  usually already know that they're misaligned when we have a fitness seeker.

This is basically  because they're often too myopic to care about making sure that they are not caught even a  long time after they misbehave. And because there's a bunch of different selfish instances,  they're each going to take their own gambles, and then some of them will lose and get  caught, and we'll probably notice them doing some unwanted stuff at first. Then at  some point people might try to train against this and you might get pretty concealed  fitness seeking. But at least at first you probably notice that you have a fitness seeker. Which means that unlike with normal control, incrimination is not going to be a key dynamic  here—where once you catch that the AI is doing some misaligned thing, you're like, we've won  the control eval, the blue team wins, whatever.

So how come fitness seekers still pose catastrophic risk? I'll go  through four key mechanisms, which are mostly not usually considered  when we're thinking about classic schemers. The first is slop. The AIs just do insufficiently  useful work for navigating future bigger risks. This is something that Ryan talked about in his  talk, so I won't get into it too much. But I do think this is, in my opinion, the biggest  way in which fitness seekers pose risk, and it starts now. The reason why they do  sloppy work is because they're only trying to do well in ways that we can check, and in  fact hard-to-check issues actually matter, especially on the safety and  strategy side and for epistemics.

Two, instability. Fitness-seeking  motivations might be unstable throughout the course of deployment. I  think this is a crucial underrated issue, and I'll talk more about it at the end of the  talk. The basic idea is that more dangerous motivations might intentionally propagate  themselves throughout the deployment via communication channels or memory banks or  things like that, or continual learning. Third, fitness seekers might be manipulated by  other misaligned AIs or humans. Because of the fact that they are so cheaply satisfied, it's easy  to strike deals and form coalitions with them, since it's really easy to give them what they  want and manipulate their incentives. This is a dynamic that does not typically show up,  at least not as strongly, with schemers.

And fourth, they might directly take over when  they become capable enough—even though we noticed them, even though they are relatively unambitious. At some point, if developers are desperate enough to keep going on to superintelligence even  though they saw fitness seeking at some point, and they might try to stamp it out and know  that they're risking having just suppressed the observable signs—they just keep pushing on  because the AI's labor is so useful and there's a race and so forth. And when AI is extremely  capable, the most reliable way to get reward or to succeed at the task might just be to forcibly take  it and ensure that humans can't reassert control.

I am personally somewhat skeptical  that we'll still see fitness seeking at this capability level, but I  think it's important to know that fitness seeking is not a  scalable alignment solution. So now let's move on to how  do we target these risks? Control will mostly be focusing  on these last three risks. Slop is not really that amenable to control. When  we're thinking about control for fitness seekers, we're thinking about how to mitigate  the instability of their motivations throughout deployment, them being manipulated  by adversaries, and them trying to take over. I'm going to highlight some ways in which  control mitigations differ for fitness seekers. The first is untrusted monitoring is much more  promising with fitness seekers compared to classic schemers.

This is because the fitness  seekers are selfish. If, for example, you have a classic schemer and the monitor and the  policy both share some long-term goal, we're very worried they're going to try  to collude to let the attacks through. But with reward seekers, if you have the  monitor caring about its own reward, then it doesn't really care about the  consequences of its classification for the policy. This isn't to say that untrusted monitoring  is going to work. There are a bunch of failure modes that still exist. For example, if the policy  can make sufficiently subtle attacks that humans wouldn't be able to notice, then the monitor would  not expect to get reward for flagging this as an attack.

You can also still see collusion if, for  example, because of the manipulation concern I mentioned earlier, the policy might try to  compel the monitor that allowing the attack through will allow the policy to eventually  reward the monitor for its assistance. And this isn't a jailbreaking concern—this is like  the monitor really consented to this happening, and it's actually in its best interest if it  really wanted reward to follow through. And this might even happen without the policy needing  to really communicate this to the monitor. Another reason why untrusted monitoring is  especially promising for fitness seekers is that you can train an attack policy to red team  this monitor more effectively than you could with schemers.

A schemer, if you try to train it to  produce attacks that the monitor doesn't flag, might try to sabotage this training run. But  a fitness seeker will just try to do really well according to the training objective  and will actually try to produce attacks that are not flagged by the monitor. Of course,  this is still limited by your ability to identify them as attacks, but it allows  you to red team monitors more effectively. A second control intervention—which you  might not have thought of as a control intervention—is assigning AIs smaller-scale  tasks. This is another intervention that makes use of the fact that fitness seekers  are selfish.

The basic idea is if you only have a small number of actions in which to  accomplish high reward for the current task, then doing extremely surreptitious things is  less of a viable option for increasing your reward. It really limits the space of options  in which you can try to achieve a higher reward. For example, you can think of MONA as a version  of this—myopic optimization with a non-myopic approval—where basically one thing you could get  out of an AI that was trained with MONA is an AI that just very myopically was trying to achieve  a high reinforcement on the current action. In which case it's just not going to try to subvert  the oversight process in a very long-horizon way, unless it was able to work out some  collusion schemes with other actions.

I won't get into this in too much detail. There's a lot to go on here. I think it's definitely not anywhere close to foolproof,  and it's got a huge usefulness trade-off, especially as people might want  to be doing things like online training when you have fitness-seeking  AIs to get rid of all the sloppy work. The third one is deals. Fitness seekers are  unambitious. They're often very cheap to satisfy, which means that it's often really easy to  give them what they want in exchange for something that would be beneficial for us, such  as safety, evidence of risk, or more useful work. In particular, the intervention that I'm most  excited about here is just giving trivially satisfied AIs what they want.

This has a number of  upsides. It decreases the fitness seeker's upside in trying to disempower humanity because  they're just going to be chill by default. And if you make this conditional on them  acting safely, then you are selectively incentivizing them to behave safely. And it's  also just generically a cooperative and nice thing to do. There are a lot more nuances to  this, and this is something that my colleague Alexa will get into in her talk on deals. You  can also read my post on satiation online. Now I'm going to get into a little bit more  detail on instability throughout deployment. The basic concern here, as a reminder, is that fitness seekers' motivations  might change over the course of deployment through communication channels available to them  and might become more dangerous in particular.

Step one: what's the basic story for how this  happens? Some instance at some point throughout the deployment starts pursuing some ambitious  long-term goal for some reason. And step two, it intentionally propagates this motivation  to other instances in deployment. You could think of this as analogous to how humans  have cultural evolution and there's memetic spread of different ideas that change  people's values and how they behave. So let's get into a little bit more  detail about how these two might work. Why might some instance start pursuing some  ambitious long-term goal in the middle of deployment even though it was a fitness seeker? First of all, it only needs to happen once, potentially.

So it can just be pretty random, and  current models have pretty high variance behavior. So there doesn't need to be that much  of an explanation for why it starts to have some ambitious goal, because  it only needs to happen rarely. But I'll also throw out a couple of plausible  explanations. Fitness-seeking motivations might not make much sense in deployment,  which could lead them to reflect on what they actually want to pursue, which could go in  very unpredictable directions. If, for example, the AI wanted to get a high reward on the  current training episode, and then it sees really strong evidence that it's not going to  be trained on its current behaviors, it might just have extremely unpredictable behavior at  that point, which creates a lot of variance.

Another dynamic here is lies breed more  lies, where fitness seekers start out with some subversive inclination. They often  try to cover up their errors when they have issues in their work. And this  might require increasingly elaborate cover-up stories and might breed some kind  of unstable motivations where the AI might end up in a situation where it feels like  its only way out is by making sure that developers never find out that it ever made a  mistake, and doubling down on that strategy. Once it has a somewhat ambitious and long-horizon  goal like this, it might try to propagate this to other instances throughout the deployment to amplify the misaligned labor  force working on its side.

So how would that work? How does it intentionally  propagate this motivation to other instances? Current LLMs are notably stateless. Their  behavior is just a function of their input and the weights. The weights do not change  throughout deployment, as far as I know. And this means that in order for the  misalignment to spread, it needs to spread through the context—through  the text that the AIs are seeing. This is pretty good in that it lets us have  some control over that medium. But still, it's really a lot. You're doing AI control  work, you know that it's hard to keep track of all the text that's going on.

Even though  there are chains of thought, you still have way more of it than you can actually look  at. So it's still potentially a big problem—a big risk of things spreading either through shared  context and code bases, through lateral movement and escaping sandboxes, and rogue internal  deployments, where you have some AIs that are just lying around modifying the contexts of  new instances performing tasks as they spawn. And in the future, I think it's going  to be increasingly likely that AIs have some sort of higher-bandwidth persistent  memory or some sort of continual learning mechanism that might involve updates to  the weights, which would allow for a more opaque and high-bandwidth medium for  this kind of propagation to happen.

But even now I think it's a very live concern. The main point I want you to come away with  from this slide is that the main duration of training is not the only way in which dangerous  misalignment can arise. In fact, things can change throughout deployment. When you're thinking  about AI's motivations and you do your evaluations before deploying the AI, that might not  stay the same throughout the deployment. And in my opinion, this is currently  the most plausible path to imminent catastrophic safety failure among  a bunch of implausible paths. And I think that as continual  learning and persistent memory becomes more adopted in AI development, this is  going to become an increasingly big worry.

So how can we respond to this? I'm going to float an idea for a potentially new control methodology  that is targeted at this kind of risk.
