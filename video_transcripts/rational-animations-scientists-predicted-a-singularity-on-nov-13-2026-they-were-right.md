If Charlemagne, in the year 800, had the data to trace human population growth through history, he would have predicted humanity would reach a singularity between the 20th and 22nd centuries. If Elizabeth I of England had access to the same data in 1600, she could have guessed it would occur during the 21st century, and Napoleon in 1800 would have been able to guess that it would be in the first half of it. In 1960, Heinz von Foerster and two co-authors actually ran the numbers, and by extrapolating from history, they predicted the human population would reach infinity on exactly November 13th, 2026. Now, the paper was at least in part humorous.

After all, the human population can't literally grow to infinity, and November 13th, 2026 is far too specific a date. Moreover, right after the paper was finished, population growth started to slow down, so the population singularity was called off. Or was it? The population growth the paper tracked was hyperbolic, meaning that the human population across history doubled in size in shorter and shorter amounts of time. According to von Foerster, this is possible thanks to humanity forming a single coalition against nature, instead of remaining in a dog-eat-dog game against each other. Part of the reason is technology advancing. More people in the population means more new ideas and technologies that enable even more people.

But modern society is great at inventing new things. So why did the trend stop? In recent decades, as nations have gotten richer, fertility rates have declined. People started having fewer children. This is called the demographic transition. So although humanity continues to invent new technologies that make everyone better off, fertility hasn't kept pace. If, instead of tracking population growth through history, we track economic growth, we see a similar story. Up through 1960, the world economy grew hyperbolically, doubling faster and faster. Now, it grows at about 2% per year. It's still extremely fast, but the trend has leveled off. It's just exponential rather than hyperbolic. To see why, we need to look back at population growth.

For most of human history, economic growth and population growth were closely tied. Before the 1700s, by far the main way the economy could grow was by adding more people to the workforce. More farmers make more food. But since the start of the Industrial Revolution, things began to change. Economic growth now also comes from automation, replacing human labor with steam engines or motors or robot arms. Essentially, instead of making children, we started making machines. But this wasn't enough to offset the demographic transition, because machines can only do some of what humans can do. A robot arm can substitute for a human worker, but it can't come up with new ideas and inventions.

Even if we make more machines, the pace of innovation remains the same. So if human population growth slows down, it makes sense for the economic growth trend to also slow down. So, going forward, we can expect technological progress and economic growth to proceed at this relatively slower pace. Or can we? There is one invention that can get us back to hyperbolic growth. Artificial intelligence. If machines can think at least as well as humans, then machines can increase the pace of innovation. And making more machines can substitute for human population growth. At least economically. An explosion of innovation. This isn't pure speculation. AI is speeding up innovation right now.

Today's AI systems are able to write their own mathematical proofs and complete software tasks that take humans entire work days. AIs aren't innovating nearly as fast as humans are, at least not yet. But humans are already using AI to innovate faster than we could on our own. Look at how quickly programmers started using Claude Code, for example. And the AI labs like Anthropic and OpenAI are using code written by their own AIs to monitor new training runs, conduct experiments, and create new products. AI is speeding up AI progress itself. AI research still relies on human researchers, but they can now work much more efficiently. And writing code to test and deploy new algorithms isn't the only way AI is speeding up progress on future AI models.

To train a model, you need computer chips, training data, and algorithms to combine the two. For data, there's what's called synthetic data, where the output that one AI produces using lots of extra compute becomes basic training data for the next AI. AI systems are also helping design better computer chips. Google says it designs its state-of-the-art processors using an AI system called AlphaChip, though it's not yet clear if AlphaChip is actually better than humans or other algorithms. But either way, it's a step towards AI being able to fully automate further AI progress. And with hyperbolic growth, what starts as a small boost to AI developers could quickly become a tidal wave of AI-enabled discoveries.

Von Foerster's model measured population growth in humans. For AIs, the equivalent might be how many copies of the best AIs we can run, and at what speed, which in turn depends on how much raw computer power we have, and how efficiently our AI systems can use it. Training an AI model takes weeks or months at the most powerful data centers in the world. But running a model that's been trained already only takes a few seconds to a few minutes, even if it has to reason through a complicated task. Every time a cutting-edge AI model gets trained, it can quickly become an entire workforce. So when we build a new AI system that's more efficient with the computing power we already have, or an AI system that produces better quality results than its predecessors, that boost gets multiplied many times over.

Working in parallel. Since part of the growth model involves copying new AIs onto existing hardware, we need to know how well AIs can work in parallel. If it turns out that a million copies of an AI can't innovate much faster than a thousand copies, then AI population growth matters less. At some point, the bottleneck to progress isn't just the number of AIs you can put to work on the problem. In a 2025 report, Forethought Research reported on interviews they conducted in March 2024 with five AI researchers from leading companies. Forethought asked them to evaluate what would happen if each employee at their AI company was replaced by 30 digital copies with the same skill set and the ability to think 30 times faster than a human.

The researchers said that the main bottleneck would likely be compute to run experiments. Ideas that look promising on paper need to be tested in experimental training runs. One researcher said that computers are already a bottleneck to experimentation because their AI company has a backlog of experiments to run. Those experiments wouldn't go any faster for these hypothetical AIs than they would for humans. Still, AIs could speed up research in many ways. They could choose experiments which are more likely to give useful data and get more insights from them. They could also focus on ideas that require smaller experiments, which are normally bottlenecked by how fast researchers can write code.

Additionally, the AI copies could catch more bugs and allocate resources across the company better. The interviewed researchers estimated that the pace of AI progress in this scenario could be anywhere from about twice as fast as it is today to 20 times faster, but they were very uncertain. Moreover, the compute bottleneck isn't absolute. In the short term, we could still make more hardware for AI. AI companies are building quickly already, but as a fraction of GDP, they're nowhere close to the biggest industrial efforts in history, like countries gearing up for war. That level of effort would be difficult, but at least there's precedent. And in the longer term, AI can speed up the process of chip manufacturing too.

But even if we can't get more compute quickly, hyperbolic growth might still happen anyway. An AI inferno. Scientific fields in general seem to have diminishing returns. New discoveries are harder to find than old ones. In the 1600s, Galileo could discover new physics by timing balls rolling down wooden ramps. In the 1800s, you just needed a small lab. In the 2000s, we need multibillion-dollar particle colliders and giant telescopes. AI probably works the same way. As AI gets better, new improvements will be harder and harder to find. On the other hand, more powerful AIs will get better at finding these difficult advancements. So we have one effect slowing down AI progress and one effect speeding it back up.

If the diminishing returns are the stronger effect, then new AI breakthroughs will become rarer and progress will depend more on hardware. But if AI improves faster than the returns diminish, breakthroughs will happen faster and faster. It's like starting a fire. If a small flame doesn't produce enough heat to keep its own fuel burning, it sputters out. If it has plenty of heat, it can quickly turn into an inferno. If the acceleration is strong enough, AIs' power might reach hyperbolic growth just through improving their own algorithms and generating their own data without any new hardware at all. This scenario is called a software-only singularity, and it would mean AIs could get very powerful very quickly, skyrocketing past human level in a matter of months.

To be clear, a software-only singularity could be incredibly dangerous. With things progressing so fast, we'd have almost no time to try to ensure the resulting AIs are aligned. And those AI systems would have architectures no one's ever seen before, so even our best existing alignment strategies might completely fail. But it is a plausible scenario. The AI Futures Project, which also published AI 2027, later released an updated mathematical model of AI takeoff. Under its assumptions, about 38% of simulations show what the authors call a taste-only singularity, where the main driver of accelerating research progress is the AI's ability to choose fruitful research paths and interpret the results.

The authors think this will be the main factor in a software-only singularity. Separately, Anson Ho and Parker Whitfill at Epoch AI also found a software-only singularity to be plausible in their own analysis, though they stressed that the data we have is weak and we need much better experiments. The Industrial Explosion. Intelligence is only one side of the problem. Once AI is smart enough to innovate like humans do, physical industry could start accelerating too. Tom Davidson and Rose Hadshar at Forethought Research called this process the industrial explosion in a research note they published in May 2025. They argue that automation in the physical world will initially proceed slower than progress in AI capabilities.

So at first, AI will be great at designing factories and coordinating labor, but it won't be able to directly act in the physical world. Because of this, they expect the industrial explosion to happen in three phases. First, AIs will direct human workers to perform tasks more efficiently. Then, robots will do the tasks in fully automated factories. And eventually, the AIs will switch over to something even more efficient than factories as we currently know them. Perhaps even nanotechnology. Humans have spent centuries optimizing our factories, but we're still not perfectly efficient. A powerful AI system could pay close attention to every worker at once, giving each worker specific, detailed instructions on how to move and what to look at in real time, rather than just high-level directions.

At the same time, it could understand and optimize the factory as a system, smoothing out even small inefficiencies. Forethought estimates AI could speed up human industry by a factor of 10, by making everyone as productive as the best workers in the best firms, and by reallocating more workers to physical labor. But this is only the beginning. Human physical labor will steadily be replaced by robots. At some point, factories will only need occasional visits from humans, and then none at all. Entire production chains will run without any human input, turning raw materials into feedstock, then components, then entire finished products fully automatically. And one of the main things they'll be producing will be more machines and robots to expand industry even further.

After all, the factory must grow. Here, Forethought estimates that, once robot production becomes autonomous, the stock of robots and physical capital in the world might initially double on the order of a year. And importantly, that doubling time will get shorter and shorter. Hyperbolic growth. How all this economic output affects humans would depend on a lot of factors. In an ideal world, everyone would be able to reap the benefits, and we'd all have abundant material wealth and the freedom to do what we please, whether that's projects we find fulfilling, or just lounging around all day. But that's not the default outcome. We could easily see that wealth and power concentrated in the hands of a few, like totalitarian rulers.

Or, if AI is gravely misaligned, it might just wipe out humanity once it's done with us. We've got plenty of other videos on these topics, if you'd like to hear more. However it happens, robot factories aren't even the upper limit. Imagine this. A tiny capsule, just a few millimeters across, lands in a spot with suitable resources. It unfolds small solar collectors for power, and starts to mine raw materials from beneath the surface, and skim gases from the atmosphere. Using microscopic machines, it builds these resources into more solar collectors, more mining equipment, and a superstructure with complex signaling and plumbing, all following a blueprint stored in the capsule's memory banks.

Step by step, it constructs a tower hundreds of feet tall, capable of mass-producing and distributing more tiny capsules, just like the one that started the whole process. That is not science fiction. That's a tree. Factories, even fully robotic ones, aren't nearly as sophisticated and efficient as biology is. Cells operate on the molecular scale directly, manipulating proteins mere nanometers across. If AI really wants to build, it will operate on that same nanometer scale. Who knows what it could do then? Our future. There's no guarantee that the future will look exactly like this. A software-only singularity is only one possibility. There's still a reasonable chance that AI capabilities grow more gradually, or even pause for a while.

And for industry, even though the broad path from humans to robots to nanotechnology seems natural, we don't know how long it will take, if it does occur. But if we continue down the current path, it looks like AI progress is just starting to feed back into itself. Even if this is only the next phase of automation, that'd be huge. But if the feedback loops run deeper, AI will grow faster than exponentially, and be the biggest thing to ever happen in history, for good or for ill. So maybe the world won't end on November 13th, 2026. But because of AI, the world could be upended soon.

And we're not just being hyperbolic. Hyperbolic growth, software-only singularities, and the AI industrial explosion are each deep topics.
