#### Text
content::
**Design for the hardest case.** Verification strong enough to support a full pause (chip registries, compute metering, inspection rights) supports every weaker bucket for free. The reverse is not true. That is why this track studies verification against the pause, even if what gets signed first is transparency.

#### Question: Open
id:: fbdb0b00-1038-4943-a043-8c2e8d8242be
content:: **Stakeholder map** (Map, about 800 words, peer reviewed)

For a hypothetical pause treaty, place every relevant actor on the supply chain, annotate each with its most likely incentive class and the leverage it holds, and mark the two or three actors whose defection would collapse the regime.

Reader: The drafting team for a hypothetical pause treaty.

This output is a map, not a memo: build it as annotated rows, not paragraphs, carrying what the brief above asks each row to hold.
assessment-instructions:: Score out of 100. 25: coverage: the actors span the supply chain from equipment and fabrication through chip design and cloud to labs and downstream deployers, plus the states involved: signatories and at least one state outside the treaty that could host the activity. 25: each actor carries an incentive class (comply, defect, hide, exaggerate, free-ride) that fits what it stands to gain or lose under a pause. 25: each actor carries the leverage it actually holds (a chokepoint, records, a jurisdiction, the power to interrupt a job). 25: two or three actors are marked as regime-collapsing, 10 for the choice and 15 for a reason that follows from their position and leverage rather than their size; any defensible set earns full points. A row that names an actor without an incentive or a leverage earns nothing for it under that criterion. Rows, not prose, is the intended form. Give credit for each point whenever the answer shows the idea, in any wording. Model answer, for the feedback, not a grading checklist: "ASML (lithography equipment, Netherlands): comply; leverage: sole supplier of EUV tools, can stop shipments and servicing. TSMC (fabrication, Taiwan): comply; leverage: most leading-edge chip production, order and production records, can refuse orders. NVIDIA (chip design): exaggerate or free-ride; leverage: designs and firmware, can ship chips with attestation and metering, holds shipment records. Cloud providers: comply; leverage: run the hardware, hold customer and usage records, can interrupt jobs. Frontier labs in each signatory: hide or defect; leverage: the weights, the training details, the capability itself. Deployers: free-ride; leverage: little beyond a diffuse signal of what models can do. United States and China (signatories): comply in public, hide at the margins; leverage: jurisdiction over their labs and clouds, export controls, intelligence services. A non-signatory state with large datacenters: free-ride or defect; leverage: jurisdiction outside the treaty, power to host paused work. Regime-collapsing: China or the United States, because if either defects the other has no reason to stay paused; TSMC, together with NVIDIA, because compute control depends on their chokepoint and records; the non-signatory host state, because it offers a place to run the paused work beyond the treaty's reach."
feedback-instructions:: Name the one row whose incentive or leverage is least defended and ask the learner to argue it. Then point to any stage of the supply chain with no actor on the map. Keep it to two short paragraphs.
