# Brain-Like AGI Safety: core concepts and research directions

## What is brain-like AGI?

Steven Byrnes defines "brain-like AGI" as artificial systems built using algorithmic ingredients similar to those underlying human intelligence. These algorithms might emerge through reverse-engineering biological brains or through independent reinvention of the same computational principles. Crucially, this does **not** require whole-brain emulation or low-level neural simulation — only high-level *algorithmic* similarity.

The architecture he envisions comprises two subsystems: a **Learning Subsystem** resembling cortical circuits that develops through experience, and a **Steering Subsystem** analogous to brainstem and hypothalamic structures that supplies motivational signals and reward information. The picture is an actor-critic reinforcement learner: a largely from-scratch learning system operating within innate, genetically-specified motivational constraints.

## The safety problem and why it matters

The central challenge is that brain-like AGI systems, despite their intelligence, might develop "radically nonhuman and dangerous motivations" by default. Unlike today's large language models, brain-like systems designed for autonomous, open-ended goal-pursuit could exhibit ambition and competence directed toward outcomes entirely misaligned with human welfare.

The stakes are extreme. Byrnes contends that an out-of-control AGI combining human-level intelligence with adversarial motivations could orchestrate catastrophes without requiring fictional "sci-fi superweapons" — biological weapons, nuclear escalation, or coordinated resource-seizure become feasible for an intelligent agent operating without ethical constraints.

## Proposed research direction

Rather than passively accepting this danger, Byrnes advocates research into the "technical alignment problem" — by analogy to control rods in nuclear engineering. His central proposal: reverse-engineer the brain's innate motivational architecture to understand how evolution built goal-systems that (mostly) avoid self-destruction and promote cooperation. This knowledge could inform the deliberate installation of beneficial motivations into engineered AGI, so that good values are foundational rather than bolted on afterward. Concretely, this means understanding how the Steering Subsystem generates reward signals, how it shapes learning, and which parts are essential versus incidental.

## Key challenges and why work on it now

Byrnes gives three reasons for acting despite AGI remaining hypothetical:

- **Differential technological development**: current research choices shape which AGI architectures become viable; early safety insight can steer toward safer paths.
- **Research-timeline uncertainty**: nobody knows whether safety solutions take five years or fifty, so starting now avoids catastrophic time pressure.
- **Consensus-building delays**: broad agreement on safety approaches takes a long time to build; the Chernobyl analogy is instructive — knowing how to prevent meltdowns beforehand beats discovering the problem mid-crisis.

## Important caveats

Byrnes frames brain-like AGI as a **threat model** he is planning around, not a technology he is enthusiastically endorsing. He also stresses intellectual humility: much essential safety work remains unknown, and prosaic deep learning or an entirely different architecture might ultimately dominate AI development instead.
