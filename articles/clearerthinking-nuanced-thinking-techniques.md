There are two kinds of people in the world: those who divide everybody into two kinds of people, and those who don't. Our brains love to dichotomize — to divide reality into neat, binary categories: right or wrong, good or bad, is or is not. This sort of binary thinking is often useful in simple situations, but it can get in the way of our ability to make sense of complex topics. For those, it's useful to apply **nuanced thinking**.

This interactive Clearer Thinking mini-course teaches learners to (1) recognize three common binary-thinking traps, (2) use three nuanced-thinking techniques to resist false dichotomies and see people and things more accurately, and (3) practice applying those techniques to real-world examples. The three techniques are **probabilistic thinking** 🎲, **gray thinking** 🐘, and **multi-factor thinking** 📊 — each an antidote to a specific binary-thinking trap.

## What is binary thinking?

Statements like "You're either with me or against me," "That political party is bad, whereas this one is good," and "The vaccine will completely prevent me from getting COVID" all suggest **binary thinking**: viewing the world in an oversimplified, either-or manner, ignoring the subtleties that are almost always present.

**Why do we do it?** Simplifying the world into rigid categories can give us the illusion of understanding, offering comfort in complex situations or sensitive topics (e.g., those that are politically heated, emotional, and/or related to one's identity). Politicians and biased media often perpetuate this by promoting simple dichotomies (e.g., their side is totally good and the other side is totally bad), worsening polarization and the spread of misinformation.

**A caveat:** binary thinking has its place. In simple matters that don't require you to be especially accurate, it can be an effective mental shortcut, allowing quick and easy understanding, decision-making, and communication. For example, binary thinking is least likely to cause harm in a casual, low-stakes situation such as telling a friend what you thought of a new TV show — as opposed to high-stakes situations like deciding which college to attend. In important situations that require accuracy, nuanced thinking will serve you much better. Nuanced thinking is about seeing beyond simple dichotomies, not jumping too quickly to conclusions, and keeping an open mind when evaluating information.

## Technique #1: Probabilistic Thinking 🎲

A common thinking trap is the **truth binary**: having absolute certainty about whether something is true or not. While we often acknowledge some doubt when questioned about our beliefs, we tend to forget this doubt in practice and act as though we are certain. Given how much uncertainty there is in the world, and the fact that we rarely have full access to the truth, the truth binary often sets us up for failure. For instance:

- "The person I just matched with on this dating app is going to be absolutely amazing." → May lead you to overlook red flags and experience repeated disappointment.
- "We're not going to have a recession this year." → May lead you to hold an excessively risky stock portfolio.
- "This one remedy will prevent me from getting sick." → May lead you to neglect other important measures to stay healthy.

A helpful alternative to the truth binary is **probabilistic thinking**: estimating roughly how likely you think it is that something is true or not. Instead of saying "I am certain that X," you could say "I think there's a 70% chance that X." You don't have to put numbers on your judgments, but doing so can be helpful. For example, a doctor uses probabilistic thinking most effectively by saying "We estimate there's a 95% chance the surgery will be complication-free," rather than "The surgery is totally safe, you'll be fine!" or "I doubt you'll have any complications."

Probabilistic thinking involves resisting the urge to jump immediately to an easy, one-sided conclusion, and instead asking yourself questions like:

- How sure am I about this viewpoint?
- In what situations is this usually true, and in what situations is it usually false?
- What is the evidence for and against this being the case?

This helps you account for uncertainty in a way that binary thinking cannot. For example — Binary thinking: "This investment will be a success, so I'm going all in!" Probabilistic thinking: "I think there's an ~80% chance this investment will perform well, so I will implement some contingency plans." Your decisions and viewpoints are far less fragile if they account for the fact that we live in an unpredictable world.

**Practicing probabilistic thinking.** The mini-course walks learners through examining a claim they feel strongly about (e.g., "Social media has a significant negative impact on mental health"). The process is: first list the evidence *for* the claim; then list the evidence *against* it; then weigh both to estimate a rough percentage chance that the claim is true. Guiding questions for arriving at an estimate include: What situations could this be true or false in? If this turned out to be false, what's the most likely reason for that? How surprised would you be if it turned out you were mistaken? Would you bet a meaningful amount of money that this view is correct, only a little, or none at all?

**Updating on evidence.** Probabilistic thinking also helps you adjust your beliefs as new information arrives — increasing or decreasing your confidence accordingly. For example, if a reputable longitudinal study appeared to show that social media use does *not* significantly correlate with long-term negative mental health effects, the correct response is to become *less confident* than before that social media has a significant negative impact (even if, overall, you still agree with the claim) — not to swing to absolute certainty in either direction. The key takeaway: probabilistic thinking lets you update your beliefs relatively easily as you receive new information. With practice, you can assign more accurate probabilities and calibrate your judgments over time.

## Technique #2: Gray Thinking 🐘

Another common thinking trap is the **goodness binary**: making black-and-white value judgments — that something is entirely good or bad, positive or negative, moral or immoral. In reality, most things contain a combination of good and bad elements, and reducing them to one simple value judgment often sets you up for failure. For instance:

- "That person is entirely bad." → May lead you to prematurely judge someone who also has admirable traits.
- "Parties are never fun." → May lead you to overgeneralize and avoid situations you might actually enjoy.
- "Technological progress will save the world!" → May lead you to dismiss or overlook the downsides of a particular technology.

A helpful alternative is **gray thinking**: acknowledging that good things are rarely entirely good and bad things are rarely entirely bad. Many things lie somewhere in between and/or contain elements of both. For example, the statement that best illustrates gray thinking about AI's effect on artists is a balanced one: "On balance, I think AI will be a negative for artists. While it may allow for new artistic techniques, it'll also likely decrease the demand for human creativity" — which weighs both sides, rather than claiming AI is purely good or purely bad for artists.

**Why it helps.** Gray thinking strengthens your understanding of the world. We often understand opinions that differ from our own in an oversimplified way; when we ignore the nuances of other perspectives, it's difficult to develop our own accurate views. Acknowledging all sides of an issue — even when we ultimately endorse one side over another — helps us escape overly simplistic opinions and get closer to seeing things as they actually are.

**Practicing gray thinking.** Take a black-and-white statement such as "The legalization of marijuana is an entirely good thing." Gray thinking steers away from binary opinions like this by acknowledging both the pros and the cons before forming a viewpoint: first brainstorm the potential *positive* effects, then the potential *negative* effects. As learners usually find, complex topics can rarely be reduced to a simple good-or-bad judgment. You may still endorse "overall, I think legalization is a good thing," but your understanding becomes more well-rounded. As F. Scott Fitzgerald said, "The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in mind at the same time and still retain the ability to function."

**Better decisions.** Acknowledging both the good and the bad helps you see that most decisions come with tradeoffs. Gray thinking helps you account for these tradeoffs and avoid creating unintentional harm through misguided good intentions. By considering all sides, we can be more reasonable and tolerant, make better decisions, and promote harmony between differing perspectives. Importantly, gray thinking isn't about always settling for a moderate stance on every issue — it's about taking all sides into account when forming a view.

## Technique #3: Multi-factor Thinking 📊

The last binary trap is the **identification binary**: the tendency to divide the world into rigid, either-or categories. Even with careful categorization, there are usually edge cases that lie between categories or don't fit any category at all. Falling into the identification binary leads to oversimplifications and prevents you from seeing things and people as they actually are. For instance:

- "Is she an atheist or does she believe in God?" → May lead you to oversimplify and misunderstand a range of diverse belief systems.
- "You're either progressive or conservative." → May lead you to miss the nuances of someone's political opinions.
- "Men aren't in touch with their emotions." → May lead you to make inaccurate predictions about others or yourself.

To avoid the identification binary on important issues, you can either create more categories or move to **multi-factor thinking**: avoiding lumping something into a fixed category and instead considering the degree to which it has different factors.

**Example — political views.** Rather than simply classifying someone as progressive or conservative (thinking in categories), you could dig into their views on particular issues (thinking in factors): gun control, abortion, immigration, economic policy, health care, homelessness, social security, taxation, religious freedom, climate change, free speech, and so on. Of course, the political label someone identifies with is predictive of their views on many of these factors, so the conservative-versus-progressive distinction isn't useless (to say so would itself be binary thinking!). But if you want to be a more careful thinker, getting beyond labels and considering people and issues case-by-case builds a more accurate understanding of the world.

**Why it helps.** Multi-factor thinking helps you see things closer to how they actually are. Categorization often causes us to treat members of the *same* category as more similar than they really are, and members of *different* categories as far more different than they really are. Considering things case-by-case avoids these simplifications, preventing loss of information and false representations.

**Practicing multi-factor thinking.** Consider a dichotomy like introvert vs. extrovert. Ask whether that label fully captures how you feel and behave. Instead of categorizing yourself as strictly one or the other, think of the many factors involved: perhaps you're one way at home and another at work; certain people give you energy while others drain it; it may depend on your mood or the topic. Brainstorming the ways you're an introvert *and* the ways you're an extrovert reveals how much a single category leaves out.

Multi-factor thinking also helps escape the "us versus them" mentality. As humans, we form in-groups and out-groups (often subconsciously) by age, ethnicity, religion, economic status, and sexual identity. This grouping can be a helpful heuristic, but it can also lead to harmful stereotypes and false generalizations (e.g., "homeless people are crazy," "immigrants are dangerous," "rich people are psychopaths"). Multi-factor thinking helps you see people for their unique characteristics rather than through the distorted lens of a stereotype.

## Summary

The mini-course covers three binary-thinking traps and the nuanced-thinking technique that counters each:

1. **Probabilistic thinking 🎲** — Counters the **truth binary**. Avoid being absolutely certain; instead consider your degree of confidence. Example: "This new initiative will be successful." → "I'm roughly 80% confident this new initiative will be successful."
2. **Gray thinking 🐘** — Counters the **goodness binary**. See that things are rarely all good or all bad; they often contain elements of both or lie somewhere in between. Example: "Social media is awful." → "On balance, I think social media is a significant net negative because it harms mental health and spreads misinformation — but it has benefits too, like making it easy to build community."
3. **Multi-factor thinking 📊** — Counters the **identification binary**. Think outside rigid either-or boxes; consider the various factors that make up individual things. Example: "He's either a success or a failure." → "A lot contributes to one's success: career, relationships, hobbies, community involvement, spiritual contentment, and more."

**Applying the techniques (worked scenarios).** The course closes with a short quiz matching each technique to a situation:

- *A town decision-maker planning a new community park*, where the project has both good and bad consequences and many parties with competing interests, is best served by **gray thinking** — seeing the pros and cons regardless of one's overall position helps avoid unintentional harm and make more inclusive decisions. (Note: this scenario is easy to mistake for multi-factor thinking; the intended answer is gray thinking, because the core challenge is weighing good against bad.)
- *An entrepreneur planning a new venture* full of unknowns, who wants to avoid certainty and instead weigh how likely different outcomes are, is best served by **probabilistic thinking**.
- *A college admissions officer* wanting to look at each applicant holistically across many facets, rather than filtering solely by GPA or test score, is best served by **multi-factor thinking**.
