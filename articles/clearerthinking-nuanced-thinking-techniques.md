# Nuanced Thinking Techniques

There are two kinds of people in the world: those who divide everybody into two kinds of people, and those who don’t.

Our brains love to dichotomize, to divide reality into neat, binary categories: right or wrong, good or bad, is or is not. This sort of binary thinking is often useful in simple situations, but it can get in the way of our ability to make sense of complex topics. For those, it’s useful to apply **nuanced thinking**.

## In this mini-course, you will:

Learn to **recognize 3 common binary thinking traps** that you may be falling into.

Learn to **use 3 nuanced thinking techniques** to resist false dichotomies, develop more robust and flexible beliefs, improve your decision-making, and see people and things more accurately - in all their subtle shades of gray.

**Practice applying these techniques** to real-world examples to help you incorporate these skills into your life.

## What is binary thinking?

What do the following statements have in common?

“You’re either with me or against me.”

“That political party is bad, whereas this one is good.”

“The vaccine will completely prevent me from getting COVID.”

They all suggest forms of **binary thinking: viewing the world in an oversimplified, either-or manner, ignoring the subtleties that are almost always present.**

### Binary thinking: why do we do it?

Simplifying the world into rigid categories can give us the *illusion* of understanding, offering comfort in complex situations or sensitive topics (e.g., those that are politically heated, emotional, and/or related to one’s identity).

Politicians and biased media often perpetuate this by promoting simple dichotomies (e.g., their side is totally good and the other side is totally bad), worsening polarization and the spread of misinformation.

A quick caveat: binary thinking *can* have its place. In simple matters that don’t require you to be especially accurate, binary thinking can be an effective mental shortcut, allowing quick and easy understanding, decision making, and communication.

- When telling your friend what you thought of a new TV show
- When making a decision on which college to attend
- When talking to your current romantic partner about past relationships

**In important situations requiring you to be accurate, nuanced thinking will serve you much better than binary thinking.** Nuanced thinking is about seeing beyond simple dichotomies, not jumping too quickly to conclusions, and keeping an open mind when evaluating information.

## Technique #1: Probabilistic Thinking

A common thinking trap is the **truth binary: having absolute certainty in whether something is true or not.**

While we often recognize some doubt when questioned about our beliefs, we tend to forget this doubt in practice and act as though we are certain of our beliefs. Given how much uncertainty there is in our world and the fact that we rarely have full access to the truth, the truth binary will often set us up for failure. For instance:

*"The person I just matched with on this dating app is going to be absolutely amazing."* -> May lead you to overlook red flags and experience repeated disappointment.

*"We're not going to have a recession this year."* -> May lead you to hold an excessively risky stock portfolio.

*"This one remedy will prevent me from getting sick."* -> May lead you to not take other important measures to stay healthy.

A helpful alternative to the truth binary is **probabilistic thinking**: this involves estimating roughly how likely you think it is that something is true or not. For example, instead of saying "I am certain that X," you could say "I think there's a 70% chance that X." You don’t have to put numbers on your judgments, but doing so [can be helpful](https://slatestarcodex.com/2013/05/02/if-its-worth-doing-its-worth-doing-with-made-up-statistics/).

Options:
- “I doubt you’ll have any complications with the surgery.”
- “We estimate there’s a 95% chance that the surgery will be complication-free.”
- “The surgery is totally safe. You’ll be fine!”

**That's not quite right.** ❌
Although the phrase “I doubt…” indicates that the doctor isn’t thinking in binary terms, a statement that more clearly conveys the doctor’s degree of confidence would have been more effective. This can be done with percentages or more precise language.

**That's right!** ✅
The doctor gives their best estimate of how likely the surgery is to lead to complications.

**That's not right.** ❌
The use of the word ‘totally’ and the confidence of “You’ll be fine!” indicates binary thinking.

Probabilistic thinking involves resisting the urge to jump immediately to an easy, one-sided conclusion, and instead asking yourself questions like:

How sure am I about this viewpoint?

In what situations is this usually true and in what situations is this usually false?

What is the evidence for and against this being the case?

**Probabilistic thinking can help you to better account for uncertainty** in a way that binary thinking cannot. For example:

Binary thinking: *"This investment will be a success, so I'm going all in!"*

Probabilistic thinking: *"I think there's an ~80% chance this investment will perform well, so I will implement some contingency plans."*

Your decisions and viewpoints are far less fragile if they account for the fact that we live in an unpredictable world.

### Probabilistic thinking can also help you adjust your beliefs based on additional evidence

If new information comes in that seems to support or contradict your hypothesis, you can increase or decrease your confidence level accordingly.

Why is it difficult to adjust your beliefs when instead you're falling into binary thinking traps?

Binary thinking hampers the ability to adapt opinions when confronted with new information.

Treating beliefs as all-or-nothing can lead you to totally dismiss moderate evidence against your claim because it's not quite enough to get you to fully change your mind.

Or it can cause you to flip-flop between extreme positions (e.g., this is certainly true, this is certainly false), rather than adjusting your confidence levels based on new evidence.

## Technique #2: Gray Thinking

You just learned about probabilistic thinking, an antidote to the truth binary.

Another common thinking trap is the **goodness binary: making black-and-white value judgments** (e.g., that something is entirely good or bad, positive or negative, moral or immoral).

In reality, most things have a combination of good and bad elements and reducing them to one simple value judgment will often set you up for failure. For instance:

*"That person is entirely bad."* -> May lead you to prematurely judge a person who may also have some admirable traits.

*"Parties are never fun.*" -> May lead you to overgeneralize and avoid unique situations that you might actually have enjoyed.

*"Technological progress will save the world!*" -> May lead you to dismiss or overlook the downsides of a particular technology.

A helpful alternative to the goodness binary is **gray thinking: acknowledging that good things are rarely entirely good and bad things are rarely entirely bad.** Many things lie somewhere in between and/or contain elements of both positive and negative.

Options:
- “Artists have nothing to worry about. AI is going to help them express their visions in new and exciting ways.”
- “I think AI is going to be detrimental for artists. It’ll make a lot of their work irrelevant.”
- “On balance, I think AI will be a negative for artists. While it may allow for new artistic techniques, it’ll also likely decrease the demand for human creativity.”

**That's not quite right.** ❌
Gray thinking is about acknowledging that things are rarely *entirely* good or all bad, even if we think that *overall* they're mostly good or bad.

**That's right!** ✅
Effective gray thinking is about acknowledging that things usually contain good and bad elements.

### Gray thinking can help to strengthen your understanding of the world.

Oftentimes we understand opinions that differ from our own in an oversimplified way. When we ignore the nuances of other perspectives, it’s difficult to develop our own accurate views.

Click here for an example of oversimplified, black-and-white viewpoints

People with opposing views on gun ownership may be inclined to dismiss each other's perspectives. Those against gun ownership may label proponents as "crazy gun nuts", while supporters may view those against it as totally disregarding individual rights.

But by failing to dig in and explore the nuances of alternative perspectives, both sides limit their understanding of the issue at hand.

Acknowledging all sides of an issue, even if we end up endorsing one side over another, helps us to escape overly simplistic opinions and get closer to seeing things the way they actually are.

### Gray thinking can help you make better decisions

Acknowledging both the good and the bad helps you to see that most decisions you make come with tradeoffs. **Gray thinking helps you account for these tradeoffs and avoid creating unintentional harm through misguided good intentions.**

## Technique #3: Multi-factor Thinking

You just learned about gray thinking, an antidote to the goodness binary.

The last binary thinking trap we will cover is the **identification binary: the tendency to divide the world into rigid, either-or categories.**

Even with careful categorization, there are usually edge cases that lie between categories or don't fit into a category at all. Falling into the identification binary can lead to oversimplifications and prevent you from seeing things and people how they actually are. For instance:

*"Is she an atheist or does she believe in God?"* -> May lead you to oversimplify and misunderstand a range of diverse belief systems.

*"You're either progressive or conservative"* -> May lead you to not dig into the nuances of someone's political opinions.

*"Men aren't in touch with their emotions."* -> May lead you to make inaccurate predictions about others or yourself.

To avoid falling into the identification binary on important issues, you can either create more categories, or move to **multi-factor thinking**.

_(button: What is Multi-factor Thinking?)_

**Multi-factor thinking is when you avoid lumping something into a fixed category and instead consider the degree to which it has different factors.**

Click to see some helpful questions to ask when utilizing multi-factor thinking

In what ways is this case similar or different from these categories?

Is this case better thought of as lying between two (or three) categories, rather than as being right in the middle of one category?

If I ignore labels for a second, what traits does this case have?

A nice use-case of multi-factor thinking could be when trying to understand someone's political viewpoints. Rather than simply classifying them as progressive or conservative (thinking in *categories*), you could dive into understanding their views on particular issues (thinking in *factors*).

Of course, the political category someone identifies with is predictive of their views on many of these factors, so the identification binary of conservative versus progressive is not *useless* (to say so would be binary thinking!). But if you want to be a more careful thinker, embracing nuance and avoiding oversimplification, then getting beyond *labels* and considering people and issues on a *case-by-case basis* can be incredibly helpful in building an accurate understanding of the world.

### Multi-factor thinking can help you see things closer to how they actually are

Categorization often causes us to treat members of the *same* category as *more* similar than they actually are.

Click here for an example of this

Suppose all you knew about a group of people was that they were all Americans. With only this categorization, you may think and act as if they share much of the same identity. But one American could be a surf instructor in Hawaii, another could work on a farm in Iowa, and another could be a consultant in New York City.

These people are obviously quite distinct from each other, but our categorization may prevent us from seeing this if it's all we look at. Multi-factor thinking in this context would mean considering the various *factors* which make up these Americans' unique identities (e.g., where they live, where they were born, what they do for a living).

Conversely, categorization can also cause us to treat members of *different* categories as far more *different* than they actually are.

Suppose all you know about two students was that one of them failed a test and the other passed. You may assume they performed quite differently, but perhaps the student who failed got a 49% and the student who passed got a 51%, scores which lie just on opposite sides of an arbitrary pass/fail cutoff.

In actuality, these students are quite similar in terms of academic performance, but our categorization (i.e. pass versus fail) may cause us to treat them quite differently. Multi-factor thinking in this context would mean considering the factors that make up each student’s unique performance.

**Considering things on a case-by-case basis allows us to avoid these simplifications, preventing us from losing information and falsely representing things.**

**Multi-factor thinking can also help you escape the trap of an *“us versus them”* mentality when it comes to your interactions with and perceptions of other people.**

As humans, we often form in-groups and out-groups as ways to shape and define our own identities. For example, we may group individuals (oftentimes subconsciously) by age, ethnicity, religion, economic status, and sexual identity.

This sort of grouping can sometimes be a helpful heuristic, but it also has the potential to lead to harmful stereotypes and false generalizations (e.g., homeless people are crazy, immigrants are dangerous, rich people are psychopaths).

Multi-factor thinking can help you see people for their unique characteristics, rather than looking at them through the distorted lens of a harmful stereotype.

## Summary

This mini-course has explored three traps of binary thinking and three different nuanced thinking techniques for avoiding it:

### (1) Probabilistic thinking 🎲

Avoid being absolutely certain in your beliefs (the **truth binary** trap) and instead consider your degree of confidence.

Example: “This new initiative will be successful.” → “I’m roughly 80% confident this new initiative will be successful.”

### (2) Gray thinking 🐘

See that things are rarely all good or all bad (the **goodness binary** trap), and often things contain elements of both or lie somewhere in between extreme good and extreme bad.

Example: “Social media is awful. It ruins people's mental health and contains so much misinformation.” → “On balance, I think that social media is a significant net negative, because it harms mental health and contains so much misinformation. But obviously it has benefits too, such as how easy it has become to build a community."

### (3) Multi-factor thinking 📊

Think of things outside of rigid either-or categorical boxes (the **identification binary** trap), and instead consider the various factors that make up individual things.

Example: “He’s either successful or he’s a failure” → “There’s a lot that contribute to one’s success: their career, relationships, hobbies, community involvement, spiritual contentment, and more.”
