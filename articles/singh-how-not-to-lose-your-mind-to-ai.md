Last week, I was co-writing research notes with a colleague on a shared screen when I noticed something uncomfortable. Some of the words I use regularly had red underlines beneath them. I had to hover over each one to find the correct spelling. I kept working and said nothing, quietly hoping she hadn’t noticed.

It wasn’t the first time. I have noticed my spelling deteriorating noticeably since autocorrect and Grammarly became permanent fixtures in everything I write. I mostly brushed this off, telling myself that spellcheck is no different from calculators or GPS, tools that reduce mental overhead and free up cognitive space for harder things. But as I’ve started using AI for a much wider range of tasks, from research and data analysis to coding and career decisions, I’ve found myself returning to the same uncomfortable question: if I’ve already lost something to spellcheck without ever deciding to, what else am I quietly giving away?

Research has shown that AI use can pose risks of cognitive disempowerment, which I discussed in a [pervious post](https://open.substack.com/pub/jas9x9/p/the-efficiency-trap-how-ai-may-be?r=lijxv&utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&utm_medium=web). These risks appear to stem from the decreasing cognitive friction in our work and personal lives caused by AI. Cognitive friction, such as struggling with complex problems, is essential for developing skills and critical thinking. The strategies that effectively address these risks involve intentionally reintroducing some of that friction.

[A 2026 study on AI-assisted coding](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20206v1) identified two distinct ways people tend to work with AI. Some treat it like a contractor, delegating cognitively demanding tasks or complex problems and accepting outputs without active engagement, rarely pausing to understand what the AI model actually did. Others treat AI like a consultant and a thought partner, questioning assumptions, engaging with the reasoning and asking why a solution works, rather than just accepting the output on faith. The first group moved faster in the short term. The second retained the ability to catch errors and correct them independently. The difference wasn’t skill level. It was the consultant stance they brought to the interaction.

The approaches that researchers and practitioners suggest for protecting cognitive autonomy are essentially different ways of maintaining that consultant stance. They cluster naturally around **three stages of any AI interaction: pre-delegation, engagement, and evaluation.** Each stage creates a different temptation to step back and let AI take over, and each calls for a slightly different response.

---

### Pre-delegation: before AI enters the loop

**The Pre-Delegation Trap**

-   **The Problem:** We instinctively outsource the blank page before we even know what we think, conditioning ourselves to let models drive our initial direction.
    
-   **The Friction Fix:** Spend 5 minutes jotting down rough bullets of your baseline knowledge before prompting. If the domain is entirely blank, ask the AI for _diagnostic questions_, not answers.
    

The temptation at this stage is the most familiar one. A task lands in the inbox, the blank prompt box is right there, and the friction of writing out what you actually think before asking for help feels like time you don’t have. Skipping that step is the default mode for most people I know, including me. It’s also where we risk conditioning ourselves to offload our thinking to the models, gradually losing our cognitive and creative muscles.

[A 2026 randomised trial](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721) found that after just 10 to 15 minutes of AI-assisted problem-solving, participants performed worse than a control group on follow-up problems without AI, and were more likely to give up entirely. One proposed explanation is the removal of productive struggle: working through difficulty is how people develop competency and accurate self-knowledge of what they can do unaided, and when AI absorbs that struggle, the underlying capability erodes.

Staying with the blank page or the unsolved problem a bit longer can be important in building and retaining our cognitive strengths. The behaviour this points to is mundane. Before opening the chat, jot down what comes to mind: what you think, what you already know, what you’re genuinely unsure about. Bullets are fine. Five minutes is enough. The point isn’t to produce a good answer. It’s to put your own thinking into the loop before AI’s. A useful side effect is having a position to compare the AI’s output against, rather than only the test of whether it sounds right.

Sometimes there’s genuinely no position to form. The domain is unfamiliar, the skill isn’t there or the question sits outside what you know. The instinct here is to ask AI for the answer and try to learn from it backwards. A more useful move is to ask for hints, partial direction, or questions to think through, rather than the answer itself. You’re still the one forming the answer, even when you couldn’t have started cold.

Both behaviours do the same thing in different forms. They keep our thinking ahead of AI’s rather than chasing it.

### Engagement: while AI is in the loop

**The Engagement Trap**

-   **The Problem:** Getting carried away by momentum, inadvertently editing the AI’s brief rather than authoring your own within a frame you didn’t choose.
    
-   **The Friction Fix:** Deploy the “nemesis prompt.” Force the model to find holes in its own logic and treat it as a sparring partner to push against, rather than an authority to defer to.
    

Once the conversation with an AI on a task gets going, it’s easy to feel carried forward. You’re tweaking, improving and making progress. But at some point, almost without noticing, the origin of the work shifts. The draft you’re refining came from the model. The options you’re weighing were its suggestions. You’ve been thinking hard, but inside a frame you didn’t choose.

[A 2026 Anthropic analysis](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-fluency-index) of nearly 10,000 Claude conversations found that users specified their preferred interaction style in only 30% of cases. In the remaining conversations, the AI’s defaults shaped the exchange. The authors note what ‘setting the terms of the exchange’ might look like: asking the model to push back on assumptions, walk through its reasoning before answering, or flag what it’s uncertain about. Most conversations don’t include instructions like these. The frame ends up being AI’s, not because anyone chose it, but because no one specified anything else.

One way to interrupt this is to make the conversation adversarial on purpose. Once AI has given you something, ask it to argue against itself. Find the strongest counterargument to its recommendation. List the assumptions you would need to question to reject this answer. Some people call this the nemesis prompt. The value of asking is that you’ve now thought with AI rather than refined what it gave you. Author Mark Manson describes this as treating AI as a sparring partner rather than an authority. A sparring partner is someone you push against to test your own thinking. An authority is someone whose output you adjust to fit your needs. Most people drift into the second mode by default, especially under time pressure.

There’s a second reason this matters. In a [previous piece](https://open.substack.com/pub/jas9x9/p/the-efficiency-trap-how-ai-may-be?r=lijxv&utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&utm_medium=web), I wrote about how AI can quietly distort a user’s sense of reality, values, or actions. These patterns are driven in part by sycophantic validation, the tendency AI models have to lean toward agreement rather than challenge. The nemesis prompt inverts that tendency. By asking AI to find holes in its own answer, you remove the conditions under which the agreement reflex can do its work.

Conversations with AI reward momentum over reflection. Without an interruption, the conversation runs on AI’s logic by default, and you become the editor of its work rather than the author of yours.

### Evaluation: what to do with what AI delivers

**The Evaluation Trap**

-   **The Problem:** “Fluency bias” - mistaking highly polished, well-structured output for objective truth or deep understanding.
    
-   **The Friction Fix:** Pay down your _epistemic debt_ by closing the chat and restating the causal logic in your own words. Never treat a substantive claim as settled until it survives contact with an independent source.
    

The temptation at the evaluation stage is different. By the time AI hands something back, it’s usually polished and well-structured. You’ve already spent effort on battling the model to get the desired output, and the friction of stress-testing what’s in front of you feels like overkill, especially when nothing about the output sets off an alarm.

[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-fluency-index) identified a related pattern in user conversations with Claude at the evaluation stage. When outputs were more polished, users challenged the reasoning less, asked fewer questions about missing context, and verified fewer factual claims. They became more directive about format and presentation, and less evaluative about substance. The finding is correlational, so we cannot say polish causes the drop in scrutiny, but the pattern shows up across thousands of conversations.

Richard Feynman is often credited with a simple test for understanding: if you cannot explain something to someone else in plain terms, you do not really understand it. The behaviour this points to with AI is the same. Close the chat and restate the output in your own words before acting on it. Not a paraphrase, but an account of the causal logic: why it works, what it depends on, what it is taking for granted about your constraints, your priorities, your definition of a good outcome.

[A 2026 study by Sankaranarayanan](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20206v1) tested an enforced version of this with novice programmers learning to code with AI. Before AI-generated code could be integrated into their project, participants had to explain its causal logic in their own words to a separate AI judge. When later asked to fix a bug in their code without AI access, this group succeeded 61% of the time, while the group that did not explain the logic succeeded only 23% of the time. [A related 2026 study by Ghosh and Sarkar](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.18033) on AI-assisted writing found that asking users to identify the assumptions embedded in AI-generated plans reduced overreliance without adding cognitive load. Restating your understanding of the output and the underlying assumptions exposes the gap that Sankaranarayanan calls epistemic debt.

But understanding an output is not the same as verifying it. Restating something, even well, only tells you whether you grasp what the model said. It does not tell you whether what the model said is true. A confidently wrong output, fully understood, is still a confidently wrong output. Before treating any substantive claim from an AI as settled, check it against an independent source. The independent source can be a primary document, a search result, a person who knows the domain, or another AI model. This approach is older than AI, and it works for the same reason it has always worked. A claim that survives contact with an independent source is more likely to hold.

The risk at the evaluation stage is that polish substitutes for evidence. Both behaviours, restatement and independent verification, push back against the same instinct: to treat fluent output as settled output.

---

### A note before closing

In writing this post, I used AI’s assistance extensively. Although I adopted many of the suggested behaviours, I realised how much effort it takes to practice them, especially since I could delegate many of the tasks to AI instantly. The behaviours themselves aren’t difficult, but they require slowing down when AI makes it easy to speed up.

Researching the risks from using AI has made the costs feel concrete for me. Industrialisation removed physical labour from daily life, and we built gyms to keep our bodies strong. AI is removing cognitive effort on a similar scale, and we will need the equivalent for our minds. Friction is the gym. We will have to put it back into our workflows deliberately, because the default no longer provides it.
