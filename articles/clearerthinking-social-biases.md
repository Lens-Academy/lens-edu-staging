## How Other People Can Make You Irrational

As Charles Darwin [wrote, in 1871](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/learning/universities/darwin-and-human-nature/moral-nature#:~:text=Letter%207685%3A%20Darwin%20to%20Morley,John%2C%2014%20April%20%5B1871%5D&text=I%20do%20not%20think%20that,a%20non%2Dsocial%20animal.%22):

"I do not think there is any evidence that [humans] ever existed as a non-social animal"

Humans are deeply social creatures. That includes all of us. But **the social aspects of our lives also cause us to act in all sorts of irrational ways**, even despite the tremendous amounts of meaning they can bring us.

These social causes of irrationality (aka 'social biases') are some of the strongest and least-discussed biases we face as humans. This tool is designed to illuminate those often-hidden social biases and empower you with strategies to help you avoid the irrationality they can cause - all while still embracing the value of social connections.

🔎 **Uncover hidden influences** that can cloud your judgment and lead to irrational beliefs and decisions.

📝 **Learn practical tips and strategies** to resist social biases.

💡 **Gain insights** into your own behavior and thought processes.

This tool talks a lot about irrationality. But what is that?

Irrationality defined

We use a common framework of rationality, where an action or belief can be irrational in two ways:

• Epistemic Irrationality: A belief is epistemically irrational if it disregards evidence or good reasoning practices, even if the belief happens to be true.

• Instrumental irrationality: An action is instrumentally irrational if a well-reasoned assessment (under the circumstances) would determine that it would take you away from your goals.

Whenever we mention irrationality, we mean it meets at least one of these conditions.

When Rationality Types Conflict (click to learn more)

Usually, true beliefs help achieve goals, and goal-directed actions often support true beliefs. But sometimes they conflict:

• An act or belief that helps achieve a goal but leads to false beliefs is **instrumentally rational** but **epistemically irrational.**
• An act or belief that fosters true beliefs but hinders your goals is **epistemically rational** but **instrumentally irrational.**

When these conflict, you must decide whether truth or goal achievement matters more in that situation—there’s no universal answer.

This way of thinking about rationality is not without [objections](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2003.tb00281.x), but it is widely used and very useful.

### What do social biases have to do with rationality?

Social forces can distort beliefs or actions, pushing us away from evidence or our goals. This can happen unconsciously or even when we’re aware but struggle to resist these forces. Recognizing these influences is key to avoiding irrational behavior.

## Groupthink {>>{"author":"Luc","timestamp":1783701585659}@@not as useful for AIS?<<}

**'Groupthink'** is a term popularized by psychologist [Irving Janis](https://archive.org/details/janis_groupthink/page/n9/mode/2up). It is deliberately phrased as a single word, in order to evoke comparisons with the dystopian modes of thinking that George Orwell described in his book *Nineteen Eighty-Four* - such as *'doublethink'* and *'crimethink.'*

Here is the definition [Janis gave](https://archive.org/details/groupthinkpsycho00jani/page/9/mode/1up):

"I use the term 'groupthink' as a quick and easy way to refer to a mode of thinking that people engage in when they are deeply involved in a cohesive in-group, when the members' strivings for unanimity override their motivation to realistically appraise alternative courses of action."

In other words, **groupthink occurs when the desire for harmony and agreement within a group leads to poor decision-making and a failure to critically evaluate other options.**

When groupthink occurs, groups prioritize consensus over critical thinking, leading members to suppress dissenting opinions (in themselves or others), experience overconfidence in collective decisions, and ignore alternative solutions.

Optional extra: Janis's framework (click to expand)

Janis argued that the following group dynamics often lead to (and are often present in) groupthink:

*Overestimations of the group*

• **Illusion of invulnerability**: creates excessive optimism that encourages taking extreme risks.
• **Belief in inherent morality**: members believe in the rightness of their cause and therefore ignore the ethical or moral consequences of their decisions.

*Closed mindedness*
• **Collective rationalization**: members discount warnings and do not reconsider their assumptions.
• **Stereotyped views of out-groups**: negative views of “enemy” make effective responses to conflict seem unnecessary.

*Pressure towards uniformity*

• **Self-censorship**: doubts and deviations from the perceived group consensus are not expressed.
• **Illusion of unanimity**: the majority view and judgments are assumed to be unanimous.
• **Direct pressure on dissenters**: members are under pressure not to express arguments against any of the group’s views.
• **Self-appointed ‘mindguards’**: members protect the group and the leader from information that is problematic or contradictory to the group’s cohesiveness, view, and/or decisions.

### How to prevent groupthink

You could just appoint one person in a group to make all decisions. But, most of the time, that won’t be a good solution at all! It might feel unfair or authoritarian, and while it technically avoids groupthink, it presents other challenges - like whether that individual will properly represent the interests of the group.

Janis [proposed the following](https://archive.org/details/janis_groupthink/page/n109/mode/2up) strategies for avoiding groupthink in settings where policy decisions are being made. These lessons can be applied to work settings more broadly:

**Encourage critical evaluation**: Assign all group members the role of 'critical evaluator'. Encourage them to air objections.

**Impartial leadership from the outset**: Leaders should avoid stating preferences or expectations when assigning tasks, instead presenting unbiased statements about the scope of the problem and the limitations of available resources, without advocating specific proposals they would like to see adopted.

**Independent groups**: Establish multiple independent groups, each with different leaders, to work on the same questions, providing varied perspectives and evaluations.

**Subgroup deliberations**: Periodically split the main group into subgroups to independently deliberate under different leaders and then reconvene to reconcile differing views.

**Solicit feedback from trusted associates** Members should discuss the group’s deliberations with trusted colleagues outside the group and report back their reactions to inform the decision-making process.

**Involve external experts**: Invite outside experts or internal non-core members to attend meetings on a rotating basis, encouraging them to challenge the group's views.

**Assign a devil’s advocate**: At every meeting evaluating policy alternatives, designate at least one member to act as a devil’s advocate to question assumptions and proposed solutions.

**Analyze Rival Intentions**: When the issue involves rivals, dedicate substantial time to evaluate warning signals, survey alternative scenarios, and understand rival intentions.

**Second-Chance Meeting**: After reaching a preliminary consensus, hold a follow-up meeting for members to express residual doubts and reconsider the decision before finalizing it.

Options:
- Encouraging critical evaluation
- Impartial leadership from the outset
- Independent groups
- Subgroup deliberations
- Soliciting feedback from trusted associates
- Involving external experts
- Assigning a devil’s advocate
- Analyzing Rival Intentions
- Second-Chance Meetings

## The Abilene Paradox

### What is it?

The Abilene Paradox occurs when a group makes a decision that nobody (or nearly nobody) in the group wants, because each member of the group believes it is the preferred option of the majority of members, so each member stays quiet about the fact that they don't like that option.

The name comes from [the following story](https://doi.org/10.1016/0090-2616(74)90005-9), which nicely illustrates the paradox:

Click to read the story (abridged)

One stifling afternoon in Texas, my family were playing a board game and enduring the relentless heat. Despite the conditions, it was a tolerable, even pleasant, way to spend the day—until my father-in-law suggested, “Let’s get in the car and go to Abilene for dinner at the cafeteria.”

I was baffled. A 53-mile drive in a car with no air conditioning? Before I could voice my reluctance, my wife chimed in, “Sounds like a great idea. How about you, Jerry?” Not wanting to be the odd one out, I mumbled my agreement and added, “I just hope your mother wants to go.”

To my surprise, my mother-in-law agreed enthusiastically, saying she hadn’t been to Abilene in a long time. And so, we piled into the car. The drive was brutally uncomfortable. The food at the cafeteria turned out to be dreadful.

Hours later, we returned home hot and exhausted.

Breaking the silence, I tried to be sociable and said, “It was a great trip, wasn’t it?” This sparked confessions from everyone. My mother-in-law admitted she hadn’t wanted to go but felt pressured because the rest of us seemed so eager. I protested, saying I only agreed to please everyone else. My wife said the same, claiming she’d gone along to keep me and her parents happy. Finally, my father-in-law admitted he never wanted to go either but suggested it because he thought we might be bored.

In the end, none of us had wanted to go to Abilene. Yet we had collectively endured a grueling 106-mile round trip through unbearable conditions to eat terrible food—all because we mistakenly believed everyone else wanted to go.

The Abilene Paradox is similar to groupthink. In both cases, people in a group attempt to conform their behavior to what they think the group wants. However, the key fact about the Abilene Paradox is that group members are *mistaken* about what others in the group actually want and still attempt to conform, resulting in irrational behavior.

### Don't be too hard on yourself

If you’ve experienced this paradox first hand, don’t be too hard on yourself. It has [been dubbed](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4406948) ‘the curse of caring too much’ and it has been posited that it often stems from a combination of

caring about other people’s preferences and

not being sure about what their preferences are.

This seems plausible in our personal relationships, but the original author of the term [has argued](https://www.academia.edu/20039923/The_Abilene_Paradox_After_Thirty_Years) that there are many more potential causes of the paradox in professional settings.

### How to avoid the Abilene paradox

[This study](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4406948) found that when a group member is the first to give their opinion to the group, they are more likely to truthfully represent their preferences. Given that, the authors recommend avoiding the paradox by ensuring that groups:

"let the member whose opinion they care about the most speak first. In doing so, the 'important person' is most likely to express their true viewpoint."

use "other voting rules" such as having people vote for decision outcomes anonymously and/or simultaneously.

It's also common, in the secondary literature, to recommend doing things that mitigate the factors identified by Harvey (mentioned above). This means doing things like:

**Improve manager awareness**, so that leaders recognize their own limitations and blind spots.

**Encourage open and constructive debate**, creating an environment where group members feel safe to express dissenting views without fear of retribution.

**Promote diversity and pluralism** as a mechanism for seeking diverse perspectives within teams.

**View conflict as an opportunity for growth and improvement rather than something to be avoided**

**Seek ways to make group members more invested in group outcomes.**

**Avoid blind deference to authority** by providing group members with tools to question and assess the validity of perceived expertise.

Options:
- Letting the person whose opinion matters most speak first
- Using other voting rules
- Improving manager awareness
- Encouraging open and constructive debate
- Promoting diversity and pluralism
- Viewing conflict as an opportunity
- Seeking ways to make group members more invested
- Avoiding blind deference to authority

## Authority Bias

Types of Conformity (discussed in this tool):

### What is it?

Authority bias is the tendency to attribute greater credibility to the opinions or instructions of those perceived as authority figures, regardless of the content of the opinions / instructions.

This bias can lead people to accept information or directives from those they perceive as authority figures without critical evaluation.

### Milgram's Obedience Experiments

The concept of authority bias was popularized by psychologist Stanley Milgram whose [obedience experiments, conducted in the 1960s](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0040525) have become some of the most famous psychology experiments ever conducted. Milgram intended to investigate how far people would go in obeying an authority figure (in this case, a scientist), even when instructed to perform actions conflicting with their personal morals.

Participants in his experiments were given the role of 'teacher' and were told by a scientist to administer increasingly severe electric shocks to a 'learner' (who was an actor) for giving wrong answers in a memory task. If participants resisted, the authority figure (the scientist) would go through this series of statements:

"Please continue" or "Please go on."

"The experiment requires that you continue."

"It is absolutely essential that you continue."

"You have no other choice, you must go on."

Despite hearing the learner's protests and cries of (simulated) pain, some participants continued delivering extremely high voltage shocks (up to 450V!) when prompted by the authority figure.

Milgram's experiments are controversial, but they're worth discussing because of their impact and fame. They’re often cited as evidence that people will commit terrible acts against their own morals if told to by an authority figure. Let’s take a closer look and explore a more nuanced perspective.

### Objections to Milgram

Milgram's experiments are widely known and influential, but they have faced criticism. Below, you'll find some of these objections. Whether these challenges are enough to discredit Milgram's findings is debated — many argue they are, while many others disagree. We've included the experiments here because they effectively illustrate the concept of authority bias (which is otherwise-supported by research), and we believe there is value in thinking critically about some of psychology's most famous studies. Key objections include:

Click here to see some reasons to doubt Milgram's findings

1️⃣ When [researchers analyzed the (previously unpublished) post-experiment interviews](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjso.12206) that Milgram conducted with some of the participants of his experiments, they found that **72% of people who 'obeyed' said they did not believe they were administering real shocks.** Instead, they believed (correctly!) that the experiment was fake *and they gave that as a reason for why they continued with it!* This throws the veracity of Milgram's conclusions into question; if people did not believe the experimental conditions were real, can we really infer anything about their real relationships to authority?

2️⃣ In experimental conditions where participants were asked to administer shocks by someone who was not an authority figure (i.e., by an actor whom they believed was another participant like them), [participants were still obedient](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3976349/) (sometimes at rates similar to those in experimental conditions with an authority), suggesting that **it might not be simple obedience to *authority* that caused people to administer the shocks.**

3️⃣ It is [debated whether](https://archive.org/details/behindshockmachi0000perr_b8r1/page/n1/mode/2up) the behavior of participants is best understood as 'obedience'. Instead, **results might be due to coercion, desire not to mess up a scientific study, or something else.**

4️⃣ Some researchers [have argued](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9248.1985.tb01584.x) that **Milgram's findings do not tell us anything generalizable about people's attitudes towards authority**, while [others argue](https://doi.org/10.1016/0191-8869(90)90183-R) that **there is no such thing as a generalized attitude towards all forms of authority.** Instead, people tend to feel differently about different cases of authority (teachers, police, etc).

### How to avoid authority bias

Beyond the Milgram experiments, authority bias has been documented in a variety of [other](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1111/j.1559-1816.1974.tb02599.x) [studies](https://www.academia.edu/7855636/Influence_The_Psychology_of_Persuasion) as well.

Fortunately, [other researchers have suggested](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-16-0143-9_7) potential solutions for avoiding authority bias, such as:

**Take the time to think logically and independently.** Consider what you might do if the cognitive shortcut of following your senior colleague’s instructions was not available to you.

**Understand the reasons behind authority figures' judgments.** Ask your colleagues, especially managers and leaders, to explain why they are making particular decisions and how they have come to their conclusions.

**Temporarily create a distance between yourself and the authority figure** (time or physical) to mitigate their influence on your own thoughts and decisions.

When in the role of an authority figure, **give others opportunity to express ideas before your own** to allow discussion and independent assessment and thought

We also suggest reflecting on the importance you give to credentials. Sometimes credentials are strong evidence that a person should be believed, and other times, less so (which can easily make us think people have authority that they don't really have, if we are not careful). You can read more about how to think about credentials [here](https://www.clearerthinking.org/post/understanding-the-difference-between-credentialists-and-non-credentialists).

Options:
- Taking time to think logically and independently
- Understanding the reasons behind authority figures' judgments
- Temporarily creating distance between yourself and the relevant authority figure(s)
- Giving others opportunity to express ideas before you express your own
- Reflecting on the importance you give to credentials

## Social Identities

Types of Conformity (discussed in this tool):

### What are social identities?

We each have many social identities. A social identity can be [defined as follows](https://books.google.co.uk/books/about/Human_Groups_and_Social_Categories.html?id=ldA8AAAAIAAJ&redir_esc=y):

A social identity is any group that you know and care that you're a part of.

Common examples of social identities include political affiliation, favorite sports team, social class, gender, sex, sexual orientation, race, religious beliefs, and many more.

Optional extra: The difference between identity and social identity (click to expand)

The framework we're using here says that not every aspect of your identity is a social identity. Things like ‘curious’ and ‘fair-minded’ can be important parts of your conception of yourself, hence they can be *identities* - but unless they pertain to membership in a social group, they are not *social* identities. See [here](https://doi.org/10.2307/2695870) for more on this distinction.

### How Social Identities Can Make People Irrational

Unfortunately, our social identities sometimes make us act irrationally. Such as when we (often subconsciously) twist or interpret evidence to match conclusions already adopted by our social groups - whether or not the evidence points that way. Here's just one example:

You might think people could agree on the facts during a debate, but research (e.g., [here](https://doi.org/10.1080/13669877.2010.511246), [here](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195341140.001.0001), and [here](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781118900772.etrds0417)) seems to show that our sense of what counts as a 'fact' is shaped by our social identities. For example, when debates involve clear scientific evidence favoring one side, studies (such as [here](https://doi.org/10.1080/13669877.2010.511246)) have found that most people still believe their group’s views align with the scientific evidence — even when they don’t.

It gets worse. [This study](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2016.2) purports to show that the better you are at reasoning, the more skilled you will be at using those reasoning skills to interpret evidence in a way that supports the ideological beliefs of your social identities (even if the evidence doesn't *really* support those beliefs). The study involved presenting people with the following challenging math problem (which only 41% people were able to answer correctly):

### Here is some data from a clinical trial of a new cream for a skin rash

Please indicate whether the experiment shows that using the new cream is likely to make the skin condition *better* or *worse*.

Options:
- People who used the skin cream were more likely to *get better* than those who didn't.
- People who used the skin cream were more likely to *get worse* than those who didn't.

### Why we do this

The authors of [that study](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2016.2) write:

"In an environment in which positions on particular policy-relevant facts become widely understood as symbols of individuals’ membership in and loyalty to opposing cultural groups, it will promote people’s individual interests to attend to evidence about those facts in a manner that reliably conforms their beliefs to the ones that predominate in the groups they are members of. Indeed, the tendency to process information in this fashion will be strongest among individuals who display the reasoning capacities most strongly associated with science comprehension."

So this sort of bias may often be:

Instrumentally rational ✅

Epistemically irrational ❌

And it's up to you to decide what to do in situations where these two trade off against each other.

### How to prevent irrationality stemming from your social identities

There aren't peer-reviewed research-based interventions here, but we like [what Ezra Klein suggests](https://bookshop.org/p/books/why-we-re-polarized-ezra-klein/10338540):

**Be mindful of which social identities are being 'activated'** when you're feeling things like defensiveness. Sometimes those emotions will be justified, but other times they won't, and you're less likely to be the "unwitting instrument of others" if you are able to reflect on and be deliberate about when you let those social identities affect your thinking.

**Cultivate (non-social) identities that will counterbalance the negative effects of your social identities.** If you find that seeing fans of a rival sports team on twitter makes you irrationally angry, then consider deliberately choosing to make something like fair-mindedness or compassion a part of your conception of yourself. Don't just try to be more fair-minded or compassionate: also seek to fold that into your identity - taking pride in it and drawing on that conception of yourself when you feel (or worry that) your social identities might be leading you to irrationality.

Options:
- Being mindful of which social identities are being 'activated'
- Cultivating (non-social) identities that will counterbalance the negative effects of your social identities

## Mimicry

We've distinguished between two reasons that you might feel social pressure to match your beliefs or actions to those of people around you:

Conformity involves matching the beliefs or actions of those around you in order to conform to their expectations or standards. Perhaps because you fear judgment or recrimination.

Mimicry involves matching the beliefs or actions to those around you because you see them as having more information or better judgment than you. That is to say, because you think they know better.

Here's a quote from [Asch's paper](http://nwkpsych.rutgers.edu/~kharber/selectedtopicsinsocialpsychology/READINGS/Asch%201951%20Group%20pressure%20and%20judgment.pdf), discussing one of the non-stooges who gave lots of incorrect answers:

He opened discussion with the statement: "If I'd been first [to answer] I probably would have responded differently"; this was his way of responding that he had adopted the majority [answers, against his own judgment]. The primary factor in his case was loss of confidence. He perceived the majority as a decided group, acting without hesitation: "If they had been more doubtful I probably would have changed, but they answered with such confidence."

So, this was a case where the non-stooge assumed that he must have been wrong in his own judgment. As such, he went against the evidence of his senses and adopted the incorrect answer.

Optional extra: Other ways we mimic others (click to expand)

**Children mimic to learn**
[Studies show](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1963-00875-001) that children learn behaviors by imitating the behavior of others. These insights have been incorporated into theories of learning such as [Social Cognitive Theory](https://www.simplypsychology.org/social-cognitive-theory.html), which emphasizes how learning behaviors occurs in a social context where we can see the consequences of those behaviors. This theory represented a departure from earlier theories which emphasized requiring more direct positive reinforcement in order to learn new behaviors.

**Mimetic desire**
Girard's [theory of mimetic desire](https://www.blackwellpublishing.com/content/bpl_images/content_store/sample_chapter/0745629474/Fleming_sample%20chapter_rene%20Girarg.pdf) argues that we as humans learn what to desire by imitating the desires of others. While the broader implications of his theory — such as its connections to status-seeking, rivalry, and scapegoating — are subject to debate, the basic insight that (at least some of) our desires are shaped by observing and emulating others is widely recognized.

### But is mimicry always irrational?

Sometimes, mimicking the beliefs or actions of others will lead you to false beliefs or actions that take you away from your goals. That's irrational by the definition we're using in this tool. For example:

You might believe that a political claim is true because it aligns with the views of your peer group, despite contrary evidence.

You might buy a product because it's popular, even though you have evidence that it's not actually useful to you.

However, we shouldn't ignore the fact that it can be rational to mimic the actions of others this way. The webcomic [XKCD](https://xkcd.com/1170) puts this very well:

_(button: How to mimic rationally)_

The key, here, is to think carefully about what the balance of evidence is telling you. If a group of people (or someone you trust) is doing something, that *is* evidence that you might benefit from doing it too. However, when the action is important, you should carefully weigh up the evidence for and against, instead of just mimicking by default.

Here are some ways to mimic rationally without falling into irrationality:

Evaluate the group's expertise: Consider whether the group has knowledge or experience relevant to the decision you're facing. If genuine experts or informed individuals are doing something, it's more likely to be worth mimicking. For example, if doctors are unanimously recommending a treatment, that's a strong signal it may be effective.

Check for independence: If everyone in the group is relying on the same source or simply copying each other, their actions might not add much new evidence. Look for diversity of opinion and independent reasoning within the group.

Consider your own evidence: Ask yourself what you already know and how strongly your evidence compares to the group's actions. If you have clear, reliable evidence that contradicts what the group is doing, it might be rational to stick to your judgment.

Gauge the consequences of being wrong: Sometimes, the stakes of a decision can influence how much weight you should give to group behavior. If copying the group is low-risk and deviating is high-risk, it might make sense to mimic even if you're unsure.

Watch for overconfidence: Groups can appear confident even when they're wrong. Consider whether their confidence is backed by solid reasoning or if it might be a result of groupthink or other forms of social pressure discussed in this tool.

Ask questions: If possible, inquire as to why the group is acting a certain way. Their reasoning might provide valuable evidence, or it might reveal flaws that give you reason to go your own way.

Test small steps first: Before fully mimicking a group, try small-scale experiments. For instance, if everyone is raving about a new productivity tool, test it out on a minor task before fully adopting it.

Stay open to changing course: Mimicry isn't a commitment. If new evidence comes to light that contradicts the group, be prepared to adjust your actions accordingly.

Options:
- Evaluating group expertise
- Checking for independence
- Considering your own evidence
- Gauging the consequences of being wrong
- Watching for overconfidence
- Asking questions
- Testing small steps first
- Staying open to changing course

## Polarization

### What is Group Polarization?

Simply put, group polarization [can be defined as follows](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9760.00148):

Group polarization

When a group discusses something, members tend to shift toward a more extreme version of their initial views.

Surprisingly, [studies repeatedly find](https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/1121142?v=pdf):

Rather than averaging out individual preferences, group discussions often lead to more extreme decisions than the initial inclinations of group members

Even when researchers hear the opinions of group members anonymously (after group discussion), group members tend to adopt more extreme views than they held before the discussion, suggesting that the polarization of the group actually changes the opinions of group members

Optional extra: Connection to political polarization (click to expand)

### How they're related

Group polarization is not quite the same phenomenon as political polarization, but [the former has been shown to lead to the latter](https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/1121142?v=pdf). This means that attempts to mitigate group polarization may help with the problem of political polarization.

### But what is political polarization?

It's common to think of political polarization as having two parts to it:

• **Opinions on issues stray towards two opposite extremes** (issue polarization)
• **Attitudes to people with opposing views become more extreme** (affective polarization)

Here is a graphic definition that comes [from the University of London (King's College)](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/policy-institute/assets/divided-britain.pdf):

Optional extra: Connection to filter bubbles and echo chambers

These two concepts are often treated interchangeably, but have [some important differences](https://doi.org/10.1017/epi.2018.32):

Filter Bubbles

A social environment in which voices espousing certain relevant views have been left out, perhaps accidentally.

Exposure to contrary viewpoints can burst a filter bubble.

Echo Chambers

A social environment in which voices espousing certain relevant views have been actively excluded and discredited.

Exposure to contrary viewpoints can reinforce an echo chamber, particularly when people in them have been trained to view all sources with opposing viewpoints with distrust.

These are two mechanisms by which group polarization can occur.

It is popular today to argue that social media contributes to group polarization by putting people into filter bubbles and/or echo chambers. However, there is evidence (such as [here](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/icwsm/article/view/14118) and [here](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjr044)) that things like Twitter and Facebook expose us to more diverse views than this claim acknowledges.

Indeed [this study](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjr044) finds that people are exposed to a wider range of viewpoints online than they are in face-to-face interactions. This probably matches your personal experience (think about how wild some of the views you've seen on Twitter can be, for example). The problem may instead be that these social media platforms show content that engages us, and viewpoints we vehemently disagree with tend to be engaging. So, although we are seeing diverse viewpoints, we are rarely having our minds changed.

### Is this irrational?

Of course, there's nothing *inherently* irrational about having an extreme point of view. In 1632, the idea that the Earth moved around the Sun (known as 'heliocentrism') was [considered so extreme](https://www.jstor.org/stable/24966665) that books about it were banned and the Roman Catholic Inquisition sentenced Galileo to house arrest (where he remained until he died) for advocating it.

Sometimes, extreme views are correct.

What makes this a source of irrationality is that groups tend towards more extreme views *whether or not those views are correct*. It's the fact that this mechanism has no connection to truth that means it will sometimes lead us astray - to beliefs that are false.

### How to prevent group polarization

Given that extreme views are not inherently bad, **you won't always want to prevent group polarization.** Instead, it's worth being aware of the phenomenon and reflecting carefully on whether it may be happening for positive or negative reasons in groups you find yourself in.

Cass Sunstein offers [two reasons](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9760.00148) for why group polarization can occur:

Reason 1: Social Comparison

People in a group tend to adjust their views to align with what they perceive as the dominant or socially desirable position within the group. This might be to signal loyalty to the group or avoid being seen as an outsider. Hence, this effect is strongest when members see themselves as sharing a social identity with each other, and weakest (or absent) when they do not.

Is this rational? This is clearly an epistemically irrational method of acquiring beliefs (since it's not a reliable way of getting to truth). But, if your wellbeing depends enough on inclusion in the group, it might be an instrumentally rational method.

Reason 2: Persuasive Arguments

Each member of a group will have thought of some, but not all, of the arguments that justify their initial position. So, in a group that largely shares the same view, members will be exposed to:

• more arguments they haven't thought of that support their view, and

• few arguments against their view.

This imbalance will likely lead to stronger conviction. Hence, this effect is stronger as there are fewer members with dissenting opinions.

Is this rational? Of course, it's often rational to believe persuasive arguments. But if you have reason to think that you're only being exposed to the persuasive arguments of one side of a contentious topic, it's justified to exercise caution and seek out alternative perspectives.

James Fishkin developed [a framework for group deliberation](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1dt006v) that some [studies indicate](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpos.2023.1127372) does not result in polarization. However, [this meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpos.2023.1127372) casts some doubt on its applicability. If there are lessons to be learned from this framework, the first among them is likely to be something that we've already stressed many times in this tool:

You will tend to arrive at more epistemically rational beliefs (and belief-forming behaviors) if you seek out diverse perspectives and arguments.

Options:
- Being conscious of whether and why group polarization might occur in your groups
- Seeking out diverse perspectives and arguments

## Social Trust

### Trust can make us irrational

It's easy to look at the beliefs of others and wonder: "How on earth can they be so foolish?!"

**The answer often lies in trust.** Maybe if you were to evaluate an idea yourself, you would see it as foolish, false, or dangerous. But, if someone you trust tells you that the idea is life-changing, you can either believe the person you trust is wrong (or deceiving you), or believe they're right. If your trust in them outweighs your skepticism, you're likely to believe them.

Some of the time, this will lead you to have false beliefs or take you away from your goals.

In other words: some of the time, this will result in irrationality.

Despite it's (mildly) positive correlation with perceived trustworthiness, you of course cannot tell someone's trustworthiness from their physical appearance, and presuming that you can is likely to be [the halo effect](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/4_J_Applied_Psychology_25_(Thorndike).pdf) at work.

Definition of the Halo Effect

A tendency to make presumptions about traits a person might have, based on your impression of that person's other (unrelated) positive qualities. So, if you like a person and have no evidence regarding how kind or trustworthy they are, you're more likely to presume that they are kind and trustworthy (merely because you like them). If you dislike someone and don't have evidence regarding whether they are spiteful or selfish, you'll be more likely to presume that they are spiteful and selfish (because you dislike them). This is a well-known cognitive bias.

### How to trust people, rationally

(1) Be aware of the halo effect; don't jump to trusting people simply because you like them or have a good feeling about them. Liking isn't a sufficient reason for trusting. Of course, trust is often [a key part of deep friendships](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0114.1987.tb00280.x) and therefore instrumentally rational, but it will not always be rational (instrumentally or epistemically) to implicitly trust everyone that you feel positively about.

(2) Philosophers who specialize in trust have made a distinction between [two-place and three-place trust](https://doi.org/10.2307/2216409). Imagine you’re talking about a friend named Erin:

Two-Place Trust

You might simply say "I trust Erin." This means you generally believe Erin is a trustworthy person, but you haven’t specified what you trust her to do. This is a broad, generalized form of trust. It’s called two-place because it can be expressed as a schematic (“Person A trusts person B”) that has two variables in it.

Three-Place Trust

Or you might say "I trust Erin to feed my cat while I'm away." Here, you’re clearly stating that your trust in Erin applies specifically to the task of feeding your cat, not necessarily to other aspects of her behavior. This is a specific form of trust that is context-dependent. It is called three-place because it can be expressed as a schematic (“Person A trusts person B to X”) that has three variables in it.

In many cases, it can be helpful to move from blind, generalized trust to more nuanced, context-specific trust. By using three-place trust, you can preserve your relationships and respect for someone's character while maintaining a rational approach to contexts that require more critical reflection - where their abilities, knowledge, or judgment might not be as strong.

Options:
- Being aware of the halo effect
- Employing three-place trust more frequently

## Impression Management

### What is it?

The need to belong and have social connection is [a fundamental human need](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/) which motivates many human actions.

This means that, when you're in at least some social situations, you're likely to care about what (at least some) people think of you. You may have other goals (e.g., to enjoy yourself) but managing other people's impressions of you [will likely sometimes be among your goals](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1990-12233-001).

Impression management

"The process by which individuals attempt to control the impressions others form of them." (source)

While it is often important to us that others like us, sometimes our drive to be liked leads to excessive impression management to the point of interfering with our other goals (and hence being irrational).

**Example:** Someone who is afraid that others won't like who they are deep down, so they put on a persona to try to be liked more - but in doing so, they end up limiting their ability to have deep connections.

**Example:** Someone who is so worried about how they are viewed at work, that they won't ever say "no", which leads to them having excessive work heaved on them that they can't handle.

The point is that impression management is not always or even mostly irrational, but that it's fairly common to overemphasize its importance and sacrifice other values of ours in the process.

Impression management

• Verbal communications about oneself

• Taking credit for or downplaying successes and failures

• Association with other people or groups

• Modifying physical appearance

• Conspicuous use of material possessions

Optional extra: Examples of irrational impression management behaviors (click to expand)

**Ingratiation**
Excessive agreement with a superior's flawed ideas can take you away from goals such as innovation.

**Verbal Communications About Oneself**
Exaggerating achievements during a job interview might lead to being placed in a role that exceeds one's actual abilities, increasing stress and the likelihood of failure.

**Taking Credit for Successes**

Buying luxury items to impress others can lead to financial instability and a focus on external validation rather than personal satisfaction.

**Food Choices**
Adopting a trendy diet for social approval rather than health can lead to inadequate nutrition or unsustainable habits.

### How to avoid irrational impression management

(1) Avoid impression management behaviors that studies have found to be instrumentally irrational, such as:

Humblebragging

Self-deprecation

Helping paternalistically

Hiding successes

Backhanded compliments

(2) If you become too focused on impression management, you can easily start acting out-of-step with your values (and thus in ways that are instrumentally irrational). For example, you might exaggerate or lie about achievements to appear more accomplished, which would be out-of-step with common values of truth and honesty.

In order to avoid this, we recommend getting clear on your values and setting goals for yourself that are aligned with the values you find that you hold most deeply.

Options:
- Avoiding irrational impression management behaviors
- Getting clear on your values and setting goals for yourself that align with them
