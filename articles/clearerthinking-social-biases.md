As Charles Darwin wrote in 1871, "I do not think there is any evidence that [humans] ever existed as a non-social animal." Humans are deeply social creatures, but the social aspects of our lives also cause us to act in irrational ways. These social causes of irrationality — "social biases" — are among the strongest and least-discussed biases we face. This interactive Clearer Thinking tool illuminates five hidden social biases and offers strategies to avoid the irrationality they can cause, while still embracing the value of social connection. It helps you uncover hidden influences that cloud judgment, learn practical strategies to resist social biases, and gain insight into your own behavior and thought processes.

## What is irrationality?

The tool uses a common framework in which a belief or action can be irrational in two ways:

- **Epistemic irrationality:** a belief is epistemically irrational if it disregards evidence or good reasoning practices — even if the belief happens to be true.
- **Instrumental irrationality:** an action is instrumentally irrational if a well-reasoned assessment (under the circumstances) would determine that it takes you away from your goals.

Whenever the tool mentions irrationality, it means at least one of these conditions is met. Social forces can distort beliefs or actions, pushing us away from evidence or our goals — sometimes unconsciously, sometimes even when we're aware of the pull but struggle to resist it. A recurring theme is that a bias can be **instrumentally rational** (it serves your interests, e.g., belonging to a group) while being **epistemically irrational** (it's not a reliable route to truth), and it's up to you to decide what to do when the two trade off.

## Bias 1 of 5: Conformity

Conformity is matching the beliefs or actions of those around you in order to meet their expectations or standards — often out of fear of judgment or recrimination. The tool covers four forms.

### Groupthink

Groupthink is when a cohesive, insular group prioritizes harmony and consensus over critical evaluation, leading to poor decisions. A useful contrast: the **WHO's early COVID-19 errors were *not* groupthink** — its decision-making involved input from many countries and experts (not a small, insular group), and the errors were rooted in institutional inertia and caution. The **Challenger disaster *is* a classic example of groupthink** — significant pressure to maintain the launch schedule (national interest, political considerations) led decision-makers to rationalize proceeding and ignore engineers' warnings about the O-rings.

Irving Janis proposed strategies for avoiding groupthink in decision-making settings, which apply to workplaces broadly: assign every member the role of **critical evaluator** and encourage objections; practice **impartial leadership** (leaders avoid stating preferences up front); set up **independent groups** with different leaders working the same question; use **subgroup deliberations** that later reconvene; **solicit feedback from trusted associates** outside the group; **involve external experts** on a rotating basis; **assign a devil's advocate** at every meeting; **analyze rival intentions** when rivals are involved; and hold a **second-chance meeting** after a preliminary consensus so members can voice residual doubts. (Simply appointing one person to make all decisions technically avoids groupthink but introduces other problems and is usually a bad solution.)

### The Abilene Paradox

The Abilene Paradox occurs when a group makes a decision that nobody (or nearly nobody) actually wants, because each member wrongly believes it is the majority's preference and stays quiet. It's similar to groupthink — in both, people conform to what they think the group wants — but the defining feature here is that members are *mistaken* about what others actually want. It's been called "the curse of caring too much," often stemming from caring about others' preferences while being unsure what those preferences are. In professional settings it can be driven by factors such as an organization with little conflict or debate, lack of diversity of perspective, and employees feeling indifferent to the organization.

To avoid it: research finds that whoever speaks first is more likely to represent their true preferences, so let the person whose opinion matters most speak first; use other voting rules such as anonymous and/or simultaneous voting; improve manager awareness of blind spots; encourage open, constructive debate where dissent is safe; promote diversity and pluralism; view conflict as an opportunity for growth; make members more invested in outcomes; and avoid blind deference to authority.

### Authority Bias

Authority bias is the tendency to attribute greater credibility to the opinions or instructions of perceived authority figures, regardless of the content — accepting directives without critical evaluation. It was popularized by Stanley Milgram's 1960s obedience experiments, in which participants ("teachers") were instructed by a scientist to administer increasing electric shocks (up to 450V) to a "learner" (an actor) for wrong answers, with escalating prompts ("Please continue," … "You have no other choice, you must go on"). Some participants continued despite the learner's protests.

Milgram ran many variations; the tool highlights three (each on 40 participants): in the **baseline** condition **25 of 40 obeyed** all the way; in the **"touch" condition** (the teacher physically holds the learner's hand to the shock plate) obedience dropped to **12 of 40**; and in the **"group pressure to disobey" condition** (two other "teachers" defy the scientist) only **4 of 40 obeyed**. This shows two opposing social forces: authority pushing people to comply, and the example of others disobeying pulling them the other way. Milgram's studies are widely known but have faced methodological objections; the tool includes them because they vividly illustrate authority bias, which is otherwise well-supported by research.

To avoid authority bias: take time to think logically and independently (imagine the shortcut of deferring weren't available); ask authority figures to explain the reasons behind their judgments; temporarily create distance (in time or space) from the authority figure to reduce their influence; when you are the authority figure, let others express ideas before you do; and reflect critically on how much weight you give to credentials (sometimes strong evidence, sometimes not).

### Social Identities

A social identity is any group that you know and care that you're part of — political affiliation, favorite sports team, social class, gender, sexual orientation, race, religious beliefs, and many more. Social identities can make us irrational when we (often subconsciously) twist or interpret evidence to match conclusions already adopted by our groups. Research suggests our sense of what even counts as a "fact" is shaped by our identities: on issues with clear scientific evidence favoring one side, most people still believe their group's views align with the science, even when they don't.

Worse, a well-known study on "motivated numeracy" found that **stronger reasoning skills can make this bias worse**. People were shown a numerically tricky skin-cream clinical-trial problem (only ~41% answered correctly); numeracy predicted success. But when the *same* problem was reframed as being about whether gun-control measures increase or reduce crime, numeracy no longer predicted getting it right — and people better at math were *more* likely to get it wrong when the correct answer conflicted with their ideology. Liberal Democrats solved it well when the answer supported gun control and struggled when it didn't; conservative Republicans showed the opposite pattern. The authors concluded that people with higher reasoning capacity are especially skilled at interpreting evidence to fit their group's ideology — which is often instrumentally rational (it protects group standing) but epistemically irrational.

There aren't strong research-based interventions here, but the tool endorses Ezra Klein's suggestions: be mindful of which social identities are being "activated" when you feel defensive, so you're less likely to become the "unwitting instrument of others"; and cultivate non-social identities (like fair-mindedness or compassion) that counterbalance the negative effects of your social identities — folding them into your self-conception and drawing on them when a social identity threatens to distort your thinking.

## Bias 2 of 5: Mimicry

Mimicry differs from conformity: you match others' beliefs or actions not to meet their expectations, but because you see them as having more information or better judgment than you — because you think they know better.

The classic demonstration is Solomon Asch's line-judgment experiment. Groups of eight judged which of several lines matched a reference line — an easy, obvious task. But seven of the eight were confederates instructed to give the same wrong answer on most trials, creating social pressure on the lone real participant. Strikingly, **only about 25% of real participants resisted enough to give all correct answers** — roughly 75% went along with the group's incorrect answer at least once. These results have been replicated many times. Asch's notes describe a participant who conformed out of loss of confidence: "If they had been more doubtful I probably would have changed, but they answered with such confidence."

Is mimicry always irrational? No. Sometimes copying others leads you to false beliefs or actions away from your goals (believing a political claim because your peer group does despite contrary evidence; buying a product because it's popular despite evidence it won't help you) — that's irrational. But mimicking can also be perfectly rational when others genuinely do have better information. The skill is learning to mimic *rationally* — copying others when they're a reliable guide and resisting when the pressure has no connection to truth.

## Bias 3 of 5: Group Polarization

Group polarization: when a group discusses something, members tend to shift toward a *more extreme* version of their initial views. Rather than averaging individual preferences, discussion often produces more extreme decisions than members started with — and this holds even when post-discussion opinions are collected anonymously, suggesting the group actually changes members' minds.

An example: a study of 1,995 court cases compared district judges deciding alone versus in groups of three, on constitutional challenges to statutes or policies (leaving the policy in place was the "cautious" option; striking it down the "extreme" one). Judges deciding alone chose the extreme option 30% of the time; **judges in groups of three chose it 65% of the time — more than twice as often.** Extreme views aren't inherently wrong (heliocentrism was once considered so extreme that Galileo was placed under house arrest), but what makes polarization a source of irrationality is that groups drift toward extremity *whether or not* the view is correct — the mechanism has no connection to truth.

Cass Sunstein offers two reasons polarization occurs. **Social comparison:** people adjust toward what they perceive as the group's dominant or socially desirable position (to signal loyalty or avoid seeming an outsider) — strongest when members share a social identity. This is epistemically irrational, though possibly instrumentally rational if your wellbeing depends on inclusion. **Persuasive arguments:** in a like-minded group, each member is exposed to more arguments supporting the shared view and fewer against it, strengthening conviction — this is worse the fewer dissenters there are. Believing persuasive arguments is often rational, but if you suspect you're only hearing one side, caution and seeking alternatives are warranted. The overarching lesson: you'll tend to arrive at more epistemically rational beliefs if you **seek out diverse perspectives and arguments**, and stay conscious of whether and why polarization might be happening in your groups.

## Bias 4 of 5: Social Trust

Trust can make us irrational. When we wonder how others can believe something foolish, the answer often lies in trust: if someone you trust tells you an idea is life-changing, and your trust outweighs your skepticism, you'll likely believe them — and some of the time that produces false beliefs or takes you away from your goals.

A meta-analysis of 338 studies examined why we trust people, finding statistically significant correlations between various traits and trustworthiness (traits such as having expertise, reputation, being an in-group member, physical appearance, and being seen as transparent). Notably, **physical appearance has only a mild positive correlation with perceived trustworthiness** — yet you cannot actually judge trustworthiness from appearance, and presuming you can is the **halo effect**: the tendency to assume a person has positive traits (like kindness or trustworthiness) based on unrelated positive impressions (like liking them), and negative traits when you dislike them.

To trust rationally: (1) Be aware of the halo effect — don't trust people simply because you like them or have a good feeling; liking isn't sufficient reason for trust. (2) Use the distinction between **two-place trust** ("I trust Erin" — a broad, generalized belief that she's trustworthy) and **three-place trust** ("I trust Erin to feed my cat while I'm away" — trust specific to a context/task). Moving from blind, generalized trust to nuanced, context-specific trust is often more rational.

## Bias 5 of 5: Impression Management

The need to belong is a fundamental human motive, so in social situations you're likely to care about what others think of you. Impression management is "the process by which individuals attempt to control the impressions others form of them." It's not usually irrational — but people commonly overemphasize its importance and sacrifice other values in the process (e.g., putting on a persona to be liked, which limits deep connection; or never saying "no" at work out of concern for how you're viewed, leading to unmanageable workloads).

Research identifies common **impression-management mistakes** that tend to backfire:

- **Humblebragging** — an attempt to promote yourself that makes you seem insincere.
- **Self-deprecation** — an attempt to signal confidence by downplaying personal qualities, which observers usually take at face value.
- **Helping paternalistically** — backfires because others infer you have negative assumptions about their emotional resilience and character.
- **Hiding success** — a genuine attempt at modesty, but when discovered, others infer you thought they'd be envious.
- **Backhanded compliment** — comes with kind words, but others realize you're trying to maintain status over them.

More broadly, the psychological literature catalogs many impression-management behaviors — ingratiation (flattery, agreement, deference), verbal self-description, taking credit for or downplaying successes and failures, association with people or groups, modifying physical appearance, conspicuous use of possessions, and even the food one eats — each aimed at reactions like liking, approval, belonging, status, and respect, and each capable of leading to irrationality.

To avoid irrational impression management: (1) avoid the mistakes shown above (humblebragging, self-deprecation, paternalistic helping, hiding successes, backhanded compliments); and (2) since over-focusing on impression management pulls you out of step with your values (e.g., exaggerating achievements conflicts with honesty), get clear on your deepest values and set goals aligned with them.
