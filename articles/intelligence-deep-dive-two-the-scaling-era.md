The last decade of artificial intelligence developments was largely inspired by scaling laws, first described by Jared Kaplan and later refined by Jordan Hoffmann, which suggest that the performance of an AI model will improve by a predictable amount with a given increase in computational resources and training data size.[^cite-227]

These are not laws of nature (like the law of universal gravitation); rather they are experimentally observed patterns (like Moore’s Law, which states that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles roughly every two years). Like Moore’s Law, the AI scaling regularities are remarkably robust: you can accurately predict the performance of a model with a thousand parameters as well as one with a trillion. The difference is the speed: AI models’ capabilities have been doubling every three to six months, not every two years. In the words of [deep learning pioneer Ilya Sutskever](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/pLHWn5SQvLM): “If you mix some compute with some data into a neural net of a certain size, you will get results, and you will know that it will be better if you just scale the recipe up. \[...\] Companies love this because it gives you a very low-risk way of investing your resources.” This observation characterizes the scaling era in AI.

However, more recent empirical work suggests that the predictive power of scaling laws may be more limited than initially assumed. While early studies demonstrated smooth improvements with scale,[^cite-228] subsequent analyses show that downstream performance and real-world capabilities often exhibit irregular, domain-dependent scaling behavior.[^cite-229] In some cases, smaller, more efficiently trained models outperform significantly larger systems, indicating that scale alone is not a reliable predictor of capability.[^cite-230] These developments suggest that the relationship between compute and performance is becoming increasingly complex, with gains now often driven by improvements in data quality, training procedures, model architecture, and inference-time compute rather than model size alone.

As a result, the scaling paradigm is increasingly complemented—and in some cases challenged—by approaches that emphasize sample efficiency and computational efficiency rather than sheer size. Advances such as improved data curation, model distillation, instruction tuning, and retrieval-augmented generation have demonstrated substantial performance gains without proportional increases in compute.[^cite-231]

It is not clear when the scaling era will end, or if it already is ending. Despite the release of ever-larger models, we have seen several smaller models rise to near the top of leaderboards.[^cite-232] Another quote from Sutskever implies that research may be more important than just scaling today: “[Scaling the pre-training is essentially tapped out](https://medium.com/@intellizab/ilya-sutskever-just-declared-the-end-of-the-scaling-era-5460f19c009e). If you just multiply the scale by 100x now, you won’t get a qualitative change in capability.”

## The Race to Scale: Compute ^the-race-to-scale

The race to scale has had a number of important consequences.

First, the extreme compute resources required to build such models began to dramatically limit the number of organizations capable of developing frontier AI models. According to [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/trends), the computing power used to train frontier language models has grown roughly fivefold per year since 2020, and the largest known AI data center now has the capacity of more than a million of Nvidia’s H100 specialized AI chips (GPUs). The capital cost of acquiring such computing infrastructure would place it beyond the reach of all but the very wealthiest international universities, while the associated energy costs alone would dwarf the budgets of most academic research projects. As a result, the development of very large, state-of-the-art models became increasingly concentrated within a small number of companies worldwide, most notably the major Silicon Valley technology firms (see also discussion in SQ8). In turn, this has meant that the development of leading AI models increasingly occurs behind closed doors.

This concentration reflects a broader structural dynamic in which access to large-scale compute becomes a prerequisite for participation in frontier research, with breakthroughs increasingly clustered in [well-capitalized organizations and regions](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/accelerating-europes-ai-adoption-the-role-of-sovereign-ai).[^cite-233] The fact that general-purpose AI would in effect largely be owned by a small number of U.S.-based private companies prompted a number of nation-states to begin considering the possibility of sovereign AI capabilities, for example, investing in [national](https://www.sovereignai.gov.uk/) and [international](https://www.euronews.com/2026/03/03/europe-unites-to-build-sovereign-cloud-and-ai-infrastructure-to-stop-reliance-on-us) schemes to develop independent AI infrastructure and GPT-class AI systems. In the U.S., shortly after his second-term inauguration, President Donald J. Trump announced an initiative called [Stargate](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/jan/21/trump-ai-joint-venture-openai-oracle-softbank), aimed at developing a $500 billion strategic compute resource for U.S. AI.

A second major consequence of the race to scale was the dramatic increase in demand for both the semiconductors required to train these models and the data on which they depend. We examined the geopolitical implications in Deep Dive One: Supply Chains and Environmental Impact; here, it is sufficient to note that this demand led to an extraordinary increase in the valuations of leading AI chip manufacturers, most notably Nvidia, the company that pioneered the development of GPUs. In October 2025, [Nvidia reached a market capitalization of $5 trillion](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cp8e970vn5vo), the largest of any company in the world, arguably making it the most valuable company in human history. This valuation exceeded the GDP of almost every nation on Earth. This extraordinary growth was driven purely by the demand for the semiconductors that are developed by Nvidia, which are the chip of choice for training AI models.

More generally, the race to scale prompted vast investment in data centers: estimates suggest that if current demand continues then [a further $6.7 trillion capital investment](https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/technology-media-and-telecommunications/our-insights/the-cost-of-compute-a-7-trillion-dollar-race-to-scale-data-centers) in data centers will be required. Many serious commentators suggest that this enormous capital investment, coupled with spend on semiconductors and ancillary hardware, is actually [propping up the U.S. economy](https://www.wired.com/story/data-center-ai-boom-us-economy-jobs/), possibly even preventing it from going into recession. The [European AI Strategy](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence) builds upon the concept of cross-national centralized AI Gigafactories—massive, gigawatt-level data centers with up to 100,000 advanced AI processors designed to enable next-generation models.

The economic justification for these investments relies on the assumption of sustained returns to scale. Yet studies suggest that the marginal gains from additional compute may be decreasing over time.[^cite-234] If so, the long-term sustainability of continued exponential investment in infrastructure may be uncertain.

There has been [considerable pushback](https://www.datacenterwatch.org/) about the scale of data center development, on a number of grounds. First, data centers in 2026 are physically enormous facilities; they are noisy and tend not to be aesthetically pleasing. It is therefore understandable that people living in the vicinity of a new data center development would resent the imposition of such facilities in their neighborhood. Some people protest the impact that large companies and technologies have on our society, and these protests have even led to [violence](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/indianapolis-councilman-says-shots-fired-at-home-and-no-data-centers-note-left-at-door). The environmental impact of data centers (energy, water, CO2) is an important issue that we cover in Deep Dive One: Supply Chains and Environmental Impact. Data centers do provide some jobs, but only about one-tenth the number provided by a similarly sized traditional manufacturing facility.

The financial model underpinning data center development on the current scale has come into question. The argument for building data centers is that the new technology requires it, and that the up-front cost will later be recouped by the benefits gained. An analogy is often made with the development of railroads in the 19th century. Laying down railroads was enormously costly at the time, but the basic infrastructure had a long timeline: railroad tracks themselves are typically good for decades, and the infrastructure to support them can have a lifetime measured in centuries. By contrast, the computers that populate data centers often get replaced after several years as newer technologies become available. Thus not only is the current investment in data centers enormous but it has a very short window in which to show returns.

Finally, the extraordinary scale of current investment in AI infrastructure has raised serious questions about economic sustainability. Much of this investment has been financed through borrowing and external capital, including pension funds and private equity, rather than solely through retained earnings of technology firms. Despite the bullish claims of big tech companies, it is far from clear whether anticipated returns justify this level of expenditure, especially as models are often improved by training new versions rather than refining existing ones. In short, [the scale of investment in data centers risks a market crash](https://time.com/article/2026/03/26/we-must-prepare-for-an-ai-bubble-now/) unless markets remain convinced that investments will be recouped.

## The Race to Scale: Data ^the-race-to-scale-2

The race to scale also triggered an intense scramble for training data. Crucially, compute-optimal training regimes (for example, [Chinchilla](https://lifearchitect.ai/chinchilla/)) show that the training data required for a model must scale proportionally with model size: larger models require comparably larger training datasets in order to show improvements. And large language models are particularly data-hungry. Although precise figures for more recent models are not publicly available, OpenAI disclosed that GPT-3 was trained on approximately 45 terabytes of textual data. At this scale, the World Wide Web provides the only realistic source of such volumes of text, and it quickly became standard practice to scrape text from virtually every available online resource. These resources included online public data sources such as Wikipedia, social media sources such as Twitter, and discussion forums such as Reddit, as well as commercial and governmental websites.

The widespread use of data sources like the World Wide Web provoked significant concerns about copyright. If an LLM has effectively ingested substantial portions of the World Wide Web, it has necessarily absorbed vast quantities of copyrighted material. Unsurprisingly, this led to widespread objections from rights holders and, ultimately, to a [growing number of legal challenges](https://copyrightalliance.org/ai-copyright-case-developments-2025/) in multiple jurisdictions. AI model providers argued that the use of these data sources in training AI models did not represent a copyright infringement since, firstly, LLMs do not store copyrighted works in any conventional sense (one cannot in general point to any specific part of an LLM’s neural networks and say, “That’s where my data is stored”), and, secondly, LLMs are not derivative works in the sense of copyright law. Copyright law was simply not designed with AI models and neural networks in mind.

There are several open lawsuits, such as the one where [the New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/27/business/media/new-york-times-open-ai-microsoft-lawsuit.html) over their use of copyrighted work. We cannot judge at this point how the many lawsuits underway will pan out; nor can we predict what the legal landscape around data, copyright, and AI models will ultimately look like. Summer 2025 court decisions in the U.S. have suggested AI use of copyrighted material for training is fair use, including cases against [Meta](https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/boards-policy-regulation/meta-fends-off-authors-us-copyright-lawsuit-over-ai-2025-06-25/) and [Anthropic](https://www.debevoise.com/insights/publications/2025/06/anthropic-and-meta-decisions-on-fair-use). At the same time, Anthropic was found to have used books in training without paying for them and eventually settled the lawsuit related to the pirated books with a $1.5 billion payout. This is the [largest copyright settlement in legal history](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/sep/05/anthropic-settlement-ai-book-lawsuit) (by some considerable distance).

Diverse and large datasets are valuable. Moreover, because large training runs are costly and therefore infrequent, systematic experimentation to determine how best to use limited data sources is not feasible. Because data is valuable, it is increasingly treated as a strategic asset. Frontier models are now trained on mixtures of public, licensed, and proprietary datasets, with exclusive access to datasets conferring significant competitive advantages for model providers.

This shift has intensified legal and political scrutiny over data usage, exposing fundamental ambiguities in existing legal frameworks that cover large-scale data ingestion and transformation. Much modern digital infrastructure now embeds data collection along with the services it provides. Televisions, self-driving vehicles, wearable devices, doorbell cameras, smart chatbot interactions, police body cameras, and more have all become platforms for data collection in addition to their primary purpose, [leading to many objections](https://www.techpolicy.press/in-house-data-harvest-how-social-platforms-ai-ambitions-threaten-user-rights/). Regulatory regimes such as the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) have begun to impose constraints on data collection and reuse, though they will need to adapt to the emerging realities of generative AI. The result is a fragmented governance landscape in which data access is simultaneously a technical, economic, and legal problem.

In response to the limitations of static datasets, there is growing interest in physical AI, encompassing robotics, simulation environments, and world models that allow for interaction rather than just passive observation. By generating data through embodied experience—whether in real-world systems or high-fidelity simulations—these approaches offer a pathway to bypass the constraints of preexisting datasets. This paradigm is particularly relevant for domains such as robotics, navigation, scientific discovery, and complex system modeling, where relevant data cannot be easily scraped from the internet but must instead be produced through experimentation or simulation.

Another obvious response to data scarcity is to use synthetic data.[^cite-235] While synthetic data has demonstrated value in specific contexts, it is not a universal substitute for real-world data, in part due to risks such as reinforcement of biases in the model. Instead, it is best understood as complementary to ground-truth data. More broadly, its increasing use reflects a shift toward more flexible training regimes in which data distributions can be actively shaped rather than passively accumulated.[^cite-236] A growing body of work demonstrates that improvements in data quality—through deduplication, pruning, and prioritization—can reduce the need for larger datasets while maintaining or improving performance.[^cite-237] These findings suggest a shift from treating data as a quantity to be maximized toward treating it as a resource to be optimized, with carefully curated datasets often yielding greater value than continued expansion of raw data volume.[^cite-238]

## Scaling and the Future of AI ^scaling-and-the-future

For the reasons described above, the future may not be a straightforward extrapolation of scaling of the kind we have seen over the recent past, but may instead require more innovative ways to use limited data and compute resources to build and run AI models.[^cite-239] Scaling laws remain empirically useful within constrained regimes, but they no longer provide a sufficient explanation for frontier capability gains.

Parallel to the scaling trend, a second current of innovation is focused on efficiency. Algorithmic advances have played a key role in recent progress. Approaches such as instruction tuning, low-rank adaptation, quantization, distillation, mixtures of experts, KV cache compression, speculative decoding, and weight sharing demonstrated that smaller, less expensive models could approach the performance of frontier models. This development has widened access to frontier methods, enabling broader experimentation and lowering barriers to entry. Gains from algorithmic efficiency may rival or exceed those from scaling alone.[^cite-240]

The field is increasingly moving beyond training monolithic models from scratch on fixed datasets. Emerging approaches emphasize continual learning, in which models are updated incrementally as new data becomes available, and modular architectures, which decompose intelligence into interacting subsystems rather than using a single, static network. Empirical progress in this direction suggests that future AI systems may rely less on one-time large-scale data ingestion and more on sustained, dynamic interaction with evolving data environments—agents improve continuously by acting, observing outcomes, and incorporating feedback, producing effectively unbounded streams of data that scale with capability rather than dataset size.[^cite-241] In addition, increasing attention is being paid to inference-time computation, where additional compute is applied during deployment rather than training. Techniques such as search, tool use, and agent-based coordination have been shown to significantly enhance performance without modifying model parameters.[^cite-242] This represents a notable departure from traditional scaling approaches (and has implications for increased computational costs).

The scaling era has been a defining feature of recent AI progress, but scaling is now evolving beyond the straightforward expansion of model size, training data, and compute. While these dimensions enabled rapid early gains, their limitations—particularly diminishing returns, inefficiencies, and concentration effects—suggest that future progress will depend increasingly on data efficiency, algorithmic design, and system-level integration. Longer contexts, autonomous agents, personalized systems, and deployment across populations and domains broaden what it means to scale, but also make reliability harder to establish: benchmarks can conceal uneven performance and agentic workflows can exceed effective human oversight. The central challenge is therefore no longer maximizing a single dimension of scale but optimizing the interaction between compute, data, and algorithms while also scaling evidence of validity, robustness, representativeness, and societal value.
