Stoic philosophy shares similarities with evidence-based psychotherapies for anxiety and depression and has surged in popularity across academia and self-help communities. Preliminary research suggests that Stoic training may reduce rumination, anxiety, and depression, but findings are limited by small samples, lack of control groups, and unclear mechanisms of change. This randomised waitlist-controlled trial evaluated the effects of a two-week online Stoic training programme on rumination, experiential avoidance, depression, and anxiety, and examined whether shifts in Stoic attitudes explained observed changes. Participants from a non-clinical sample were allocated to either the Stoic training group (n = 56) or a waitlist control group (n = 63). The intervention involved three daily Stoic practices and readings every other day. Assessments were conducted at baseline, post-intervention, and one-month follow-up. Complete case analysis of data was conducted using 2x3 Mixed ANOVAs. Group by timepoint interactions were explored using ANCOVAs with baseline scores as covariates. One-way ANOVAS explored within groups changes.  
  
The training group showed significantly lower post-intervention and follow-up experiential avoidance and depression symptoms, and reduced anxiety at follow-up compared to the waitlist group. Stoic attitudes increased significantly in the training group compared to the control group across the study. Within group analyses showed significant reductions in experiential avoidance and depression symptoms with large effect sizes, increases in Stoic attitudes, decreases in anxiety symptoms with medium to large effect sizes, and a significant decrease in rumination at follow-up only. Clinically significant improvement in depression occurred in 38% of the training group post-intervention and 34% at follow-up. For anxiety, 25% showed clinical improvement post-training, increasing to 32% at follow-up.  
  
This is the first methodologically rigorous evaluation of Stoic training. Findings suggest that brief Stoic interventions may meaningfully reduce experiential avoidance and depressive symptoms, with delayed improvements in anxiety. Future research should test longer-term interventions, include active control groups, and further explore the role of Stoic attitudes as a mechanism of change.
