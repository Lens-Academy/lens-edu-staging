The **Internal Family Systems** (IFS) model is a [non-pathologizing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strength-based_practice "Strength-based practice") approach to individual [psychotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychotherapy "Psychotherapy") developed by [Richard C. Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_C._Schwartz "Richard C. Schwartz"). It combines [systems thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_psychology "Systems psychology") with the view that the mind is composed of relatively discrete [subpersonalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subpersonality "Subpersonality"), or "parts," each with its own unique viewpoint and qualities. The goal of the therapy is to help clients access their "[Self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self "Self")"—described as a core state of compassion and clarity that remains undamaged by [trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_trauma "Psychological trauma")—to heal and integrate these parts. In 2015, IFS was listed on the [National Registry of Evidence-based Programs and Practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Registry_of_Evidence-based_Programs_and_Practices "National Registry of Evidence-based Programs and Practices") (NREPP) as an [evidence-based practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence-based_practice "Evidence-based practice"). A 2025 [scoping review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_review "Systematic review") published in _Clinical Psychologist_ identified IFS as a "promising therapeutic approach" for conditions such as [PTSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder "Post-traumatic stress disorder"), [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder "Major depressive disorder"), and [chronic pain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_pain "Chronic pain"), noting significant symptom reduction in [pilot trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_experiment "Pilot experiment"). It is frequently applied in the treatment of [complex post-traumatic stress disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_post-traumatic_stress_disorder "Complex post-traumatic stress disorder") (C-PTSD), [anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety_disorder "Anxiety disorder"), and [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_\(mood\) "Depression (mood)").

The model has been widely adopted in [clinical trauma treatment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma-informed_care "Trauma-informed care") and popularized by media such as the film _[Inside Out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inside_Out "Inside Out")_. However, it has also faced scrutiny regarding its training practices and rapid expansion. A 2025 investigative report by _[New York Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Magazine "New York Magazine")_ criticized the model as "[pseudoscientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience")," alleging that "parts work" can be destabilizing for clients with [complex trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_post-traumatic_stress_disorder "Complex post-traumatic stress disorder") if protective mechanisms are bypassed too quickly. In response to the report, the IFS Institute disputed the characterization of the therapy as pseudoscientific, stating that the cases cited involved extreme [misapplications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malpractice "Malpractice") of the model distinct from standard training protocols.

## Theory and background ^theory-and-background

IFS has three core theoretical principles: Non-pathological [multiplicity of the mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_identity "Plural identity"), [systems theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_theory "Systems theory"), and an adaptation of [Buddhist philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_philosophy "Buddhist philosophy")'s practice of [witnessing consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindfulness "Mindfulness"). Broadly, these principles are used to non-judgmentally observe and evaluate complex mental systems. This approach builds on Richard Schwartz's experiences with persons experiencing [eating disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eating_disorder "Eating disorder"), who he noticed often spoke about the self as various conflicting "parts" or sub-personalities. Trained as a [family therapist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_therapy "Family therapy"), Schwartz noticed that these parts exhibited common presentations in family therapy, leading him to develop the approach. IFS is distinct from other parts-based therapies (such as [Gestalt therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestalt_therapy "Gestalt therapy")) in its emphasis on the systemic relationships between parts and the existence of an undamaged "Self" that acts as an active leader of the system, rather than a passive witness.

Unlike [psychodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychodynamic_therapy "Psychodynamic therapy") or [cognitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_therapy "Cognitive therapy") approaches that focus on [insight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insight "Insight") or [behavioral change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavior_change_\(individual\) "Behavior change (individual)"), IFS is an experiential therapy. The goal is not merely to talk about the parts, but to directly engage with them in the present moment to facilitate an emotional shift (often described as "unburdening").

### Metaphysical distinctions ^metaphysical-distinctions

Richard Schwartz's later work introduces [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics") concepts distinct from the core clinical model, specifically "guides" and "unattached burdens," which are described as external entities rather than internal parts. According to Schwartz, unattached burdens—described as [ancestral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestor "Ancestor") energies or external entities—tend to enter a person's mind during [disassociated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociation_\(psychology\) "Dissociation (psychology)") states, such as [trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_trauma "Psychological trauma") or [psychedelic experiences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychedelic_experience "Psychedelic experience"). "Guides" are described as benevolent entities, whereas unattached burdens do not have a positive intent.

In recent expansions of the model, Schwartz has explicitly aligned these concepts with [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") traditions, describing the "[Self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self "Self")" not merely as a [psychological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological "Psychological") state but as a sacred [essence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essence "Essence") connecting the individual to a larger "[Divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity "Divinity")" [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness "Consciousness"). This approach draws parallels between IFS unburdening techniques and [shamanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamanism "Shamanism") practices. Schwartz proposes that negative entities can be removed using [visualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_image "Mental image") techniques (such as imagery of light or fire), a practice labeled "[exorcism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exorcism "Exorcism")" by critics but described by proponents as an [energetic release](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catharsis "Catharsis") or the healing of [intergenerational trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transgenerational_trauma "Transgenerational trauma").

While IFS utilizes language that can appear spiritual, other proponents describe these terms as [phenomenological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_\(psychology\) "Phenomenology (psychology)") [metaphors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor "Metaphor") for [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network_\(biology\) "Neural network (biology)") or dissociated affective states. In clinical practice, the "Self" is often framed not as a metaphysical [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul "Soul"), but as a state of "neurological integration" where the [prefrontal cortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefrontal_cortex "Prefrontal cortex") is able to observe emotional reactivity without being overwhelmed by it.

### Theoretical foundations and distinctions ^theoretical-foundations-and-distinctions

While the concept of the mind as a multiple entity exists in earlier schools of psychology—including the ["Id, Ego, and Superego"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Id,_ego_and_superego "Id, ego and superego") of [Psychoanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalysis "Psychoanalysis") and the "Parent, Adult, Child" model of [Transactional Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactional_Analysis "Transactional Analysis")—IFS diverges from these models in its specific orientation toward the parts.

Earlier models often focused on strengthening the "Ego" or "Adult" to manage or control the other parts, which were frequently viewed as primitive or chaotic. In contrast, IFS posits that the "manager" (or Ego) is itself a collection of protective parts, not the core "Self".

The goal of IFS is not to use the Ego to control the system, but to "unblend" parts to reveal the _Self_—described by Schwartz as an undamaged, innate essence characterized by qualities such as calmness, curiosity, and compassion. This parallels [Jungian psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jungian_psychology "Jungian psychology")'s practice of "Active Imagination", though IFS systematizes the process into a clinical protocol. Schwartz also cites the influence of [Virginia Satir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Satir "Virginia Satir")'s "Parts Party" and [Gestalt therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gestalt_therapy "Gestalt therapy") (specifically the empty chair technique) as precursors to the direct engagement with internal subpersonalities.

## The Self and parts ^the-self-and-parts

IFS posits that the mind is made up of [multiple parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plural_identity "Plural identity"), and underlying them is a person's core or true [Self](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self "Self"). [Like members of a family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_therapy "Family therapy"), a person's inner parts can take on [extreme roles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysfunctional_family "Dysfunctional family") or subpersonalities. Each part has its own perspective, interests, memories, and viewpoint. A core tenet of IFS is that every part has a [positive intent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altruism "Altruism"), even if its actions are counterproductive or cause [dysfunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abnormality_\(behavior\) "Abnormality (behavior)"). There is no need to fight with, coerce, or eliminate parts; the IFS method promotes internal connection and harmony to bring the mind back into balance.

### The Self ^the-self

IFS therapy aims to heal wounded parts and restore mental balance. The first step is to access the core Self and then, from there, understand the different parts in order to heal them. According to Schwartz, "Self energy" (which permits healing) can be deepened or accessed during a "[non-dual state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondualism "Nondualism")," a state he notes is sometimes facilitated by the use of [psychedelics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychedelic_drug "Psychedelic drug").

Schwartz identifies eight qualities that characterize the state of "Self-energy," often referred to as the "8 Cs":

-   [Compassion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compassion "Compassion")
-   [Curiosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curiosity "Curiosity")
-   [Clarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insight "Insight")
-   [Creativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creativity "Creativity")
-   [Calm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calmness "Calmness")
-   [Confidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence "Confidence")
-   [Courage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courage "Courage")
-   [Connectedness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nondualism "Nondualism")

### Parts ^parts

In the IFS model, parts are categorized into three roles:

-   **Exiles:** Parts that carry the [pain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_pain "Psychological pain"), [shame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shame "Shame"), or [fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear "Fear") from past [trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_trauma "Psychological trauma"). They are often [isolated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repression_\(psychoanalysis\) "Repression (psychoanalysis)") ("exiled") from the rest of the system to prevent the individual from feeling this pain.
-   **Managers:** A type of "Protector" part that plays a proactive role. They try to [maintain control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Executive_functions "Executive functions") and prevent the Exiles from being [triggered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma_trigger "Trauma trigger") by managing the environment, relationships, and daily functioning.
    -   **Self-like parts**: A specific subtype of manager that imitates the qualities of the Self. These parts often appear calm, analytical, or even compassionate, but they lack the true connectivity and curiosity of the Self. Distinguishing between the "true Self" and a "Self-like part" is a key aspect of the clinical protocol.
-   **Firefighters:** A type of "Protector" part that plays a reactive role. When an Exile is triggered (breaking through the Managers' defenses), Firefighters [act impulsively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coping#Negative_techniques_\(maladaptive_coping_or_non-coping\) "Coping") to extinguish the emotional pain, often through behaviors like [substance use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_use_disorder "Substance use disorder"), [binge eating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binge_eating "Binge eating"), or [dissociation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociation_\(psychology\) "Dissociation (psychology)").

### Relationships between parts ^relationships-between-parts

IFS focuses on the relationships between parts and the core Self. The goal of therapy is to create a cooperative and trusting relationship between the Self and each part. There are three primary types of relationships:

-   **Protection:** Provided by managers and firefighters, who intend to spare exiles from harm and protect the individual from the exile's pain.
-   **Polarization:** Occurs between two parts that battle each other to determine how a person feels or behaves in a certain situation. Each part believes that it must act as it does in order to counter the extreme behavior of the other part.
-   **Alliance:** Formed between two different parts if they are working together to accomplish the same goal.

### Cultural and legacy burdens ^cultural-and-legacy-burdens

In addition to "personal burdens" acquired from a client's direct life experience, IFS posits that parts can carry _legacy burdens_—beliefs, emotions, or shame transmitted through family lines (aligning with the concept of [transgenerational trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transgenerational_trauma "Transgenerational trauma")). The model also identifies _cultural burdens_, which are beliefs absorbed from societal systems (such as [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism "Racism"), [patriarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchy "Patriarchy"), or [individualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individualism "Individualism")) that parts internalize to survive in their environment. Schwartz argues that healing requires unburdening these systemic influences to allow the Self to lead without the constraints of societal conditioning.

## Method ^method

IFS practitioners report a therapeutic method for individual therapy based on the following principles. In this description, the term "protector" refers to either a manager or firefighter.

Parts in extreme roles carry "[burdens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shame "Shame")": painful emotions or negative beliefs they have taken on as a result of past harmful experiences, often in [childhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Childhood_trauma "Childhood trauma"). These burdens are not intrinsic to the part and therefore they can be released or "unburdened" through IFS therapy, allowing the part to assume its natural healthy role. The Self is the agent of psychological healing. Therapists help their clients to access and remain in Self, providing guidance along the way. Protectors often soften up or release from their roles when they establish a harmonious relationship with the Self.

Some protectors unburden themselves from their roles only once the exiles they are protecting have been unburdened. There is no attempt to work with exiles until the client has obtained permission from the protectors who are protecting it. It is paramount in IFS therapy that the therapist ensures that the client's Self has established trust and safety with the protectors before approaching the exiles. IFS therapy emphasizes that when protectors are not respected or bypassed, there is potential for their backlash. Such bypassing can result sometimes in some serious firefighter parts activating, such as [suicidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide "Suicide") parts. Proponents argue that this protocol makes the method relatively [safe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trauma-informed_care "Trauma-informed care"), even when working with traumatized parts.

The Self is the natural leader of the internal system. However, because of past harmful incidents or relationships, protectors have stepped in and taken over for the Self. One protector after another is activated and takes the lead, causing [dysfunctional behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abnormality_\(behavior\) "Abnormality (behavior)"). Protectors are also frequently in conflict with each other, resulting in internal chaos or stagnation. The aim is for the protectors to trust the Self and allow it to lead the system, creating internal harmony under its guidance.

The first step is to help the client access the Self. There are two methods for accessing Self-energy of the client:

-   **Insight approach:** Clients self-engage with the protector parts facilitated by the therapist.
-   **Direct access:** A technique where the therapist talks directly to the client's part (rather than the client reporting what the part is saying). This is often used when a protector part is too blended to separate from the client's Self.

### Differentiation (Unblending) ^differentiation-unblending

A primary technique in IFS is the "6 Fs," a protocol used to help the client [differentiate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_psychology#Individuation "Analytical psychology") ("unblend") their core Self from a protective part:

-   **Find:** Locate the part in the body or mind.
-   **Focus:** Turn attention inward toward the part.
-   **Flesh out:** Describe the part's sensory details (image, sound, or sensation).
-   **Feel toward:** Assess how the client feels toward the part (e.g., annoyed, afraid, or curious). If the feeling is not neutral or compassionate, another part is blended and must be asked to step back.
-   **Befriend:** Learn about the part's role and positive intent.
-   **Fear:** Ask the part what it fears would happen if it stopped doing its job.

Once the Self gets to know the protector(s), and the protector(s) feel understood by the Self, the internal [attachment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attachment_theory "Attachment theory") rupture is repaired. The process of understanding the protector's positive intent through witnessing can be a slow and iterative process. Then, with the protector's permission, the client accesses the exile(s) to uncover the childhood incident or relationship that is the source of the burden(s) it carries. The exile is retrieved from the past situation and guided to release its burdens. Finally, the protector can then let go of its protective role and assume a less extreme role or a healthy one.

### Protocol for working with exiles ^protocol-for-working-with

Once protectors have granted permission, the process for working with exiles follows a specific sequence designed to heal the wounded part. This protocol is often described in steps including witnessing, reparenting, retrieving, and unburdening:

-   **Witnessing**: The client's "Self" invites the exile to share its story or memory. The Self witnesses the pain and validates the exile's experience without being overwhelmed by it.
-   **Reparenting**: The Self enters the memory (imaginally) to offer the exile the love, protection, and validation it needed at the time of the trauma but did not receive.
-   **Retrieval**: The Self invites the exile to leave the traumatic scene or time period and come to a safe place in the present (such as an imaginal garden, the client's current home, or another safe location).
-   **Unburdening**: The exile is invited to release the painful emotions, beliefs, or physical sensations ("burdens") it has carried. This is often visualized as releasing a substance (like sludge or smoke) or giving up a heavy object to the elements (fire, water, wind, earth, or light).
-   **Invitation**: Once unburdened, the exile is invited to receive positive qualities (such as joy, playfulness, or innocence) to fill the space where the burden previously existed.

### Mechanism of change ^mechanism-of-change

Proponents of the model argue that the "witnessing" and "unburdening" phases of IFS parallel the neurological process of [memory reconsolidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_reconsolidation "Memory reconsolidation"), by satisfying the brain's requirements for unlocking and updating a [traumatic memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traumatic_memories "Traumatic memories") pathway. Specifically, the model simultaneously activates the original emotional memory (via the exile) while creating a [contradictory experience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_dissonance "Cognitive dissonance") of safety and compassion (via the Self), a state known as a "mismatch experience" or prediction error, which is necessary to permanently revise [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network "Neural network").

## Specialized protocols ^specialized-protocols

### Couples therapy (IFIO) ^couples-therapy-ifio

Intimacy from the Inside Out (IFIO) is a specific protocol for applying IFS to [couples therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Couples_therapy "Couples therapy"), developed by Toni Herbine-Blank. The method focuses on helping each partner "unblend" from their protective parts during conflict to communicate from a place of Self-leadership. A core technique involves the "U-Turn," where partners are guided to turn their attention inward to their own reactive parts rather than focusing on their partner's behavior.

### Somatic IFS ^somatic-ifs

Somatic IFS, developed by Susan McConnell, integrates the IFS model with [somatic psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatic_psychology "Somatic psychology"). This approach emphasizes that parts are not merely mental constructs but are anchored in the body and nervous system. The clinical practice involves five tools: somatic awareness, conscious breathing, radical resonance, mindful movement, and attuned touch. The goal is to help clients witness and unburden parts that are held as chronic tension or physical symptoms.

## Reception and research evidence ^reception-and-research-evidence

In a 2013 [randomized controlled trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial "Randomized controlled trial") with 79 participants published in the [Journal of Rheumatology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Journal_of_Rheumatology "The Journal of Rheumatology"), [rheumatoid arthritis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheumatoid_arthritis "Rheumatoid arthritis") patients receiving IFS therapy demonstrated [statistically significant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_significance "Statistical significance") reductions in pain and physical impairment (p < .05) and [depressive symptoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder "Major depressive disorder") (p < .01) compared to the control group, though the intervention did not yield significant improvements in objective measures of disease activity (joint swelling and inflammation). The study noted that sustained improvements were observed one year post-intervention.

In 2015, IFS was listed on the [National Registry of Evidence-based Programs and Practices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Registry_of_Evidence-based_Programs_and_Practices "National Registry of Evidence-based Programs and Practices") (NREPP) as an [evidence-based practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence-based_practice "Evidence-based practice"). The registry rated the model as 'effective' for improving general functioning and [well-being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-being "Well-being"), and 'promising' for treating [phobias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobia "Phobia"), [panic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_disorder "Panic disorder"), and [generalized anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_anxiety_disorder "Generalized anxiety disorder"). The NREPP was subsequently discontinued and removed from the SAMHSA website in 2018.

The model gained significant prominence in the field of trauma therapy following the publication of _[The Body Keeps the Score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Body_Keeps_the_Score "The Body Keeps the Score")_ (2015) by psychiatrist [Bessel van der Kolk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_van_der_Kolk "Bessel van der Kolk"), who endorsed IFS as an effective treatment for emotional regulation and somatic dissociation.

In 2021, a pilot feasibility study of IFS for 17 adults with [PTSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder "Post-traumatic stress disorder") and a history of childhood trauma showed a significant reduction in symptoms, with 92% of completers no longer meeting diagnostic criteria after 16 weeks. However, authors noted that as a pilot study without a control group, these findings were preliminary.

While large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain limited compared to [modalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therapy "Therapy") like [cognitive behavioral therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_behavioral_therapy "Cognitive behavioral therapy"), a 2025 [scoping review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_review "Systematic review") published in _Clinical Psychologist_ analyzed the growing body of [peer-reviewed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review "Peer review") research. The review identified IFS as a "promising therapeutic approach" for conditions such as [PTSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder "Post-traumatic stress disorder"), [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder "Major depressive disorder"), and [chronic pain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_pain "Chronic pain"), noting significant symptom reduction in [pilot trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot_experiment "Pilot experiment"). However, the authors concluded that further rigorous, large-sample studies are required to fully establish [efficacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Efficacy "Efficacy") and determining specific [contraindications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contraindication "Contraindication").

In 2025, [_New York Magazine_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_\(magazine\) "New York (magazine)") ([_The Cut_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cut_\(publication\) "The Cut (publication)")) published an investigative report linking IFS techniques to [malpractice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malpractice "Malpractice") allegations at the [Castlewood Treatment Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alsana "Alsana"). Former patients alleged that aggressive "parts work" was used to induce [false memories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_memory "False memory") of abuse. Richard Schwartz had served as a paid consultant for the facility. In response, the IFS Institute stated that the specific malpractice allegations at Castlewood Treatment Center involved a former consultant rather than the model's founder, and reiterated that official IFS protocol explicitly cautions therapists against validating recovered memories without corroborating evidence. It was stated that the [coercive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coercion "Coercion") techniques described—such as forcing "parts" to reveal trauma before a client is stable—violate the model's core safety protocols.

## History ^history

IFS was developed by [Richard C. Schwartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_C._Schwartz "Richard C. Schwartz") in the 1980s. Schwartz developed the model based on his work with [family therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_therapy "Family therapy") clients who described their internal lives as interacting parts, though he has also cited [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") influences and "guides" in the development of his later theories. The model was initially met with skepticism in academic [psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatry "Psychiatry"), with some concerns raised regarding its safety. However, it later gained significant traction among clinicians. Schwartz eventually left academia and pursued a "grassroots" approach, promoting the model to a wider audience, including [practitioners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychotherapy "Psychotherapy"), [coaches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coaching "Coaching"), and the general public. In 2000, he founded the Center for Self-Leadership (later renamed the IFS Institute) to promote the therapy.

The model gained significantly wider public recognition in 2015, a surge Schwartz attributes to the release of the Pixar film _[Inside Out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inside_Out "Inside Out")_, which depicts a similar internal system of personified emotions.
