_TLDR: Figure out what you care about, stare at the hard problem, understand it to its object-level foundations, understand why the solutions don't work so far, write your assumptions and uncertainties about the universe, and go create value._

So a frightening amount of my job can be described as AI "strategy", which I think of as decomposing goals into action. Doing this well requires:

1.  Information and "information flow", up-to-date knowledge about the world and decisionmakers for the factors in that system
2.  Choosing an appropriate high-level goal
3.  The skill to break that goal into subgoals, to subsubgoals, then into action steps.

In research or in policy, choosing the right problem to work on and the right solutions to attempt is often called "taste." This is so valuable that when we're hiring for certain roles, we have this as the first filter: we assume that the amount of "taste" that a person has is an immovable object, and then see if we can select for or develop other traits (conscientiousness, domain knowledge) after that.

Conventional wisdom will have us believe this takes years to develop or is an innate talent. I don't know if this is the case! But I think that a whole lot of people would benefit from having more strategic taste, instead of deferring to other people's models of the problem/landscape of the solutions.

So, while not knowing at all whether this will work or not, I'll provide some procedure for developing a better sense of the question. And I'll restrict it to AI safety, because that's the field in which I think I have some experience.

---

## Understand the questions ^understand-the-questions

The thing about taste is that it requires knowing what the questions are. It is quite an obvious thing, but asking repeatedly — what do I care about? What is my big goal (make AI go well)? My big goal (ensure everyone has freedom)? And do an exercise of breaking it down from there.

## Reflect on what you care about ^reflect-on-what-you

If you want to figure out what you care about, think about what your goals are, and then ask why.

"I want to solve climate change." Why do I want to do that? "Because climate change will hurt millions of people in the next few years." Why does that matter? Because people's happiness and flourishing is terminally valuable. _That_ is what you care about.

"I want to earn money." Why? Money is not a terminal value (unless you terminally value a number going up in your bank account) — it only is valuable because you can use it to buy things. "I want money for a sense of security and the ability to support my family." Because their security and happiness is terminally valuable.

## Read ^read

The other thing is making sure that you are sufficiently well-read, not for being well-read's sake, but for two purposes:

**Making sure that you have every critical factor in the model.** Ideally, you want to minimize unknown unknowns, factors that if you realized them (eg, the intelligence community is actually really competent, and is doing location tracking already, and making a big deal of this will alert the Chinese) would change or even flip your actions.

**Helping you have the confidence in your own ideas** such that you say: "if I, fairly well-read and well-socialized in the field of X, have not heard of this idea, even if it has been written about in some obscure place already, it is still worth writing about / actioning about." TLDR: know enough so you know if your ideas are original enough.

A common failure mode is to pick a problem, decide that you will "lock in", and then shut out the rest of the world. Maximize your ability to find things out that could dramatically affect the strategic picture.

In practice, this means:

Moving to the place where this is done (SF for tech, DC for politics, LA for entertainment, NYC for everything else)

Surrounding yourself with people who have similar goals and different ones — you don't want to lock yourself in to some uninteresting problem, and cross-field synergies often work well

## Why won't things go well? ^why-wont-things-go

One fun exercise: so in the case of "oh this AI thing might not go well", say the words: "AI is going to go well", and notice what objections your gut brings up to that, instinctively. Break it into the subcomponents. Eg, "Superintelligent AIs will follow human instructions", "If technical AI alignment is solved, society will be okay", "Everyone will benefit from AI automation".

This is a bit like an exercise where you repeatedly say "my life is fine" out loud, then eg. "my social life is fine" "my professional life is fine"; "my relationship with my boss is fine" so on in smaller chunks, until you realize what is wrong, because you can hear the lie.

Continue to break down the points into smaller points until you **get confused about the object-level**.

Perhaps you are confused about some fact about the world (eg. will the AGI be coming soon?) or no longer at the object-level at all, but instead social reality: for example, "oh I'm actually just deferring to this person/'scientific consensus' who believes X" or "I'm just following the precautionary principle / \[some other heuristic\] generally applied".

## Resolve confusions ^resolve-confusions

Once you've done this, try your damnest to resolve your confusions about the object-level claim. Some ideas of how you could:

Doing a ton of directed reading/research

Chatting with the AIs — be careful, they tend to be good at amplifying whatever thing you subconsciously believe, and are not very good at fighting back

Speaking to a friend who has thought about this — in general, people who have just been around for longer have asked themselves the same questions, and would find it interesting to do that kind of exploration with you

Or experts in the field — I'd recommend reaching out to them cold with a specific question about their work, while doing enough prep such that it would be incredibly unlikely you could've figured out the answer by reading things on the internet / speaking with Claude

The one common one for policy people is _how technical should I be?_ Probably just as technical as you need to be to understand the shape of the problem. If you need a quick answer: [here's my best guess](https://jason.ml/technical). (will take 60-100 hours)

## Understand existing efforts ^understand-existing-efforts

Simultaneously, I'd recommend looking at the space of solutions, and understanding and developing opinions about them. There are at least some smart people that are trying to help — why are the things they are doing not sufficient?

Usually by asking this question, you'll realize there is maybe a structural thing going on (the bottleneck to any project happening is X funders' risk aversion, or lack of money overall, the people aren't that talented) or that they have missed a key strategic insight (you would be shocked at how few people actually do the steps outlined above, and reason from first principles).

## Isn't this obvious? ^isnt-this-obvious

**Most people are not actually trying to solve the problem.** They are not looking at the whole thing in any kind of holistic way, and then continuously and repeatedly asking themselves the question, "What is the most impactful thing I can do on the margin, given everything else that is going on, to help?"

**Personal fit is overrated.** I think that people generally overweight "personal fit." Yes, you shouldn't do something that you hate, but in general, I've seen many extremely talented people in one field, work and find enormous success doing something else.

**People are under dumb constraints.** The other thing is that very talented people can still be under very dumb constraints (visa reasons or family not allowing them to move to the place things are happening, policy being sufficiently poorly funded that all family-oriented people wanting to do something higher-paying, it not being "cool" among the social group that that person hangs out with to do this thing, people's attachment to their own identity as working on "hard technical problems" and not willing to schlepp).

**Status ladders are strong.** I'd also very strongly consider what the status ladders that are at play at the moment are, and what the general pull of the field is to do (consider, e.g., the SPAR to ERA/Pivotal to MATS to Anthropic Safety Fellow to Anthropic pipeline, for example). That's not to say high-status things aren't valuable, just that there is a well-trodden path in all fields, and you should be suspicious of them because they are less counterfactual.

**Selective agency.** Some very accomplished people tend to have selective agency ([related](https://jason.ml/execution)) where they can, for example, apply a lot of optimization pressure on applying to programs, but are less able to act without structure.

**Be wary of the "portfolio approach"** allowing you to justify doing anything — where the argument is implicitly that many people should be making many different bets, so I'm going to say "bet X that I want to do" is good because decorrelated bets are good. It's one thing to say "my portfolio approach works" but the principled way to do this, of course, is to elucidate all of the assumptions, figure out which sets of assumptions are systematically underrated by the portfolio.

---

If you take only one thing from this post, it is that you need to **stare at the hard problem**. This can be uncomfortable for a variety of reasons: it might make you realize that all of your previous work is less valuable than you thought. Once you've done this exercise, go create value.
