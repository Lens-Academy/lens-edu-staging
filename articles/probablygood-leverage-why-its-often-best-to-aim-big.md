**Leverage** refers to the influence you have over how resources like time, money, and labor (particularly of others) are spent. The more of these resources you’re able to influence, the larger your leverage. Increasing your leverage in a [promising cause area](https://probablygood.org/career-guide/analyzing-cause-areas/) can be a great way to increase your impact. In fact, high leverage is often what separates the most impactful careers from less impactful ones.

Let’s say you’ve been lucky enough to receive two job offers from a great charity. This particular charity distributes antibiotic tablets to [help fight trachoma](https://www.sightsavers.org/protecting-sight/ntds/what-is-trachoma/), a potentially blinding eye infection. The first job involves distributing these tablets directly to the people who need them. The second job is a [fundraising](https://probablygood.org/career-profiles/fundraising/) role. If you meet your fundraising targets, you’ll be able to fund _ten_ additional tablet distributors.

Which job should you accept?

Putting various complications aside, it’s clear the secondrole has potential for much more impact than the first. The main reason why is that it grants more _leverage_. In the fundraising role, you’re able to make ten times the difference by influencing the resources of others—first, by persuading people to donate and then by using this money to pay for the labor of additional antibiotic distributors.

## Examples of high leverage

This is just one example of how leverage allows you to put more resources towards solving pressing issues than the resources you can give yourself. There are several other kinds of roles that could allow you to influence resources beyond your own. Such roles might allow you, for example, to influence how budgets are spent, change the causes people work on, amplify important ideas, or enable others’ work.

To make things a little more concrete, here are a few more examples of how careers can give you leverage:

-   **Roles where you have influence over large sums of money**. For example, [working as a grantmaker](https://probablygood.org/career-profiles/grantmaking/) can allow you to direct sizable amounts of money at your discretion. Similarly, some governmental roles can let you influence where huge governmental budgets are spent.
-   **Roles where you [manage others](https://probablygood.org/career-profiles/people-management/).** If you’re a great manager, you can increase the productivity of those working for you. A talented research manager will often have more impact than a talented researcher, because they can multiply the impact of _many_ talented researchers.
-   **Roles where you reach large audiences.** If you’re a good communicator, you can use your skills to persuade people to pursue impact. For example, a popular podcast could convince [hundreds of people to pledge some of their income to effective causes](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/hd2LeisjAmzdJdGb3/the-most-successful-ea-podcast-of-all-time-sam-harris-and) —generating millions of dollars for charity.

These are just a few examples of how to increase your career’s leverage. There are many more examples, though—some of which have truly massive impact potential, such as persuading a government or large organization to spend even a tiny fraction of its budget or efforts on a pressing issue.

One great example of this is the Lead Exposure Elimination Project (LEEP). LEEP is a small organization, but it managed to convince the Malawian government to regulate lead paint, saving an [estimated $180 million of future earnings](https://leadelimination.org/malawi_cost-effectiveness_intro/) that would have been lost [due to the cognitive effects of lead poisoning](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/lead-costs-developing-economies-nearly-1-trillion-dollars-annually/). This is _many_ times more than most of us will be able to earn in a lifetime, and far more impact than anyone could achieve without leveraging the resources of others. These sorts of high-leverage opportunities can therefore play significantly into our career decisions if we’re seeking maximum impact.

## Other factors to consider

While leverage is pretty important (as we’ve pointed out thus far), it still needs to be considered alongside [other factors](https://probablygood.org/career-guide/) when planning for a career with high [counterfactual impact](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/counterfactual-impact/). For one, it’s vital to work in a cause area with [important, solvable problems](https://probablygood.org/career-guide/analyzing-cause-areas/). If I could convince the US government to dig the world’s largest hole, for example, I would have enormous leverage. However, I would have zero positive impact.

Of course, ensuring you’re using your leverage for good isn’t always as simple as avoiding digging useless holes. For example, you might be able to convince someone to make a significant investment in medicine to combat a neglected tropical disease, which is an important problem to solve. But if the medicine doesn’t work, it won’t do any good. So, leverage in a promising cause area isn’t enough to make a positive impact; it also matters how effective the specific solution is that you’re using your leverage to deploy.

## Potential for harm

It’s worth keeping in mind that high-leverage roles often carry the highest risk for large direct _harm_. Sometimes, this is obvious—like high-leverage jobs in organizations which clearly do harm (e.g. marketing for a tobacco company).

But there’s also a risk in taking roles where your work leads to surprising (and unintentional) damage, like replacing water pumps in remote villages [with less effective alternatives](https://objectsindevelopment.net/the-playpump/). When combined with high amounts of leverage, such harm will unfortunately be amplified.

High leverage roles also carry increased potential for high [_counterfactual harm_](https://probablygood.org/career-guide/considering-counterfactual-impact/) – that is, making the world worse than if you hadn’t taken the role. For example, if I had done a particularly good job at raising money for the trachoma prevention charity, I might have inadvertently persuaded people away from donating to an even _more_ effective charity. Or by getting the US government to dig an enormous hole, I’d probably be taking away money from more important areas, like foreign aid or social security.

Another form of counterfactual harm occurs if you’re less effective than someone else who could have taken your job. In high-leverage roles, especially those that attract talented, impact-driven people, this risk increases. For such roles, it’s _particularly important_ you’re a good fit.

However, in some cases, you can be fairly certain of making a positive counterfactual impact, even if you’re unsure about your fit. For example, taking [high-paying job to donate more to effective causes](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/earning-to-give/) gives you leverage by influencing how funds are used. Even if you don’t choose the absolute best charities, you’re likely still doing more good than someone without an intention to donate.

## Considering leverage

All in all, we think it’s important to take leverage seriously when making career decisions. This might not mean pursuing a high-leverage role immediately, but it could mean getting yourself in a [good position to take on](https://probablygood.org/core-concepts/career-capital/) an influential role in the future.
